f:T2cd4,Grade 5 ABRSM violin is the milestone exam. Pass it, and you've earned the right to attempt Grades 6–8 (which require Grade 5 theory as a prerequisite — read your local rules). Technically, Grade 5 demands vibrato, shifts to 4th and 5th position, the first three-octave scales for some keys, and a far wider repertoire including full sonata movements.

> Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 (this post) → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8.

Learning Outcome

- Apply continuous vibrato to sustained notes.- Shift fluently between 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th positions.- Play three-octave G and A major scales (some syllabi).- Play two-octave scales in all major keys + most minor keys (harmonic + melodic).- Use sul G (play on G string only) and sul ponticello / sul tasto colour techniques.- Identify all triads (major, minor, diminished, augmented) at sight.- Play a complete Baroque sonata movement + one concerto movement.

Chapter 1 — Vibrato: the expression

Vibrato is the small, rapid pitch oscillation that adds warmth and life to sustained notes. Without it, classical violin tone sounds naked.

Three vibrato types

| Type | Source | Speed | When ||------|--------|-------|------|| Wrist vibrato | Wrist rocks back and forth | Medium | Most repertoire || Arm vibrato | Whole forearm rocks | Slow + wide | Romantic, big tone || Finger vibrato | Finger joint flexes | Fast + narrow | Rare; modern works |

Wrist is the standard first vibrato taught at Grade 5.

Learning wrist vibrato — step by step

1. Air vibrato — without violin. Hold left arm up as if holding violin. Wave hand at the wrist, palm-up to palm-down motion, for 30 sec. This is the motion.2. Vibrato on knee. Sit. Place finger on knee. Rock wrist gently, finger stays in contact, pad rolls. Build motor pattern.3. Vibrato on violin neck (no string). Finger on the neck, not string. Rock wrist.4. Vibrato on string with finger anchored. 3rd finger on A (= D5). Bow a long D on the A string. Rock wrist; finger should roll (not slide). Pitch should wobble below the pitch and back to it.5. Slow metronome vibrato. 4 wobbles per beat at 60 bpm = 4 Hz. Build to 6 Hz over 6 months.6. Apply to scales. Vibrate every sustained note in a slow scale.

> Where I got stuck: my wrist locked. Vibrato felt impossible for 3 months. Fix: spend 5 minutes per day on the "knee drill" — no violin, no string, just the wrist motion. The brain learns motion separately from instrument. Once the wrist could rock freely, transferring to the violin took one week.

Pitch direction

Vibrato should wobble below the in-tune pitch back up to it. Never above. The brain hears the upper limit of the oscillation as "the note".

Where to start using it

Don't vibrate every note. Start with:- Long notes (half-notes, whole-notes).- Phrase peak notes.- Final note of a phrase.

By Grade 6, you'll vibrate almost every note that's longer than a quaver. At Grade 5, just the obvious ones.

Chapter 2 — Shifting to 4th and 5th positions

4th position

On the A string: 1=E5, 2=F♯5, 3=G♯5, 4=A5.

Note: 1 in 4th position = E5 = the same note as the open E next door. Useful for staying on one string.

5th position

1=F♯5, 2=G♯5, 3=A5, 4=B5.

5th position is where the violin's neck meets the body. The thumb position changes — you'll start using the "shoulder" of the violin as a reference.

The shift map (3-octave G major)

To play G major across 3 octaves, you traverse:- G string: 1st position G-A-B-C- D string: 1st pos D-E-F♯-G- A string: 1st pos A-B-C-D- E string: 1st pos E-F♯-G- Shift to 3rd pos on E: A-B-C- Shift to 5th pos on E: D-E-F♯ (fingers 2-3-4)- Shift to 7th pos on E for the top G (3rd finger) — or go to 6th position for D-E-F♯-G (1-2-3-4); depends on edition

> Trick: learn the shift route as a fixed sequence. Mark "III" or "V" above the music. Practise just the route (no rhythm) until the shifts are automatic; then add timing.

Silent shift drill (essential at Grade 5+)

1. Bow open A.2. Slide 1st finger up to D5 (3rd pos).3. Continue sliding to F♯5 (5th pos).4. Back down to D5, then to 1st position B.5. Repeat 10×, very slowly. No glissando audible.

Daily. The shifts smoothen over weeks.

Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine

3rd position — the workhorse

3rd position is the second most used position after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd.

Finding 3rd position

On the A string:1. Play open A.2. Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).3. Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5.

Other strings analogous:- G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.- D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.- E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D.

The shift motion

Three components, in this order:1. Release finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).2. Thumb and hand travel together up the neck.3. Press the new finger at the target.

The finger that leads the shift is called the guide finger. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1.

> Trick: practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a "scrape", the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide.

2nd position

Less common than 3rd. The hand sits halfway between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F.

2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits just above 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches.

How to choose position

| Reason | Position to use ||--------|------------------|| Reach high notes | Higher position || Stay on same string (tone colour) | Whatever position keeps you there || Avoid awkward string crossing | The position that smooths the line || Score marks it | Composer chose; obey || Difficult fingering pattern | Try 2nd position |

Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce

Spiccato is the foundation of all advanced bowing. The bow lifts off the string between notes — not because you lifted it, but because the bow's elasticity bounces it.

How spiccato works

The bow is flexible. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the "sweet spot" (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note.

Spiccato practice progression

1. Drop test. Hold the bow over the A string, let it fall from 1 cm above, watch it bounce. Find the bow position where bounce is most controlled (usually 4–6 inches above the middle, near the balance point).2. One bounce per drop. Drop, lift, drop. Slow.3. Two bounces per drop without re-lifting. The bow bounces itself.4. Continuous bouncing on open string at metronome 60.5. Add scale notes. D major scale, spiccato.6. Speed up gradually to 100, then 120.

> Trick: spiccato fails when you grip the bow. Relax thumb + pinky; let the bow do the work. If your wrist hurts after spiccato practice, you're forcing it. Stop, shake out, restart looser.

Détaché vs spiccato vs staccato — quick distinction

| Bowing | Bow contact | Sound ||--------|-------------|-------|| Détaché | On string | Smooth separation || Spiccato | Bounces off | Light, dancing || Staccato | On string, sharp stop | Short, dry || Slurred staccato | On string, multiple stops one direction | Pulsed long line |

Chapter 4 — New keys: E major, A♭ major

E major (4 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯)

Fingering: high 2 everywhere (F♯, C♯, G♯), plus a high 3 for G♯ on the D string and D♯ on the A string. Coming after the flat keys, E major feels easy because the fingers sit "high" and two open strings (E and A) are in the key.

A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭)

The hardest playable key at Grade 4. Two "low" fingers on most strings; A♭ itself (instead of open A) creates an unusual hand position.

Most editions require A♭ for one octave only at Grade 4. Two-octave A♭ is Grade 5+.

> Trick: don't memorise A♭ note-by-note. Memorise it as a modified D major shape, with low 1 on the D string and a low everywhere else. Pattern recognition over note recall.

Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves)

B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering:

Wait — that's a lot of "low" fingers across the scale: every B♭ and E♭, plus F and G on the E string. The hand shape changes per string.

> Trick: the rule for any key with flats: drop the finger that plays the flatted note half a finger-width toward the nut. For B♭ major, that's 2 on G string, 1 on D, 1 and 4 on A, 4 on E. Memorise the pattern of which finger is low, not the abstract scale.

Top note: B♭5 (4th finger on E in 1st position).

Why this key matters

B♭ is the first key violinists genuinely struggle with. The hand has to relearn its default spacing. Master B♭ by Grade 3, and every flat key after becomes intuitive.

Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave)

E♭ has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. One octave only at Grade 3.

Fingerings vary a little between editions. The point: 3 flats means every B♭, E♭ and A♭ gets a "low" finger. Get the pattern of lowness right and your fingers will land.

> Where I got stuck: I tried to memorise E♭ as "B♭, E♭, A♭" abstractly. Fix: I stopped naming the flats and just thought "low-low-low" in the right places. Faster, more reliable.

Chapter 3 — The three minor forms

Grade 2 introduced natural minor. Grade 3 adds harmonic and (sometimes) melodic.

Natural minor (review)

Same notes as the relative major. A natural minor:

Harmonic minor — raises the 7th

The leading-tone effect that classical music expects:

That raised 7th creates an augmented 2nd between F and G♯ (3 semitones — a full step + half-step). This gives harmonic minor its Middle-Eastern or Spanish flavour.

> Trick: harmonic minor sounds exotic compared to natural minor. Sing both ascending — natural feels "modal/folk", harmonic feels "classical/dramatic". After ten A/B comparisons, your ear locks the difference.

Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down

Two different note sets in one scale. Composers chose this in the Baroque to handle melodic lines without the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd "bump".

Grade 3 typically requires melodic minor only on paper; full playing comes Grade 4.

Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato

A new bowing. Notation: slur (curved line) + dot under each note within the slur.

The bow stays on the string; the wrist makes a small stop-start motion for each note while moving in one direction.

> Where I got stuck: I lifted the bow between notes — that's staccato volante (flying staccato), Grade 6 stuff. Fix: practise on open strings, bow stays on the string, only the wrist pulses. Picture a bow that "hesitates" 4 times in one stroke.

Hooked bowing

Two unequal notes (often dotted-quaver + semiquaver) on one bow with a tiny stop between them:

Used to keep the dotted figure on the same bow direction throughout a phrase. Saves bow-direction confusion.

Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview

True shifting (moving the hand to 2nd/3rd/etc. positions) is Grade 4 territory, but Grade 3 introduces the concept and may use one shift in a piece or as a "step out" of 1st position.

Why shift?

1. Reach higher notes the 1st position can't.2. Stay on one string for a phrase (changing tone colour).3. Make awkward fingerings easier.

What 3rd position feels like

In 3rd position, the 1st finger plays what the 3rd finger plays in 1st position. So on the A string:

- 1st position: 3rd finger = D5.- 3rd position: 1st finger = D5.

The hand moves up the neck by about 1 inch.

First shift exercise

On the A string:1. Open A, then 3rd finger D (1st position).2. Slide the 1st finger up the string until it sounds D.3. Now you're in 3rd position. Play 2 (E) and 3 (F♯).

Don't lift; slide smoothly. The thumb travels with the hand.

> Trick: the thumb moves at the same time as the fingers. If the thumb stays back, you get a stretched, painful hand. Practise the shift with eyes closed, only feeling the smooth slide. Listen for the slide pitch — at first it'll glissando, later it'll be silent.

Grade 3 may include one piece with a marked shift; Grade 4 requires the technique.

Chapter 1 — Two-octave scales arrive

A one-octave scale spans two strings (that was Grade 1); a two-octave scale now traverses three or four strings — G major uses all four.

G major (two octaves)

Grade 2 typically asks for the scale to reach G5 (2nd finger on E in 1st position). Some editions extend to A5 (3rd finger).

D major (two octaves)

Top note here is A5 (3rd finger on E). The full two octaves end on D6, which needs a shift up the E string — so for now I stop at A5.

C major (one octave) — first piece with no sharps

> Trick: C major is the shock key for violin beginners. Every other scale you've played has the half-step between 2 and 3. C major has it between 1 and 2 on the D string. Mark the music with an arrow over the F natural for the first 2 weeks.

F major (one octave)

Half-step lands between open A and finger 1 on the A string (A → B♭). The B♭ requires placing finger 1 low — closer to the nut than normal.

> Where I got stuck: "low 1" felt unnatural for weeks. The finger wants to land where it always does. Fix: physically mark a tape line for "low 1" position on the A string for one practice session. Once your finger learns the distance, peel the tape off.

Chapter 4 — Longer slurs and bow planning

Grade 1 slurs were 2–4 notes. Grade 2 introduces 6, 8, even a full scale (8 notes) on one bow.

The bow-budget rule

Total bow length = 25 inches (varies by player + bow).For 8 notes in one bow: 25 ÷ 8 ≈ 3 inches per note.

You cannot play loud + slow + long-slurred. Pick two. Grade 2 long slurs are usually mezzo-piano and steady — the bow conserves itself.

Slur with string crossing

Example: slur "open A → 1st finger B → open E" in one bow. Two string changes inside one stroke. The right wrist + elbow tilt to roll between strings without breaking the sound.

> Where I got stuck: every slur with a string crossing scratched on the crossing point. Fix: slow practice with the metronome at 40 bpm, paying attention to the angle of the bow hair at the crossing. The bow should tilt gradually, not snap. Drill: 5 minutes a day for 2 weeks → solved.

Practice progression

1. 2-note slur, same string.2. 2-note slur, crossing strings.3. 4-note slur, same string.4. 4-note slur, crossing strings.5. 8-note slur = full scale.

Don't jump steps. Each stage takes about a week.

> Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → … → 08 Grade 8.

Learning Outcome

- Place fingers 1, 2, 3, 4 in 1st position on all four strings.- Play one-octave scales of G, D, A majors (separate bows).- Play one-octave arpeggios for the same keys.- Read fluently in treble clef across the first 1.5 octaves above Middle C.- Use détaché (separate) bowing cleanly.- Begin to feel "in tune" — recognise when a finger placement is sharp or flat.- Play 3–5 Grade 1 / Initial Grade pieces with hands together.

Chapter 1 — 1st Position fingering

What "1st position" means

The position number tells you where the first finger sits on the fingerboard. In 1st position, the index finger sits just below the nut — about a whole tone above the open string. From there, fingers 2, 3, 4 extend up the fingerboard, each roughly a whole tone apart (with one half-step adjustment per scale).

The diagram shows the Grade 1 pattern: whole steps between the fingers, except a half-step between fingers 2 and 3. In other keys, the half-step location shifts (Chapter 3).

Finger numbering

| Finger | Number ||--------|--------|| Index | 1 || Middle | 2 || Ring | 3 || Little (pinky) | 4 || (Thumb supports underneath, no number) | — |

This is opposite to piano — there, the thumb is 1. On the violin, the thumb has no number because it never stops a string; its job is to brace the neck.

> Trick: coming from piano, I kept calling my thumb "finger 1" and reaching with my index expecting it to be "2". The fix: every Grade 1 piece marks "1" above the index. Trust the printed number, not your old habit.

Placing fingers cleanly

1. Fingertip down, not the pad — the smaller contact area produces a cleaner stop.2. Curved finger — knuckles bent.3. Just enough pressure to stop the string vibrating above the finger. Too much pressure = sharp pitch + finger fatigue.4. Drop and lift — don't slide. Vertical motion only.

> Where I got stuck: I pressed every finger too hard for two months. My left hand cramped after 10 minutes. The fix: deliberately under-press until the note buzzes; then add the minimum extra pressure to stop the buzz. That's the right amount. Most students press 2-3× more than needed.

Chapter 2 — Tuning revisited (the violinist's ear)

You learnt to tune in Post 00. Grade 1 adds double-stop tuning — bow two adjacent open strings together and listen for the perfect 5th.

How a perfect 5th sounds

When two strings tuned a perfect 5th apart sound together, the combined tone is smooth and ringing. When slightly off, you hear beats — a wobbling, rough pulse like an out-of-sync washing machine.

To tune by 5ths:1. Tune A to A=440 with a tuner.2. Bow A and D together gently. Adjust D's fine tuner until beats vanish.3. Bow D and G together. Adjust G.4. Bow A and E together. Adjust E.

> Trick: sit in a quiet room. Beats are easier to hear without background noise. After 6 months of tuning by 5ths, you'll do it automatically — and you'll have built a perfect-5th template in your ear that helps every interval recognition exercise.

Chapter 3 — The three Grade 1 scales

G major (one octave, G string + D string)

Open G → 1st finger A → 2nd finger B → 3rd finger C → open D → 1st finger E → 2nd finger F♯ → 3rd finger G.

Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 on each string. (B→C and F♯→G are half-steps.)

D major (one octave, D string + A string)

Open D → 1 E → 2 F♯ → 3 G → open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D.

Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 again.

A major (one octave, A string + E string)

Open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D → open E → 1 F♯ → 2 G♯ → 3 A.

Same half-step pattern.

> Trick: G, D, A majors all use the same finger pattern in 1st position: 0-1-2-3 with the half-step between 2 and 3 on each string. Memorise one hand shape; transfer to all three keys. This is the "violin equivalent" of the piano's W-W-H-W-W-W-H — but expressed as finger placement.

Arpeggios (Grade 1)

The tonic triad spelled out: root, third, fifth, octave.

Practise scales and arpeggios in pairs: G major scale → G major arpeggio → G again. Builds the key-feel.

Chapter 4 — Bowing: détaché and slur

Détaché

"Detached" — each note gets its own bow stroke (down or up). The bow doesn't lift between notes; it changes direction on the string.

In Grade 1 you play almost everything détaché. Goal: smooth, even tone with no scratching at the bow direction change. The wrist makes a tiny "give" at each change, like a pendulum bottom.

Slur

A curved line over two or more notes means play them in one bow stroke (one direction, no change). Same hair, multiple finger changes.

> Where I got stuck: I scratched every bow change for the first 2 months. Fix: practise the bow change motion itself without a string — just air bow with the empty bow, smoothly reversing direction. The wrist learns to soften the change. Then add the string back.

Bow distribution

On a slur of 4 notes, you have to "ration" the bow length so you don't run out. Roughly: divide the bow into 4 equal parts; one part per note.

Practice: slur 2 notes per bow, then 4, then 8. By Grade 3 you'll be slurring entire scales in one bow.

Chapter 5 — Reading music for violin

Violin reads in treble clef only (unlike the piano, which uses both). Range covers from open G (below the staff, under two ledger lines) up to many ledger lines above. By Grade 1 you read up to about A or B above the staff (3rd finger on E string).

Notes you'll meet in Grade 1

| Note | Where on violin ||------|------------------|| G3 | Open G string (lowest) || A3 | 1 on G || B3 | 2 on G || C4 | 3 on G || D4 | Open D || E4 | 1 on D || F♯4 | 2 on D || G4 | 3 on D || A4 | Open A || B4 | 1 on A || C♯5 | 2 on A || D5 | 3 on A || E5 | Open E || F♯5 | 1 on E || G♯5 | 2 on E || A5 | 3 on E (top note Grade 1) |

> Trick: when sight-reading a Grade 1 piece, mark the string and finger above each note in pencil for the first read. After 3 plays through, erase the markings — your hand has memorised the pattern. By the end of Grade 1 you should be doing this in your head, not on paper.

How to study a piece

1. Listen to a recording 3 times before touching the violin.2. Sight-read pizzicato (pluck the strings) — checks left-hand alone before the bow complicates it.3. Bow slowly — 1/4 of target tempo with metronome.4. Hands together at tempo — only once you can play each separately without thinking.5. Memorise the first 4 bars by week 1.

> Trick: always say note names aloud as you play during slow practice. The voice + hand link locks the note-name-to-pitch connection. Later you'll do this silently, but the verbal pass is the cement.

Chapter 7 — Intonation: the violin's hardest problem

Unlike piano (perfect pitch every key), violin pitch depends entirely on where your finger lands. Even 1 millimetre off = audibly out of tune.

How to develop intonation

1. Listen to open strings constantly. Cross-check stopped notes against open strings of the same name.2. Drone practice. Bow an open string, then play a scale on the next string. The open string is your reference; stopped notes that match the harmonic ring are in tune.3. Use ear-training apps. "Tonal Energy", "Functional Ear Trainer".4. Sing the note first, then play it. Singing pre-tunes your hand.

> Where I got stuck: I couldn't tell if I was sharp or flat — everything sounded "kind of right". Fix: bow the open A while pressing 4th finger on D (which should match A). If the two pitches fight — beats appear — I was off. If they fuse into one ringing tone — in tune. After a month of drone practice, my finger landed correctly without conscious thought.

Tape on the fingerboard (controversial but works)

Many teachers put small pieces of tape across the fingerboard at the 1st, 3rd, and 4th finger positions for absolute beginners. Provides a visual guide for finger placement.

Pros: speeds up first 3 months; reduces frustration.Cons: trains eyes-on-fingerboard habit; must be removed by Grade 2 or it becomes a crutch.

Use it if you're self-teaching and frustrated. Remove it as soon as you can hit the notes without looking. By Grade 1 exam, no tape.

Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills

ABRSM Grade 1 violin aural is identical to piano aural at Grade 1:

- Clap back a 2-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4.- Sing back a 3-4 note phrase.- Identify the time signature by listening to a tune.

Violin-specific aural exercise

- Match pitch — examiner plays a note on the piano; you play it on the violin. Tests pitch perception + finger placement under pressure.- Tuning judgment — examiner plays two notes; you say which is higher or whether they're the same.

> Trick: practise pitch matching daily for 60 seconds. Pick a random note on a piano (or use a tuning app), then find it on your violin. By Grade 1 exam you should hit within a quarter-tone first try.

17:T4132,The first post in a self-study violin series. Goal: by the end you can hold the violin without straining, name every open string, draw a straight bow that makes one sustained tone, and play three short open-string pieces. The violin is harder than the piano at the start — there's no "pressed key = right pitch" guarantee — but the payoff is the most expressive single-line instrument we have.

> Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8.

Learning Outcome

- Hold the violin with relaxed posture, no shoulder hunching.- Hold the bow with the correct grip and produce a straight stroke.- Name the four open strings (G, D, A, E) low to high and find them on the staff.- Tune with a clip-on tuner or app.- Play three open-string exercises that produce a clear, ringing tone.- Understand the difference between violin, viola, cello, and double bass — pick the right one.

Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now)

The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is unfretted — you place your finger somewhere and the precision of placement decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise.

In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match.

> Where I started: I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point.

Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument

Size matters first

Violins come in fractional sizes for children: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 4/4 (full). Adults use 4/4 unless they have unusually short arms. Measure from neck-base to the centre of the palm with arm extended:

| Arm length (neck to palm) | Size ||---------------------------|------|| < 36 cm | 1/8 || 36–43 cm | 1/4 || 43–48 cm | 1/2 || 48–55 cm | 3/4 || ≥ 55 cm | 4/4 (full) |

What to buy as an adult beginner

A "beginner outfit" includes: violin, bow, case, rosin, shoulder rest. Brands in the $200-$500 range that don't disappoint:

- Yamaha V3, V5, V7 — reliable workhorse, well-set-up.- Stentor Student II, Conservatoire — UK-popular student line.- Cremona SV-175, SV-500 — entry-level, decent set-up.- Eastman VL80, VL100 — Chinese workshop, often resold by reputable shops.

Avoid $50-$100 Amazon "VSO" (Violin-Shaped Object) instruments — they're often un-tuneable, with cheap pegs that slip and bridges that are wrong.

Accessories you'll definitely need

| Item | Notes ||------|-------|| Rosin | Light or dark; "Pirastro Goldflex" or "Hidersine" are safe starts || Shoulder rest | Kun, Everest, Bonmusica — try in person if possible || Tuner | Clip-on (Snark, KLIQ) or phone app (Tunable, Cleartune) || Music stand | Foldable, cheap. Use it from day one || Metronome | App fine. Essential by week 4 || Spare strings | Dominant or Tonica brand for first replacement || Soft cloth | Wipe rosin dust off strings + body after every session |

> Trick: the violin shop visit is worth it even if you order online. Spend 30 minutes at a real shop trying setups in your size range. Your ear immediately tells you which one rings — pay the extra $100 for the one that sings. Cheap-and-dull will discourage you within a month.

Chapter 3 — The instrument anatomy

Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these):

- Scroll, pegs, peg box — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs- Nut — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard- Fingerboard — the long black wooden strip your fingers press- Bridge — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration- F-holes — the two carved openings; let sound out- Soundpost — small wooden dowel inside the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.- Bass bar — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.- Tailpiece — anchors the strings at the bottom- Fine tuners — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)- Chinrest — where your chin rests- Shoulder rest — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder

Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin

Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years.

Standing

1. Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.2. Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).3. Head facing forward, then turn it slightly left to look down the fingerboard.4. Place the violin on your left collarbone, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.5. The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — don't grip with your jaw. The shoulder rest does the work.

> Where I got stuck: I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest higher until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is supported, not gripped. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit.

Sitting

Same upper-body posture. Sit on the front half of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch.

Left-hand position

- Thumb rests on the side of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.- Fingers curve over the strings.- Wrist straight — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.- A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger.

Common mistakes:- Wrist collapsed under the neck (the "frying pan grip") — kills agility.- Thumb wrapped over the top of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.- Fingers flat on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down.

Chapter 5 — The bow

Anatomy of the bow

- Stick — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)- Hair — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings- Frog — black bottom piece where the screw lives- Screw / button — adjusts hair tension- Tip / point — top end- Grip / wrap — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog

Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life)

1. Pick the bow up at the frog with your right hand.2. Thumb bent, tip placed in the notch between the frog and the wrap.3. Middle finger opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.4. Ring finger rests on the side of the frog.5. Pinky curved on top of the stick, near the screw.6. Index finger wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight.

All fingers curved, never locked straight. The grip is light — too tight and the bow can't move.

> Trick: practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — then pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows.

Tightening the bow

Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a standard pencil fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings.

Always loosen after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber.

Rosin

Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times before every session. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone.

Chapter 6 — Tuning

The four strings, low to high: G — D — A — E. Each interval is a perfect 5th (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5.

With a clip-on tuner or app

1. Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.2. Pluck or bow each string one at a time.3. Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise.

Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments)

Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing into the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Push-and-turn together.

- Turn away from you (top of peg moves forward) = string winds up = pitch rises.- Turn toward you = pitch falls.

Small turns. Pegs are sensitive. Tune the G first, then D, A, E.

Fine tuning (small adjustments)

The little screws on the tailpiece. Turn clockwise to raise pitch, counter-clockwise to lower. Use these for daily small corrections (< quarter-tone).

> Where I got stuck: I terrified myself snapping a string in week one. Fix: tune slowly and small. If a string is way off, raise it gradually, not in one big peg crank. Snapped strings are normal — buy a backup E string ($5) and stop being afraid.

Tuning A first (orchestral convention)

In orchestral settings, tune A first (because the oboe gives an A). Then tune D, G, E by listening for perfect fifths with the open A. This is your first ear-training exercise — when the two strings are exactly a 5th apart, the combined sound is clean. When slightly off, you hear "beats" — a wobbling pulse.

Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing

Before fingers go on strings, master pulling a clean tone on each open string.

The straight bow

The bow must move perpendicular to the strings (parallel to the bridge). If it angles toward the fingerboard or the bridge, the contact point changes and the tone breaks up.

Practice exercise:1. Place the bow flat on the A string, about halfway between bridge and fingerboard.2. Slowly pull a downstroke (bow moving from frog toward tip). Count "1-2-3-4".3. Pause. Reset. Upstroke (tip toward frog). Same count.4. Watch the bow in a mirror — is it staying parallel to the bridge?

Contact point

Three knobs control your sound:- Weight — how much the bow presses down (mostly from the index finger via the stick)- Speed — how fast the bow moves- Contact point — distance from the bridge (closer to bridge = louder + sharper; closer to fingerboard = softer + warmer)

Balance these three. A faster bow needs more weight to maintain pressure. A bow close to the bridge needs slower speed to avoid scratching. The "sweet spot" for a clean, warm tone is about 1.5–2 cm from the bridge for a normal mezzo-forte dynamic.

> Analogy: think of the bow like painting. Speed is how fast you move the brush. Weight is how hard you press. Contact point is which part of the paper you're stroking. Change any one and the paint stroke changes. Beginners change all three accidentally and produce a mess. Master one knob at a time.

Three first exercises

Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string

Whole bow length, 8 beats per stroke. Down-up-down-up on each string in turn: G, D, A, E. Listen for a clean, sustained tone with no scratches or wobbles. Five minutes daily.

Exercise 2 — String crossings

Bow A for one beat, then D for one beat. The forearm rotates from elbow; wrist stays neutral. Do A-D-A-D-A-D, then D-A-D-A. Then add G-D and A-E. Tests bow-arm coordination without fingers complicating things.

Exercise 3 — "Hot cross buns" (open strings only)

The melody fits perfectly on three open strings if you treat it as A-D-A-D-G-D-A. Just bow each string in the rhythm of the tune. You'll feel embarrassed playing nursery rhymes — and then proud, because you sound musical.

Chapter 8 — How to practise

The single most important rule: slow and small reps beat fast big ones. Five minutes of focused open-string bowing teaches more than thirty minutes of trying to play melodies you can't yet.

Daily routine, 25 minutes

| Block | Time | What ||-------|------|------|| Tune | 2 min | Tuner first, then check by ear || Bow hold check | 2 min | Hold + release the bow on a pencil 5 times || Open-string long bows | 8 min | All 4 strings, full bow, 8 beats per stroke || String crossings | 5 min | A-D, D-A, G-D, A-E || Exercise piece | 5 min | One short pattern (open strings only this month) || Cool-down | 3 min | Play any open string slowly, listen to the tone, end on a clear sound |

Slow practice rules

1. No scratch, no rush. If the tone is bad, slow down.2. One thing at a time. Bow speed or bow weight or contact point — not all three at once.3. Mirror. Use a mirror or phone front-camera for 5 min per session for the first 3 months. The eye catches what the hand doesn't.4. Stop before fatigue. Tired = tense = bad habits. Better to stop at 20 minutes feeling fresh than 45 minutes feeling done.

> Trick: after every session, record 30 seconds on your phone. Open string, long bow, full attention. Listen back. Week 1 will sound rough. Week 8 will sound transformed. The recording is your honest progress meter when your ears in the moment lie.

Chapter 9 — Common beginner mistakes

| Mistake | Fix ||---------|-----|| Crooked bow (drifts toward fingerboard) | Mirror practice. Mark the bridge-side bow lane with tape on your bow if needed || Death-grip on the bow | Lay the bow on your palm, close the fingers gently, lift. Fingers curved, never locked || Pressing the string too hard with left fingers | The fingertip just needs to touch the string firmly enough to stop it vibrating above. When to move on

You're ready for Post 01 — Grade 1 Theory & Repertoire when you can:- Tune all four strings with a tuner unaided.- Produce a clean, sustained tone on each open string for 8 beats.- Cross strings smoothly without scratching.- Play "Hot cross buns" or "Twinkle Twinkle" (open-string version) recognisably.- Hold the violin and bow for 15 minutes without fatigue.

If you're not there yet, stay here another 2-3 weeks. Grade 1 adds fingers; without solid open-string tone, fingered notes will sound worse, not better.
