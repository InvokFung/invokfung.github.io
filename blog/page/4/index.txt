e:T2ab3,The aim decides where the object ball goes. The tip spot and the power decide what the white does. This lesson covers the five tip spots, the safe zone, keeping the cue level, five power levels, how the white slides and then rolls, and the straight-pot family: stop, follow, screw, stun run-through, long-range screw and the drag shot.

> Series map. ★ Basics: 01 Cue action and elevation → 02 Aiming and potting → 03 Tip and power (this post) → 04 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles · ★★★ Advanced: 09 Escaping snookers → 10 Multi-ball shots → 11 Safety → 12 Position play · ★★★★ Hard: 13 Break-building → 14 Special shots · Reference: 15 Rules, tactics and practice.

Learning Outcome

- Know the five basic tip spots and keep the tip inside the safe zone.- Keep the cue level for low shots, and see why.- Pick a power from 1 to 5 by how far the white would roll.- Know where the white stops sliding and starts rolling, for each tip height.- On a straight pot: stop, follow, screw and stun run-through on purpose.- Screw from long range, and play a drag shot to arrive slowly from far away.

Two signs used in every clip:

| Sign | Means ||------|-------|| white circle with a blue dot | the white seen from behind; the blue dot is where the tip hits || five bars | power: 1 gentle … 5 very hard |

Chapter 1 — The tip

1 · Five spots on the white

| Spot | What it does | Where ||------|--------------|-------|| centre | plain ball: the white slides, then rolls on | here || ▲ top | follow: the white runs on after the hit | here || ▼ bottom | screw (backspin): the white comes back | here || ◀ left / right ▶ | side: changes the angle off a cushion | post 05 |

Start with the centre. Most pots need nothing else.

2 · Stay inside the safe zone

Keep the tip inside the green circle, about half-way from the centre to the edge. Further out, the tip slides off the ball (a miscue) and the white goes anywhere.

> Trick: chalk before every shot. Most miscues are a dry tip, not a bad spot.

3 · Low shots: keep the cue level

To hit low, lower the whole cue (post 01, tip height) and keep it nearly flat, about 3° ✓. Raising the butt to reach the bottom of the white ✗ makes the tip strike down into the ball.

> Watch out: a raised cue plus even a little side makes the white curve (swerve, post 14). On low shots that curve is a common, hidden cause of missed pots.

Chapter 2 — Power

4 · Power 1 to 5

The series describes power by how far the white would roll on its own, hit in the centre from the baulk line:

| Power | The white alone rolls… ||-------|-------------------------|| 1 | to the blue || 2 | to the top cushion || 3 | up, and back past the blue || 4 | up, and back to the baulk line || 5 | up, back, and up again |

Most pots are power 2–3. Power 5 is for breaking up a pack or a long escape. A softer hit is easier to control.

> Trick: before a shot, picture the white's journey and pick the number. Then play that number, not "hard" or "soft".

5 · Watch it: a straight pot, centre ball

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b01clipstraight.webp" alt="Clip: a straight red into the corner pocket. Tip in the centre, power 2. The cue moves back and forth, goes straight through, the red goes in and the white rolls a little after it"></div>

Centre, power 2, cue straight through. The red drops ✓ and the white follows it a little way. Why it follows is the next picture.

6 · Where the white starts to roll

A struck white first slides. The cloth then rubs it into a natural roll. Where that happens depends on the tip height (model, power 3):

| Tip | Backspin lasts to | Rolling from ||-----|-------------------|--------------|| high, 7/10 of the ball's height | none | at once || a little above centre | none | 31 cm || centre | none | 58 cm || a little below centre | 42 cm | 79 cm || low | 88 cm | 103 cm |

This one picture explains the next four sections:

- Stop = the white reaches the red while it has no spin: the moment the blue part ends.- Screw = it reaches the red while still blue (backspin).- Follow = it reaches the red while green (rolling).- A centre hit on a 2 m pot is rolling long before it arrives, so it follows.

Chapter 3 — Stop, follow, screw and the in-betweens

7 · Stop: the white stays where it hits

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipstop.webp" alt="Clip: straight red into the middle pocket. Tip a little below centre, power 3. The red goes in and the white stops exactly where it touched the red, marked with a yellow ring"></div>

A little below centre, power 3, the white 50 cm from the red. It arrives with no spin left, hands all its speed to the red and stops ✓.

8 · Follow: the white runs on

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipfollow.webp" alt="Clip: the same pot. Tip above centre, power 2. The red goes in and the white rolls on after it, about a third of a metre"></div>

Above centre, power 2. The white is rolling when it hits, so it keeps going after the red, here about a third of a metre.

> Watch out: too much follow on a straight pot and the white follows the red into the pocket (an in-off, a foul).

9 · Screw: the white comes back

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipscrew.webp" alt="Clip: the same pot. Tip low, power 3. The red goes in and the white spins back towards the player, about 60 centimetres"></div>

Low, power 3, and let the cue go through the white. The backspin is still on when it hits, so the white comes back, here about 60 cm.

10 · Stun run-through: a few centimetres on

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/s03clipstunrun.webp" alt="Clip: the same straight pot into the middle pocket. Tip just below centre, power 3. The red goes in and the white runs on 16 centimetres past where it touched, marked with a yellow ring; a faint ghost ball marks the contact"></div>

Between stop and follow. The tip goes just below centre, a little higher than for the stop. The white arrives with a touch of forward roll and creeps on, here 16 cm (model: stop at 0.21 R below centre, run-through at 0.09 R below).

Use it when you need the white a short way forward with a firm, straight-feeling hit. A soft follow over the same distance would be more affected by the cloth.

11 · Further away? Hit lower, or firmer

Backspin wears off on the way (section 6). So to stop the white:

| Distance | Tip | Power ||----------|-----|-------|| short (30 cm) | just below centre | 3 || medium (80 cm) | clearly lower | 3 || long (1.5 m) | low | 4 (more power keeps the spin on) |

12 · More power, more screw

The same low tip: power 3 brings the white back about 60 cm ✓. Power 4 brings it back more than a metre, here into the pocket behind ✗.

> Common pitfall: "My screw doesn't work." Usually the tip is not low enough, or the cue stops at the ball. Let it go through.

13 · Screw from long range

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/s03cliplongscrew.webp" alt="Clip: a straight red near the top middle pocket, the white 1.25 metres behind it. Tip low, power 4. The red goes in and the white screws back about 38 centimetres"></div>

The white is 1.25 m from the red. In the model:

| Power | To bring the white back 40 cm ||-------|-------------------------------|| 3 | not possible: even the lowest safe tip (0.5 R) runs on about 20 cm || 4 | tip 0.34 R below centre: back about 38 cm ✓ |

So for long screw, power comes first: the white must still be blue (backspin) when it arrives. Then set the distance with the tip height. Small changes matter: at power 4 the full 0.5 R low sent the white back almost 1.5 m, into the far pocket.

14 · The drag shot: hit firmer, arrive slowly

You want the white to reach a ball 2 m away slowly, at 0.55 m/s. Two ways (model):

| | Starts at | Time to arrive ||-|-----------|----------------|| centre ball, soft | 1.20 m/s | 2.8 s || drag: low, firmer | 2.07 m/s | 2.3 s |

The drag shot loses its extra speed while the backspin wears off. From 96 cm on, the two shots are the same: both roll at the same speed and reach the red at 0.55 m/s. The drag shot just gets there sooner.

Why play it (coaching advice): a firm, normal-feeling stroke is easier to keep straight than a very soft push, and a fast-moving white is less pushed off line by the cloth than a slow one.

Try it at the table

| Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Cue on the line | white on the brown spot, hit it up over the blue and pink spots, centre ball, power 2 | it comes straight back to the tip || Power ladder | white on the baulk line, play power 1, 2, 3, 4 | where it stops matches the picture || Stop ladder | straight blue into a middle pocket, white 30, 60, 90 cm away | the white stops dead every time || Follow, run-through, screw | the same shot | follow 30 cm, run on 15 cm, screw 30 cm, screw 60 cm || Long screw | white 1.25 m from a straight red | back 40 cm, power 4 || Drag | white in baulk, a ball 2 m away; drag, then a soft centre ball | both arrive at the same speed |

Cheat-sheet

Chapter 1 — The debugging loop

Most wasted debugging time comes from changing things at random. The fix is a loop borrowed from the scientific method:

1. Observe. What exactly happened? Copy the full error text. Note the input, the time, the version.2. Hypothesize. Make one specific guess: "one order item has no  key".3. Test one change. Design a check that proves or kills the guess. Change one thing only.4. Record. Write down what you tried and what happened. Then loop.

A tiny log in a text file is enough. It stops you testing the same idea twice, and it becomes your bug report or commit message:

> Watch out: if you change two things and the bug vanishes, you learn nothing. You do not know which change fixed it, or if one of them only hid it.

Chapter 7 — Supply-chain attacks: how they really happen

An attacker who cannot break into your app can break into something your app installs. The main patterns:

This lesson covers the whole of aiming: the ghost ball, the classic miss, ball fractions and the angles they give, thin cuts, why long and thin pots are hard, how much room each pocket gives, and the small "drag" on slow cuts.

Chapter 1 — Finding the aim

1 · Three steps, from the pocket back

1. Line from the pocket through the middle of the ball.2. Where that line comes out of the back: the red dot.3. A ball touching the red dot: the ghost ball. Send the white's centre to the ghost's centre.

The red dot never moves when the white moves. Only the cut changes.

2 · What you see down the cue

Aim at the +, the ghost's centre. It sits on empty cloth, half a ball beyond the red dot. Pick a speck on the cloth there and aim at it.

> Trick: walk in behind the ball first, looking at the pocket. Find the red dot from there, then walk round to the white.

3 · Watch it: a stun pot

The yellow follows the dashed line into the pocket ✓. Hit a little below the middle (a stun) and the white leaves at a right angle (the square). Post 04 shows where it goes with other spins.

4 · The classic miss: aiming at the red dot

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b00clipmiss.webp" alt="Clip: the white is sent straight at the red dot; it touches the yellow early, the hit is too full, and the yellow misses the pocket and hits the cushion"></div>

The red dot is easy to see, so it is tempting to send the white straight at it. But the white is a ball, not a point: it touches early, the hit is too full, the yellow does not turn enough, and it misses ✗.

> Common pitfall: "I keep missing thick." Move the aim out to the ghost's centre: one ball-radius beyond the red dot.

Chapter 2 — Ball fractions and thin cuts

5 · Ball fractions and the angles they give

Down the cue, look at how much of the object ball the white covers. That fraction sets the angle the ball leaves at:

| Cover | Where the white's centre points | The ball leaves at ||-------|--------------------------------|--------------------|| full | the ball's centre | 0° (straight on) || ¾ | halfway between centre and edge | about 14° || ½ | the ball's edge | 30° || ¼ | half a radius outside the edge | about 49° || ⅛ | three-quarters of a radius outside the edge | about 61° |

The half ball is the one to learn first: the white's centre points at the ball's edge, and the ball goes off at 30°. The others are "a bit fuller" or "a bit thinner" than that picture.

> Trick: the angles are not evenly spaced. Moving the aim half a radius, from full to ¾, turns the ball 14°. Moving it only a quarter of a radius, from ¼ to ⅛, turns it another 12°. That is why thin cuts feel fussy.

6 · How thin is very thin?

Past ⅛, the overlap is measured in millimetres:

| The ball turns… | The white overlaps it by… ||-----------------|---------------------------|| 60° | about 7 mm (≈ ⅛ ball) || 70° | about 3 mm || 80° | less than 1 mm |

So an 80° cut is a "nearly miss". Leave yourself fuller pots when you can.

7 · Two ways to aim a thin cut

Use whichever your eye likes; many players check one with the other.

1. Ghost ball. Put the ghost against the far side of the red and aim the white's centre at the ghost's centre. On thin cuts the ghost sits almost a whole ball out to the side.2. Edge to edge. Send the white's edge to pass just inside the red's edge. For a near right angle, "just inside" means less than 1 mm.

> Trick: on very thin cuts, stand up and look from the side first. Decide how far out the ghost is, then get down and keep that picture.

8 · Watch it: almost sideways

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b04clipthin.webp" alt="Clip: the red is near a side cushion, not far from a corner pocket; the white comes from below. The white barely touches the red's left side, the red runs to the right along the cushion into the corner pocket, and the white carries on almost straight to the side cushion and comes back"></div>

The white only grazes the red. The red goes off about 76° from the white's line and runs into the corner ✓. The white hardly notices and carries on.

> Watch out: the red gets only a small part of the white's speed (here about a quarter). This pot needs power 4. At power 2 or 3 the red stops short.

Chapter 3 — What makes a pot hard

9 · Thin cuts spread your error

The same tiny mistake on both (the white 2 mm to one side). Straight pot: the ball's paths stay together. Thin cut: they fan out wide. The thinner the cut, the more an error grows.

10 · Distance shrinks the room

The same straight red, 50 cm from the corner. Move the white back and the room to pot shrinks (model, medium pace):

| White behind the red | Aim may turn by (total) | Room at the red ||----------------------|------------------------|-----------------|| 30 cm | 2.2° | about 12 mm || 80 cm | 0.72° | about 10 mm || 150 cm | 0.36° | about 9 mm |

The room at the red stays about 1 cm. But from further back, 1 cm is a smaller turn of the cue. Twice as far back, about half the room.

11 · Watch it: a long pot

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/s02cliplong.webp" alt="Clip from behind the white in the baulk area. A red sits near the top cushion, not far from the top-right corner pocket, with a yellow dashed line to the pocket. The white rolls the length of the table, the red drops into the corner and the white runs on"></div>

The white is 2.5 m from the red, and the red is 0.6 m from the pocket. The model gives only 0.12° of room: about 5 mm at the red. That is why the long pot is the big test of a straight cue.

What coaches usually advise for long pots (coaching advice, not a rule):

- Centre ball or a touch below, no side. Side bends the white's path (post 05).- Medium pace. Firm enough to reach the pocket at a good speed, not a slam.- Hold still until the red is in. Moving early moves the cue.

> Trick: practise long pots with the white and red in the same places each time. The same picture, ten times, trains the eye faster than ten different pots.

Chapter 4 — How much room the pocket gives

12 · The corner pocket: into the jaws vs along the cushion

| The ball rolls in… | Room for the ball's centre (model) ||--------------------|------------------------------------|| straight into the jaws (45°) | about 10 cm || along the cushion | about 1.6 cm: the ball must stay within about 1.6 cm of the cushion |

A ball sent into the jaws has lots of room. A ball along the cushion has to stay tight to it; drift 2 cm off it and the ball misses. Post 07 covers balls on the cushion.

13 · The middle pocket closes as the angle gets flatter

The middle pocket is cut into a straight cushion, so a ball coming in at a slant meets the far knuckle:

| Coming in… | Room for the ball's centre (model) ||------------|------------------------------------|| straight | about 10 cm || 40° off straight | about 6–7 cm || 60° off straight | about 4 cm |

> Trick: on a slant, aim the ball at the middle of the hole itself (the yellow dot, behind the cushion line), not at the middle of the gap between the knuckles. That keeps it away from the far knuckle.

Chapter 5 — A small correction

14 · Slow cuts get dragged a little (throw)

At contact the white rubs on the red and drags it a little the way the white is moving. This is throw. The red goes a touch fuller (less cut) than the ghost-ball line, more on slow shots.

If your slow cuts keep missing on the thick side:

| Fix | How ||-----|-----|| Overcut a hair | aim a fraction thinner than the ghost ball || Add pace | a firmer hit drags less (power 4: about 1.5 cm off at 1 m instead of 6 cm) || Side | a touch of side the right way cancels it (post 05) |

Try it at the table

| Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Straight pots | white, red and pocket in a line, 30 cm then 80 cm apart | the white stops dead (stun, full ball) || Fraction ladder | the same red; play it full, ¾, ½, ¼ by moving the white | say the fraction before each shot; the ball goes where you said || Ghost check | put a coin where the ghost ball sits, look from behind the white, take the coin away, play | your eye learns where the ghost is || Thin cuts | a red 30 cm from a corner, near the cushion; the white from the middle | pot it at power 4 || Long pots | the white in baulk, a red near a top corner | ten in a row from the same spot || Middle slant | the blue on its spot, the white at three angles | aim at the hole's centre; how many drop? |

Chapter 1 — The cue action

1 · The cue on the line

The white goes where the cue points at the moment of the hit. Line the cue up behind the white, on the line to where you aim, and keep it on that line from the back swing to the end of the stroke.

> Watch out: a small turn of the cue, about 7° here, misses a red only half a table away.

2 · Follow through

Let the cue run on past where the white was, along the same line. A cue that stops at the ball usually turns at the last moment ✗.

3 · Feather, pause, strike

> Common pitfall: a quick, jerky back swing. The cue changes speed and line. Slow back, smooth through.

4 · A pre-shot routine

Do the same steps in the same order on every shot. The point is repetition: the stroke stays the same under pressure as in practice.

5 · Chalk, and stay in the safe zone

- Chalk the tip every shot or two. A dry tip slides off the white: a miscue.- Keep the tip inside about half the radius from the centre. Further out, even a chalked tip miscues (post 03).

Chapter 2 — Tip height

"Raise the cue 20°" means lifting the back (butt) end of the cue, so the cue points down at the white. The tip still hits the same spot. The yellow wedge is the angle between the cue and the cloth.

| Elevation | When ||-----------|------|| about 3° | every normal shot: as level as the cushion allows || about 12° | the white near a cushion (section 9) || about 20° | swerve on purpose (posts 05, 09, 14) || about 60° | massé, a big curve (post 14) |

8 · Keep it low

The same aim and the same side, only the cue raised more each time (model, from post 14). With side, a raised cue curves the white: 10 cm off the line after 1 m at 10°, 55 cm at 45°.

So keep the cue as low as the table lets you. If normal long pots keep missing, check your elevation first.

9 · Near a cushion

Near a cushion the cue has to pass over the rail, which is higher than the cloth, so it points down more (geometry of these set-ups):

| White | Cue raised ||-------|------------|| 20 cm off the cushion | about 12° || close to the cushion | about 13° |

You can only reach the top half of the white. Keep the tip near centre, the stroke short and smooth, and let pace, not spin, do the work.

10 · Over a ball

When a ball sits just behind the white, the cue has to go over it. Here, with a red 15 cm behind, the cue must be raised about 17°.

- Play it smoothly and not too hard.- Keep the tip near centre: with this angle, side curves the white (section 8) and screw is weaker.- Don't touch the ball behind: moving any ball with the cue is a foul.

Chapter 1 — Two component worlds

Terminology

- React Server Components (RSC) — a React architecture in which some components run only in a server environment (at build time or per request) and never ship their code to the browser.- Server Component — the default for every component under . Can be , can read files, databases and secrets. Cannot use state, effects, or browser APIs.- Client Component — a component in the client module graph. Rendered to HTML on the server for the first load, then hydrated and interactive in the browser. Can use hooks and event handlers.- RSC payload (also called the Flight payload) — the serialised result of rendering the Server Component tree: rendered output, placeholders for Client Components with references to their JavaScript, and the props passed to them.- Hydration — React attaching event handlers and state to server-rendered HTML so it becomes interactive.- Boundary — the point in the module graph where a server module imports a  module.

Server Components are not SSR

SSR is an old idea: run the whole React tree on the server, emit HTML, then ship all of the same code to the browser and hydrate it. Every component exists twice, once on each side.

Server Components change the second half. A Server Component runs only on the server; its code is never sent to the browser, so it has nothing to hydrate. Only Client Components are both server-rendered (for the first HTML) and shipped (for hydration).

| Question | Server Component | Client Component ||---|---|---|| Where does its code run? | Server only (build or request time) | Server (first-load HTML) and browser || Is its code in the JS bundle? | No | Yes ||  component,  in body | Yes | No (use  to unwrap a promise) || , ,  | No | Yes || Read secrets, DB, file system | Yes | Must not || Re-renders on state change | No (re-rendered only by a new server request) | Yes || Default in  | Yes | Only below a  boundary |

The first-load pipeline

On a first visit to a dynamic route, three things happen in order:

1. RSC render. The server renders the Server Component tree. Wherever it meets a Client Component, it does not run it; it writes a placeholder plus a module reference and the serialised props. The result is the RSC payload.2. SSR of the client parts. The server then renders the Client Components to HTML, using the payload as input, so the first response is complete HTML.3. Browser. The HTML paints immediately (non-interactive). The client JavaScript loads, React reads the payload (inlined in the HTML) to reconcile the tree, and hydrates only the Client Components.

On a later client navigation, step 2 and the HTML are skipped. The router fetches only the RSC payload for the segments that change and merges it into the existing tree.

> Trick: when asking "does this code run in the browser?", ignore the file name and folder. Follow the imports from the nearest  file. That is the only rule the bundler uses.

Chapter 2 — DNS: from name to IP address

Packets are routed by IP address, not by name. DNS (RFC 1034/1035) is the distributed phone book that turns names into addresses.

There are 13 named root server identities ( to ), each served from many sites by anycast. The authoritative server holds the real records, and is usually run by your DNS host or CDN.

Every answer carries a TTL (time to live) in seconds. Caches may reuse the answer until the TTL runs out. That is why a DNS change takes time to reach everyone: old answers live until their TTL ends.

> Trick: before you move a site to a new IP, lower the TTL (for example to 300 s) a day ahead. After the move, raise it again.

Chapter 4 — The TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS (Transport Layer Security) gives three things: confidentiality (only the two ends can read), integrity (changes on the way are detected), and authentication (you reached the real ).

TLS 1.3 was defined in RFC 8446 (August 2018). In July 2026 the IETF published RFC 9846, which obsoletes RFC 8446. It calls itself a minor update that keeps the version number 1.3 and stays backward compatible. It also obsoletes RFC 5246, the TLS 1.2 spec.

1. ClientHello. The client guesses which key-exchange group the server accepts and sends a public key for it at once. This guess is what saves a round trip compared with TLS 1.2.2. ServerHello. The server sends its own public key. Both sides now run ECDHE (Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman, Ephemeral): each mixes its private key with the other's public key and gets the same shared secret. Someone watching the wire cannot compute it.3. Keys are derived from that secret. Everything after ServerHello is encrypted, including the certificate.4. CertificateVerify. The server signs a hash of all handshake messages so far with the private key of its certificate. This proves it owns the certificate, not just a copy.5. Finished. Each side sends a MAC over the whole transcript. If an attacker changed any earlier message, the MACs do not match and the connection dies.6. The client sends its HTTP request together with its Finished. Total: one round trip (1-RTT).

The key pairs are ephemeral: new for each connection, then thrown away. So stealing the server's certificate key later does not decrypt recorded old sessions. This is forward secrecy.

Resumption and 0-RTT

After a handshake the server can send a session ticket. Next time the client presents it as a pre-shared key (PSK) and may send data in its very first flight: 0-RTT early data.

> Watch out: 0-RTT data can be replayed. An attacker who records the first flight can send it again, and the server may act on it twice. RFC 8446 says 0-RTT data has no non-replay guarantee between connections, and it is not forward secret either. Allow it only for safe, idempotent requests such as . Chapter 5 — Certificates and the chain of trust

Key exchange gives encryption, but encryption with whom? The certificate answers that. It is a statement, "this public key belongs to ", signed by a Certificate Authority (CA).

- Subject Alternative Name (SAN) — the host names the certificate is valid for. Clients check the SAN, not the old Common Name.- notBefore / notAfter — the validity window. Outside it, the certificate is rejected.- Issuer — who signed it. The issuer has its own certificate, and so on up the chain.

The server sends the leaf plus the intermediates. It does not send the root: the client must already have it in its trust store. The client builds a path from the leaf to a trusted root and checks every signature, the dates, the host name against the SAN, and key-usage limits. How long a certificate may live — the rules today

Public TLS certificates follow the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements. Ballot SC-081v3, adopted on April 11, 2025, shrinks the maximum validity in steps (Baseline Requirements, section 6.3.2):

| Certificate issued on or after | Issued before | Maximum validity ||--------------------------------|---------------|------------------|| — | 2026-03-15 | 398 days || 2026-03-15 | 2027-03-15 | 200 days ← applies now || 2027-03-15 | 2029-03-15 | 100 days || 2029-03-15 | — | 47 days |

The same ballot cuts how long a CA may reuse a domain-validation result: 200 days from 2026-03-15, 100 days from 2027-03-15, 10 days from 2029-03-15. So a certificate issued today lives at most 200 days, and yearly manual renewal is already gone.

Revocation. If a key leaks, the CA can revoke the certificate and publish that in a CRL (signed list of revoked serial numbers) or via OCSP (ask about one certificate). Let's Encrypt turned off its OCSP service on August 6, 2025, and now publishes revocation data only through CRLs. Short lifetimes also limit damage: a leaked short-lived certificate soon stops working anyway.

> Trick: every publicly trusted certificate is recorded in public Certificate Transparency logs. Search your domain on a CT search site such as crt.sh to see every certificate ever issued for it.

Chapter 12 — Don't fully validate email with a regex

Real email syntax (RFC 5322) allows quoted local parts, comments and other rare forms, so a "complete" regex is huge, hard to read and still does not tell you whether the mailbox exists. The HTML standard itself does not follow RFC 5322 for : it calls its own simpler rule a "willful violation" of that RFC, because the RFC grammar is too strict in some places and too loose in others to be practical.

A sensible approach:

1. Do a light sanity check: one , no spaces, a dot in the domain part.2. Send a confirmation email. Only a click on the link proves the address works.

This check rejects some legal but odd addresses (such as ) and accepts some that bounce. That is fine: its only job is to catch typos early.

Chapter 13 — Catastrophic backtracking and ReDoS

Most engines (Python, JavaScript, .NET, PCRE, Java) are backtracking engines. When part of a pattern fails, they go back and try the other ways the earlier parts could have matched. Usually there are only a few ways. But nested or overlapping quantifiers create an exponential number of ways, and on text that almost matches, the engine tries them all.

The textbook case is  against . The inner and outer  can split the s in about 2ⁿ ways, and the final  makes every split fail. A timing on one Windows 11 laptop with Python 3.11.9 (one run each — an informal measurement, not a benchmark; your numbers will differ, the doubling will not):

Every 2 extra characters cost about 4 times more. Extrapolating, 40 characters would take more than ten hours. That is ReDoS (regular expression denial of service): one short, crafted input keeps a CPU core busy. OWASP lists typical "evil" shapes: , , , . Real-life versions look innocent, like  for "words separated by spaces" — in the same test, 22 letters plus a  took 0.25 s.

This is not theory. On 2 July 2019, one Cloudflare WAF rule with a badly backtracking regex (the part ) exhausted CPUs across Cloudflare's network and caused about 27 minutes of downtime.

Fixes, from best to last resort

1. Rewrite the pattern so each character can be matched only one way.

| Evil | Safe | Why ||------|------|-----||  |  | the nesting added nothing ||  |  | same language, one way to split ||  |  | each repeat must end with a space, so splits are fixed |

On 100,000 characters, the rewritten  finished in about 1 ms in the same test. It also gave the same yes/no answer as the original on every test string tried (, , , , , ).

2. Forbid backtracking into a part with an atomic group  or a possessive quantifier (, , ). Once matched, the engine will not try other splits. Python supports both since 3.11; PCRE and Java support both; .NET supports atomic groups only; JavaScript supports neither.

3. Use a linear-time engine for untrusted patterns or input. Google's RE2, Go's  and Rust's  crate guarantee time linear in the input size (Rust documents O(m × n) for pattern size m and text size n). The price: no backreferences and no lookarounds. .NET 7 and later offer  with the same kind of guarantee; Windows PowerShell 5.1 runs on .NET Framework and does not have it. After its outage, Cloudflare planned to move to RE2 or the Rust engine for exactly this reason.
