e:T10c9,On a straight pot you can make the white stop, follow the ball, or come back towards you. It is the same aim each time; only the tip spot and the power change. This is the first real control of the white, and the start of position play.

> Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw (this post) → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack → 12 Safety → 13 Position · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes → 15 Swerve and special spots.

Learning Outcome

- Stop the white dead on a straight pot.- Make it follow through, or screw it back.- Know why a longer shot needs a lower tip or more power.

1 · Stop: the white stays where it hits

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipstop.webp" alt="Clip: straight red into the middle pocket. Tip a little below centre, power 3. The red goes in and the white stops exactly where it touched the red, marked with a yellow ring"></div>

A little below centre, power 3. The white arrives still sliding, with no spin left, so it hands all its speed to the red and stops ✓.

2 · Follow: the white runs on

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipfollow.webp" alt="Clip: the same pot. Tip above centre, power 2. The red goes in and the white rolls on after it, about a third of a metre"></div>

Above centre, power 2. The white is rolling forwards when it hits, so it keeps going after the red, here about a third of a metre.

> Watch out: too much follow on a straight pot and the white follows the red into the pocket (an in-off, a foul).

3 · Screw: the white comes back

<div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b02clipscrew.webp" alt="Clip: the same pot. Tip low, power 3. The red goes in and the white spins back towards the player, about 60 centimetres"></div>

Low, power 3, and let the cue go through the white. The backspin is still on when it hits, so the white comes back, here about 60 cm.

> Trick: keep the cue level and low. A raised cue on screw shots gives curve and miscues.

4 · Further away? Hit lower, or firmer

Backspin wears off while the white slides on the cloth. So to stop the white:

| Distance | Tip | Power ||----------|-----|-------|| short (30 cm) | just below centre | 3 || medium (80 cm) | clearly lower | 3 || long (1.5 m) | low | 4 (more power keeps the spin on) |

The same idea works for screw: a longer shot needs a lower tip, more power, or both.

5 · More power, more screw

The same low tip: power 3 brings the white back about 60 cm ✓. Power 4 brings it back more than a metre. Here that is too much: it runs into the pocket behind ✗.

> Common pitfall: "My screw doesn't work." Usually the tip is not low enough, or the cue stops at the ball. Let it go through.

6 · Try it at the table

| Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Stop ladder | straight blue into a middle pocket, white 30 cm, then 60, then 90 cm away | the white stops dead every time || Follow and screw | the same shot | follow 30 cm, then screw 30 cm, then screw 60 cm || Mark the spot | put a coin where the white should stop | land on the coin |

Cheat-sheet

| Want | Tip | Power ||------|-----|-------|| Stop (straight) | a little below centre | medium (3) || Follow | above centre | gentle to medium (2–3) || Screw | low (inside the safe zone) | medium to firm (3–4) || Longer shot | lower tip, or more power | |

Chapter 1 — Who are you, and what may you do?

Two questions sit behind every protected request:

1. Authentication (AuthN) — who are you? The user proves an identity, for example with a password plus a code.2. Authorization (AuthZ) — what may you do? The app checks whether this identity may read or change this thing.

They fail in different ways. A perfect login does not help if any logged-in user can open  that belongs to someone else. That second bug is authorization, and Dev Essentials 12 · OWASP Top 10 shows it as "Broken Access Control".

HTTP has a status code for each.  means "I do not know who you are" (not logged in, or a bad token).  means "I know who you are, and you may not do this". A  is also fine for "not yours" when you do not want to reveal that the thing exists.

Terminology

- Credential — the thing that proves identity: a password, a private key, a code.- Factor — a kind of proof: something you know (password), have (phone, security key), or are (fingerprint). MFA means two or more different kinds.- Session — the server remembers "this browser is logged in as Ada" after the login step.- Token — a string the client sends with each request to show it is logged in. It can be a random ID (a reference) or a signed document such as a JWT (a value).- Identity provider (IdP) — a service that logs users in for other apps (Google, Microsoft Entra ID, Okta, Keycloak).

Chapter 2 — Storing passwords

Never plain text, never reversible encryption

If you store passwords as plain text, one database leak exposes every user — and most users reuse passwords on other sites. Encryption is not the answer either. Encrypted data can be decrypted, so whoever steals the key gets every password back. You want a one-way function: easy to check a guess, impossible to reverse. That is a hash. But not any hash.

Why SHA-256 is the wrong hash

SHA-256 and MD5 are built to be fast. A modern GPU can compute billions of them per second. If an attacker steals your table of SHA-256 password hashes, they hash every word from a big list of leaked passwords and compare. Password hashing functions are built to be slow and memory-hungry on purpose. One login costs your server a fraction of a second. For an attacker trying billions of guesses, the same cost becomes enormous. Being memory-hard matters too: GPUs have many cores but little memory per core, so a hash that needs many MiB per guess slows them down.

Salt and pepper

- Salt — a random value, different for every password, stored next to the hash. Two users with the same password get different hashes, and precomputed tables ("rainbow tables") become useless. Modern libraries make and store the salt for you.- Pepper — one secret value shared by all passwords, stored outside the database (a secrets manager or an HSM). OWASP says a pepper "should not be stored along with the generated hash". If only the database leaks, the hashes cannot be cracked without it. A pepper is defence in depth, not a replacement for a slow hash.

The algorithms and the current OWASP parameters

The OWASP Password Storage Cheat Sheet (checked September 2026) gives this order of preference. The quotes are exact:

| Algorithm | When | OWASP parameters ||-----------|------|------------------|| Argon2id | First choice | "a minimum configuration of 19 MiB of memory, an iteration count of 2, and 1 degree of parallelism" || scrypt | If Argon2id is not available | "a minimum CPU/memory cost parameter of (2^17), a minimum block size of 8 (1024 bytes), and a parallelization parameter of 1" || bcrypt | Legacy systems | "a work factor of 10 or more and with a password limit of 72 bytes" || PBKDF2 | When FIPS-140 compliance is required | "PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256: 600,000 iterations"; "PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512: 220,000 iterations" |

OWASP also lists Argon2id settings that give about the same protection. You can trade memory for iterations: , , , , .

> Watch out: bcrypt only uses the first 72 bytes of the input in most implementations. Longer passwords are silently cut, so two different long passwords can match. OWASP says to "enforce a maximum password length of 72 bytes". Note that 72 bytes can be fewer than 72 characters in UTF-8 (see Dev Essentials 01 · Text Encoding). Pre-hashing the password before bcrypt has its own traps (null bytes, "password shucking"), so for new systems just choose Argon2id.

Chapter 4 — Password rules from NIST SP 800-63B-4

NIST SP 800-63B-4 is the US government guideline on authentication. The final version was published on 31 July 2025. It supersedes SP 800-63B (revision 3). Many companies follow it, and it drops several old habits. The key rules, quoted:

| Rule | NIST 800-63B-4 text ||------|---------------------|| Minimum length, password alone | "SHALL require passwords that are used as a single-factor authentication mechanism to be a minimum of 15 characters in length" || Minimum length, with MFA | may be shorter "but SHALL require them to be a minimum of eight characters in length" || Maximum length | "SHOULD permit a maximum password length of at least 64 characters" || Composition rules | "SHALL NOT impose other composition rules (e.g., requiring mixtures of different character types)" || Rotation | "SHALL NOT require subscribers to change passwords periodically" — but "SHALL force a change if there is evidence that the authenticator has been compromised" || Blocklist | compare new passwords "against a blocklist that contains known commonly used, expected, or compromised passwords" || Unicode | "Each Unicode code point SHALL be counted as a single character" || Password managers | "SHALL allow the use of password managers and autofill functionality"; "SHOULD permit" paste || No truncation | "SHALL verify the entire submitted password (e.g., not truncate it)" |

Chapter 5 — Hardening the login

Rate limiting

Without a limit, an attacker can try thousands of passwords per minute. NIST requires that the verifier "SHALL limit consecutive failed authentication attempts using a specific authenticator on a single subscriber account to no more than 100". Most apps choose a much lower number, plus a limit per IP address. The app in Chapter 7 allows 5 failures per account per 15 minutes. Dev Essentials 14 · API Reliability covers rate-limit algorithms in depth.

> Watch out: a hard lockout per account lets anyone lock a real user out on purpose. Prefer growing delays or a temporary block, and give the real user a way to recover.

Generic error messages

Say "Invalid username or password" in both cases. If "no such user" and "wrong password" give different messages, attackers can find which accounts exist (user enumeration). Watch the timing too. If unknown users return instantly while real users take 50 ms to hash, the delay leaks the same fact. The Chapter 7 code hashes a dummy value for unknown users to keep the timing close. Registration and "forgot password" pages leak the same way, so give them neutral replies such as "If that account exists, we sent an email".

MFA with TOTP (RFC 6238)

A Time-based One-Time Password is the 6-digit code in an authenticator app. Server and app share a secret. Both compute  and cut the result down to 6 digits. RFC 6238 builds on HOTP (RFC 4226) and recommends a 30-second time step. It needs only the standard library:

Passkeys and WebAuthn

TOTP codes can still be phished: a fake site asks for the code and replays it within 30 seconds. Passkeys fix this. A passkey is a key pair created by the user's device or password manager through the WebAuthn browser API. The server stores only the public key. At login, the device signs a random challenge with the private key. The browser ties each credential to the site's origin, so a look-alike domain cannot use it. WebAuthn Level 3 became a W3C Recommendation in 2026, and NIST 800-63B-4 requires phishing-resistant authenticators at its highest level (AAL3). Use a maintained WebAuthn server library rather than writing the checks yourself.

The revocation problem

Stateless has a price. Once a JWT is issued, it stays valid until . Logout, a password change, or "block this account" cannot cancel it, unless you add a denylist, and then you have a server-side store again. The usual answer is short-lived access tokens (minutes) plus a long-lived refresh token that is tracked server-side, so it can be revoked. Chapter 9 — OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect

What OAuth 2.0 is for

OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749) is about delegated authorization. It answers: "may this app act on my behalf at that API?" For example, a printing site asks to read your photos from a cloud drive, without ever seeing your cloud password. Chapter 1 — The debugging loop

Most wasted debugging time comes from changing things at random. The fix is a loop borrowed from the scientific method:

1. Observe. What exactly happened? Copy the full error text. Note the input, the time, the version.2. Hypothesize. Make one specific guess: "one order item has no  key".3. Test one change. Design a check that proves or kills the guess. Change one thing only.4. Record. Write down what you tried and what happened. Then loop.

A tiny log in a text file is enough. It stops you testing the same idea twice, and it becomes your bug report or commit message:

> Watch out: if you change two things and the bug vanishes, you learn nothing. You do not know which change fixed it, or if one of them only hid it.

Chapter 7 — Supply-chain attacks: how they really happen

An attacker who cannot break into your app can break into something your app installs. The main patterns:

- Typosquatting — a malicious package whose name is one typo away from a popular one.- Dependency confusion — a public package with the same name as your private internal one, which a misconfigured installer prefers.- Compromised maintainer account — a stolen password or token, or a phished 2FA code, followed by a malicious release of a real, trusted package.- Malicious install scripts —  /  code runs on your laptop or CI runner the moment you install, before you import anything.- Takeover of an abandoned asset — a domain, CDN or package that passes to a new owner.

Chapter 1 — Two component worlds

Terminology

- React Server Components (RSC) — a React architecture in which some components run only in a server environment (at build time or per request) and never ship their code to the browser.- Server Component — the default for every component under . Can be , can read files, databases and secrets. Cannot use state, effects, or browser APIs.- Client Component — a component in the client module graph. Rendered to HTML on the server for the first load, then hydrated and interactive in the browser. Can use hooks and event handlers.- RSC payload (also called the Flight payload) — the serialised result of rendering the Server Component tree: rendered output, placeholders for Client Components with references to their JavaScript, and the props passed to them.- Hydration — React attaching event handlers and state to server-rendered HTML so it becomes interactive.- Boundary — the point in the module graph where a server module imports a  module.

Server Components are not SSR

SSR is an old idea: run the whole React tree on the server, emit HTML, then ship all of the same code to the browser and hydrate it. Every component exists twice, once on each side.

Server Components change the second half. A Server Component runs only on the server; its code is never sent to the browser, so it has nothing to hydrate. Only Client Components are both server-rendered (for the first HTML) and shipped (for hydration).

The first-load pipeline

On a first visit to a dynamic route, three things happen in order:

1. RSC render. The server renders the Server Component tree. Wherever it meets a Client Component, it does not run it; it writes a placeholder plus a module reference and the serialised props. The result is the RSC payload.2. SSR of the client parts. The server then renders the Client Components to HTML, using the payload as input, so the first response is complete HTML.3. Browser. The HTML paints immediately (non-interactive). The client JavaScript loads, React reads the payload (inlined in the HTML) to reconcile the tree, and hydrates only the Client Components.

On a later client navigation, step 2 and the HTML are skipped. The router fetches only the RSC payload for the segments that change and merges it into the existing tree.

Chapter 2 — DNS: from name to IP address

Packets are routed by IP address, not by name. DNS (RFC 1034/1035) is the distributed phone book that turns names into addresses.

There are 13 named root server identities ( to ), each served from many sites by anycast. The authoritative server holds the real records, and is usually run by your DNS host or CDN.

Every answer carries a TTL (time to live) in seconds. Caches may reuse the answer until the TTL runs out. That is why a DNS change takes time to reach everyone: old answers live until their TTL ends.

> Trick: before you move a site to a new IP, lower the TTL (for example to 300 s) a day ahead. After the move, raise it again.

Chapter 4 — The TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS (Transport Layer Security) gives three things: confidentiality (only the two ends can read), integrity (changes on the way are detected), and authentication (you reached the real ).

TLS 1.3 was defined in RFC 8446 (August 2018). In July 2026 the IETF published RFC 9846, which obsoletes RFC 8446. It calls itself a minor update that keeps the version number 1.3 and stays backward compatible. It also obsoletes RFC 5246, the TLS 1.2 spec.

1. ClientHello. The client guesses which key-exchange group the server accepts and sends a public key for it at once. This guess is what saves a round trip compared with TLS 1.2.2. ServerHello. The server sends its own public key. Both sides now run ECDHE (Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman, Ephemeral): each mixes its private key with the other's public key and gets the same shared secret. Someone watching the wire cannot compute it.3. Keys are derived from that secret. Everything after ServerHello is encrypted, including the certificate.4. CertificateVerify. The server signs a hash of all handshake messages so far with the private key of its certificate. This proves it owns the certificate, not just a copy.5. Finished. Each side sends a MAC over the whole transcript. If an attacker changed any earlier message, the MACs do not match and the connection dies.6. The client sends its HTTP request together with its Finished. Total: one round trip (1-RTT).

The key pairs are ephemeral: new for each connection, then thrown away. So stealing the server's certificate key later does not decrypt recorded old sessions. This is forward secrecy.

From OpenSSL 3.5 on, the default key shares offer the hybrid post-quantum group  (classic X25519 plus ML-KEM-768) as well as X25519, so you may see that name instead.

Resumption and 0-RTT

After a handshake the server can send a session ticket. Next time the client presents it as a pre-shared key (PSK) and may send data in its very first flight: 0-RTT early data.

> Watch out: 0-RTT data can be replayed. An attacker who records the first flight can send it again, and the server may act on it twice. RFC 8446 says 0-RTT data has no non-replay guarantee between connections, and it is not forward secret either. Allow it only for safe, idempotent requests such as . Chapter 5 — Certificates and the chain of trust

Key exchange gives encryption, but encryption with whom? The certificate answers that. It is a statement, "this public key belongs to ", signed by a Certificate Authority (CA).

- Subject Alternative Name (SAN) — the host names the certificate is valid for. Clients check the SAN, not the old Common Name.- notBefore / notAfter — the validity window. Outside it, the certificate is rejected.- Issuer — who signed it. The issuer has its own certificate, and so on up the chain.

The server sends the leaf plus the intermediates. It does not send the root: the client must already have it in its trust store. The client builds a path from the leaf to a trusted root and checks every signature, the dates, the host name against the SAN, and key-usage limits. How long a certificate may live — the rules today

Public TLS certificates follow the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements. Ballot SC-081v3, adopted on April 11, 2025, shrinks the maximum validity in steps (Baseline Requirements, section 6.3.2):

| Certificate issued on or after | Issued before | Maximum validity ||--------------------------------|---------------|------------------|| — | 2026-03-15 | 398 days || 2026-03-15 | 2027-03-15 | 200 days ← applies now || 2027-03-15 | 2029-03-15 | 100 days || 2029-03-15 | — | 47 days |

The same ballot cuts how long a CA may reuse a domain-validation result: 200 days from 2026-03-15, 100 days from 2027-03-15, 10 days from 2029-03-15. So a certificate issued today lives at most 200 days, and yearly manual renewal is already gone.

Revocation. If a key leaks, the CA can revoke the certificate and publish that in a CRL (signed list of revoked serial numbers) or via OCSP (ask about one certificate). Let's Encrypt turned off its OCSP service on August 6, 2025, and now publishes revocation data only through CRLs. Short lifetimes also limit damage: a leaked short-lived certificate soon stops working anyway.

> Trick: every publicly trusted certificate is recorded in public Certificate Transparency logs. Search your domain on a CT search site such as crt.sh to see every certificate ever issued for it.

Chapter 12 — Don't fully validate email with a regex

Real email syntax (RFC 5322) allows quoted local parts, comments and other rare forms, so a "complete" regex is huge, hard to read and still does not tell you whether the mailbox exists. The HTML standard itself does not follow RFC 5322 for : it calls its own simpler rule a "willful violation" of that RFC, because the RFC grammar is too strict in some places and too loose in others to be practical.

A sensible approach:

1. Do a light sanity check: one , no spaces, a dot in the domain part.2. Send a confirmation email. Only a click on the link proves the address works.

This check rejects some legal but odd addresses (such as ) and accepts some that bounce. That is fine: its only job is to catch typos early.

Chapter 13 — Catastrophic backtracking and ReDoS

Most engines (Python, JavaScript, .NET, PCRE, Java) are backtracking engines. When part of a pattern fails, they go back and try the other ways the earlier parts could have matched. Usually there are only a few ways. But nested or overlapping quantifiers create an exponential number of ways, and on text that almost matches, the engine tries them all.

The textbook case is  against . The inner and outer  can split the s in about 2ⁿ ways, and the final  makes every split fail. A timing on one Windows 11 laptop with Python 3.11.9 (one run each — an informal measurement, not a benchmark; your numbers will differ, the doubling will not):

2. Forbid backtracking into a part with an atomic group  or a possessive quantifier (, , ). Once matched, the engine will not try other splits. Python supports both since 3.11; PCRE and Java support both; .NET supports atomic groups only; JavaScript supports neither.
