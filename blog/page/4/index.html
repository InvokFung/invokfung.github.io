AWS Course — 00 · Cloud Fundamentals, Regions, IAM, and First Steps
Created2026-05-19|Cloud AWS
AWS is the largest public cloud provider, offering over 200 services from raw compute and storage to managed databases, ML training, IoT, and satellite ground stations. For a beginner, that menu is overwhelming. This first post grounds the rest of the series in the core mental model: what the cloud actually is, how AWS organises its physical world (regions and AZs), how you authenticate (IAM), and how you avoid an unwanted bill in week one. The remaining posts go service by service. > Series map: 00 Fundamentals (this post) → 01 EC2 & VPC → 02 Load Balancers & Auto Scaling → 03 Lambda & Serverless → 04 Step Functions → 05 Terraform IaC → (future: S3 deep dive · RDS · DynamoDB · ECS/EKS · CloudWatch · CI/CD · cost optimisation · security best practices). Learning Outcome - Explain what "cloud computing" means and the IaaS / PaaS / SaaS layers.- Locate AWS regions and availability zones; understand why they matter for latency and failure isolation.- Create an AWS account with billing alerts in place.- Understand IAM: users, groups, roles, policies — and the rule "never use the root account".- Use the AWS CLI to make your first API call.- Recognise the free tier and the most common surprise-bill traps. Chapter 1 — What the cloud is Terminology - Cloud computing — renting compute, storage, network, and services from a provider on-demand, billed by usage rather than capital purchase.- Region — a geographic area (e.g., in Northern Virginia, in Hong Kong). Each region is fully independent.- Availability Zone (AZ) — one or more isolated data centres inside a region, connected by low-latency links. Each region has ≥ 3 AZs.- Edge location — smaller PoPs (point of presence) used by CloudFront (CDN) and Route 53 (DNS). Hundreds worldwide.- Service — a single AWS product. EC2, S3, Lambda are services.- API — every AWS service is driven by an HTTPS API. Console, CLI, SDKs, and IaC tools all call the same APIs underneath. Service-model layers | Layer | What the provider runs | What you run | Example AWS ||-------|------------------------|--------------|-------------|| IaaS (Infrastructure) | hardware, virtualisation, network | OS + everything above | EC2, EBS, VPC || PaaS (Platform) | OS + runtime | your app code | Elastic Beanstalk, App Runner || FaaS (Functions) | runtime + scheduling | a single function | Lambda || SaaS (Software) | everything | only data + config | WorkMail, Connect | Trick: when picking a service, ask "how much do I want to manage?". The deeper you go (IaaS → SaaS), the less control and the less ops work. There is no universal best — pick the layer that matches your team's appetite. Grade 3 is where the syllabus starts feeling like real music theory: triplets, minor scales in three flavours, three more keys, primary triads (I, IV, V), and your first written chord symbols. The repertoire jumps too — Bach two-part inventions, Burgmüller's middle studies, Schumann children's pieces, simple Tchaikovsky. > Series map: 00 → 01 → 02 → 03 Grade 3 (this post) → 04 Grade 4. Chapter 1 — Three More Keys: A, E♭, and Their Minors | Major | Sharps/Flats | Relative minor ||-------|--------------|----------------|| C | none | A m || G | F♯ | E m || D | F♯ C♯ | B m || A | F♯ C♯ G♯ | F♯ m || F | B♭ | D m || B♭ | B♭ E♭ | G m || E♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ | C m | A major (3 sharps) and E♭ major (3 flats) extend the symmetric pair around C. > Trick: visualise the circle of fifths as a clock. C is at 12. Each step clockwise adds a sharp (G, D, A, E, B…). Each step counter-clockwise adds a flat (F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭…). Grade 3 covers ±3 steps from C: clockwise to A, counter-clockwise to E♭. Reading a fast key signature When you see three sharps in the order F♯ C♯ G♯, the last is G♯ → up one semitone → A major (or its relative F♯ minor). When you see three flats in the order B♭ E♭ A♭, the second-to-last is E♭ → E♭ major (or its relative C minor). Chapter 2 — Minor Scales: Three Flavours A minor key has one key signature (the natural minor) but musicians use three common scale shapes built from that signature. Natural minor (自然小調) Use the relative-minor key signature, start from the minor tonic. Pattern in semitones: $$\textnormal{W H W W H W W} \quad = \quad 2,1,2,2,1,2,2$$ Harmonic minor (和聲小調) Raise the 7th degree of the natural minor. The interval between 6 and 7 becomes an augmented 2nd (3 semitones) — gives the unmistakable "exotic" sound. Melodic minor (旋律小調) Ascending: raise the 6th and 7th. Descending: revert to natural minor. > Where I got stuck: I kept mixing harmonic and melodic.> - Harmonic minor exists to fix harmony — it gives the V chord its major-3rd leading tone (G♯ in A minor) so the V → i cadence sounds strong. Raise the 7 only.> - Melodic minor exists to smooth melody — the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd (F → G♯) is awkward to sing, so going up you also raise the 6 (F → F♯) to "smooth" the gap. Coming down you don't need the lift, so revert.>> Framing them by purpose (harmony-fixing vs melody-smoothing) made them stop blurring. All three on one diagram Chapter 3 — Triplets A triplet is three notes played in the time of two of the same value. Notated with a "3" above or below a group: In 4/4, a beat that would normally hold two quavers (♪♪) is squeezed to fit three (♪♪♪). The three notes are equal, faster than quavers, slower than triplet semiquavers. Counting triplets Subdivide each affected beat into three syllables: "tri-pa-let" or "1-trip-let, 2-trip-let". Pick one and stick with it. > Trick: when first practising triplets, tap the beat with your foot while saying "1-trip-let, 2-trip-let, 3-trip-let, 4-trip-let" out loud. Foot is the beat (4 taps per bar), voice is the subdivision (12 syllables per bar). Once you can do this without the piano, the piano part is mechanical. Duplet — the inverse In compound time (where each beat naturally divides into 3), a duplet is two notes played in the time of three. Marked "2". Less common at Grade 3 but worth knowing. Chapter 5 — Augmented and Diminished Intervals (just the tritone) Grade 3 expands the interval qualities to include augmented and diminished — but only for the augmented 4th (A4) and diminished 5th (d5), which are the same six-semitone gap (the tritone, 三全音). These two are enharmonic: same sound, different spelling. Whether you call it A4 or d5 depends on note names — count the staff positions: - C → F♯: positions = 4 (C–D–E–F) → 4th → "augmented" (one semitone larger than P4).- C → G♭: positions = 5 (C–D–E–F–G) → 5th → "diminished" (one semitone smaller than P5). > Trick: a tritone divides the octave exactly in half ($12 / 2 = 6$ semitones). It's the most dissonant interval in tonal music — historically called diabolus in musica ("the devil in music") because medieval theorists found it so unstable they avoided writing it. Today it's the engine of jazz and blues. Knowing the history hooks the memory. Chapter 6 — Primary Triads (I, IV, V) A triad (三和弦) = three notes stacked in thirds. Root, third, fifth. The primary triads of any key are built on degrees 1, 4, 5 — the tonic, subdominant, dominant. They handle the bulk of harmonic motion in tonal music. In a major key > Trick: in a major key, the primary triads (I, IV, V) are all major chords. Roman numerals are uppercase to show this. The supportive triads (ii, iii, vi) are minor — lowercase. The vii° is diminished — lowercase + degree mark. In a minor key (harmonic) Because harmonic minor raises the 7th, the V chord becomes major (it has the raised leading tone) — even though the key is minor. > Where I got stuck: V in minor is major. This seems like a contradiction — until you realise it's deliberate: the major V supplies the leading tone that pulls back to the tonic. Without that, the V → i cadence would sound weak. Once you internalise this purpose, the rule is obvious. Writing triads — Grade 3 expectations Root position only. Bass note = root. Stack a 3rd and 5th above. No inversions yet (Grade 4-5 territory). In 4-part writing (Grade 5+) you also need to know how to double notes, but Grade 3 only asks for the three-note triad spelled out.
