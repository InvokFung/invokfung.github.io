3D Printing Course — 06 · CAD for Printing
Created2026-05-25|Engineering 3D Printing
Post 05 established that a printed part's dimensions differ from its nominal dimensions in ways you can measure and partly compensate. This post is about the tool where those decisions get encoded. Choosing a CAD paradigm, understanding what happens when a solid becomes a triangle mesh, picking an interchange format that carries units, and writing a model whose clearances are named parameters rather than typed numbers — these are the things that determine whether a design can be revised in five minutes or has to be rebuilt from scratch. The theme throughout: a model is a program that produces geometry, and it should be written with the same discipline as any other program. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing (this post) → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - Choose between parametric history-based, direct, code-CAD and mesh/sculpting paradigms based on what the part is for.- Explain the difference between a BREP solid and a triangle mesh, and state exactly what is lost when one becomes the other.- Build a fully-constrained parametric part driven by a parameter table, so a single value change propagates correctly.- Pick a file format deliberately: STEP for interchange, 3MF for printing, STL only when nothing else is accepted, and know why STL causes 25.4× scaling errors.- Set mesh export tolerances so faceting does not become the chord error of Post 05.- Diagnose and repair non-manifold edges, flipped normals and self-intersections.- Encode Post 05's measured clearances as named variables, and apply printing-aware modelling rules for chamfers and wall thickness.- Select correctly between printed threads, heat-set inserts, captive nuts and tapping.- Version-control CAD work, and know why code-CAD is structurally better at it. Chapter 1 — The paradigms, and what each is for Terminology - Parametric, history-based modelling — the model is a recorded sequence of operations (a feature tree) driven by named dimensions. Editing a dimension replays the tree. Fusion 360, Onshape, SolidWorks, FreeCAD, Solid Edge.- Direct (explicit) modelling — you push and pull faces of the geometry directly; there is no history to replay. Fast for edits to imported geometry that has no tree. Plasticity, SpaceClaim, Fusion's direct-edit mode, most of FreeCAD's Part workbench.- Code-CAD (script-CAD) — the model is source code that evaluates to geometry. OpenSCAD, CadQuery, build123d, JSCAD, ImplicitCAD, Zoo/KittyCAD's KCL.- Mesh / sculpting — you manipulate a triangle mesh directly, or a voxel/dynamic-topology proxy of one. Blender, ZBrush, Nomad Sculpt, Meshmixer.- Design intent — the reasons behind the geometry, encoded so that later edits respect them. "This hole is centred on that boss" is design intent; "this hole is at X = 17.5" is a coincidence that happens to be true today.- Feature tree — the ordered list of operations that builds a parametric model.- Constraint — a geometric or dimensional relationship in a sketch (coincident, tangent, horizontal, equal, or a driving dimension) that removes a degree of freedom. Comparison | Paradigm | Strength | Weakness | Right for ||---|---|---|---|| Parametric history-based | design intent survives editing; dimensions are first-class; assemblies and drawings | tree can break on edit ("topological naming"); GUI-heavy; files are opaque binaries | functional mechanical parts — the default for this course || Direct | fast, no tree to break, excellent on imported dumb solids | edits are not repeatable or parameterised; no design intent recorded | modifying a downloaded STEP; quick one-off geometry || Code-CAD | exact parameterisation, diffable text, real version control, loops and libraries, scriptable batch generation | no interactive dragging; steeper entry; fillets on complex geometry can be awkward | parts with families of sizes; anything you will regenerate; anything you want in git || Mesh / sculpting | organic, artistic, unlimited form | no dimensions, no constraints, no solid guarantee, hard to make anything fit | figurines, characters, artistic objects, decorative surfaces | Why mesh tools are the wrong default for functional parts Blender is superb software and the wrong tool for a bracket. The reasons are structural, not stylistic: 1. No dimensions. A mesh stores vertex coordinates. It does not store "this face is 20 mm from that face because a bearing is 20 mm wide". Change one thing and you re-derive everything by hand.2. No constraints. Nothing enforces that two faces stay parallel or that a hole stays centred. Move a vertex and the design intent is silently gone.3. No solid guarantee. A mesh can be non-manifold, self-intersecting or open, and Blender will happily let you build and export one. A BREP kernel refuses to produce an invalid solid.4. Curves are already discretised. A "cylinder" in Blender is already a fixed polygon count. In BREP it is an exact analytic surface until you export it, which is precisely the distinction that controls hole accuracy (Chapter 4).5. Fillets and chamfers are approximations. Blender's bevel modifier is a mesh operation; a CAD fillet is an exact tangent surface. Where mesh tools are right: sculpting an organic shape, retopologising a scan, or artistic detailing. And there is a legitimate hybrid — sculpt the organic form, export it, and use it as a body inside a parametric model that handles the functional interfaces. Browser-based CAD Onshape runs entirely in the browser with a genuine cloud-native document model and built-in versioning and branching; its free tier makes documents public, which is fine for hobby work and disqualifying for anything confidential. Fusion 360 has a browser client with a reduced feature set. TinkerCAD is excellent for teaching and simple assemblies, but is constructive-solid-geometry-on-a-mesh underneath and does not scale to functional design work. Chapter 2 — BREP solids and triangle meshes This is the single most important technical idea in the post, because every accuracy problem in Post 05's Chapter 4 traces back to it. Two ways to describe a shape BREP (boundary representation) describes a solid by its boundary, as a topological structure of faces, edges and vertices, where each face carries an exact analytic surface — a plane, a cylinder, a cone, a sphere, a torus, or a NURBS patch — and each edge an exact curve. A cylindrical hole is stored as "cylindrical surface, axis here, radius 3.0". That radius is exact at every scale. Zoom in a million times and it is still a perfect circle. Triangle mesh describes a surface as a list of vertices and a list of triangles. There are no analytic surfaces. A cylindrical hole is stored as some number of flat facets approximating a circle. The approximation is fixed at the moment of export. | | BREP | Mesh ||---|---|---|| Curved surfaces | exact analytic / NURBS | flat facets approximating the surface || Precision | resolution-independent | fixed at export || Topology | faces, edges, vertices with adjacency | triangles that may or may not connect properly || Validity | kernel enforces a watertight solid | nothing enforces anything || Booleans | exact | approximate, fragile, and can fail || Fillet a 3 mm edge | one operation, exact | rebuild the surrounding topology by hand || File size for a smooth shape | small | large — smoothness costs triangles || Units | carried in the format (STEP) | not carried by STL at all || What the slicer wants | not this | this | The conversion, and what it destroys The slicer needs a mesh. It slices by intersecting planes with triangles, which is a fast, robust, well-understood operation. So somewhere between CAD and printer, tessellation happens: the BREP's analytic surfaces are approximated by triangles. What is lost: 1. Exactness on curves. A cylinder becomes a prism. This is the polygonal chord error of Post 05: because tessellation places vertices on the true surface, an internal cylinder becomes an inscribed polygon and prints undersize, while an external cylinder becomes inscribed too and prints undersize on its outer dimension. The error is controllable but never zero.2. Feature identity. "This is a hole for an M3 screw" becomes anonymous triangles. Nothing downstream knows it was a hole.3. The feature tree. All design intent, all parameters, all constraints. Gone.4. Editability. You can push a mesh around; you cannot change the diameter of a hole that no longer exists as a concept.5. Exact adjacency, if the tessellator is sloppy — producing the mesh-integrity problems of Chapter 4. The practical rule that follows: keep the BREP as the master. Export a mesh as a build artefact, the way a compiler emits a binary. Never edit the mesh and treat it as the source. If you find yourself repairing an STL, the real fix is upstream. > Watch out: this is why "I downloaded an STL and want to change the hole size" is a hard problem and "I downloaded a STEP and want to change the hole size" is a two-minute job. When choosing what to publish or archive, publish both — the STEP so people can modify it, the mesh so people can print it immediately. Chapter 3 — A parametric workflow that survives editing Sketch discipline A sketch is the 2D profile that features are built from, and it is where most parametric models go wrong. - Fully constrain every sketch. A fully-constrained sketch has zero degrees of freedom; every entity is locked by constraints and dimensions. Most CAD packages colour it differently when it is. An under-constrained sketch will silently change shape when you edit something else — the single largest source of "my model exploded when I changed one number".- Prefer geometric constraints over dimensions. Coincident, collinear, tangent, symmetric, equal. These express intent; a dimension expresses a value. A hole constrained symmetric about the origin stays centred when the part gets wider. A hole dimensioned at 17.5 mm from the left edge does not.- Sketch on planes and on datums, not on model faces, where you can. Sketching on a face creates a dependency on that face's identity; if a later edit removes or splits the face, the sketch loses its reference. This is topological naming, and it is why parametric trees break.- Keep sketches small. One sketch per feature is easier to debug than one heroic sketch containing everything.- Sketch the design intent, not the picture. If two features must always be the same size, constrain them equal rather than dimensioning both to the same number. It is not "3.4".- A wall thickness is a consequence of the extrusion width times an integer. It is not "1.2".- A pocket depth is a consequence of the component that goes in it plus a clearance. It is not "6".- A part length is a consequence of the things it must span. It is not "80". Every number that is a consequence should be an expression referencing a parameter, not a literal. That is the whole discipline. Chapter 4 — Formats, export settings, and mesh integrity The formats | Format | Carries | Units | Use it for ||---|---|---|---|| STEP (ISO 10303, AP242) | exact BREP solids, assemblies, colours, and PMI in AP242 | yes | interchange between CAD packages; archival master; anything you want someone else to be able to modify || 3MF | triangle mesh plus units, colour, materials, transforms, build items, metadata, and slicer settings via extensions | yes | sending a model to the printer; sharing something ready to print; the modern replacement for STL || STL | triangles. That is the entire format | no | only when the recipient accepts nothing else || OBJ | triangles plus UVs, materials via , vertex colours by convention | not reliably | mesh interchange with graphics and texturing tools || glTF / GLB | mesh + PBR materials, optimised for real-time delivery | yes (metres) | viewing a model on the web — not for printing | Why STL causes 25.4× scaling errors The STL format is a list of triangles with floating-point coordinates and per-facet normals. It has no header field for units, no metadata section, no colour, no scale. A vertex at means twenty somethings. If the author worked in inches and the recipient's slicer assumes millimetres, the part arrives 1/25.4 of its intended size — or 25.4× too large in the other direction. This is not a bug in any particular tool; it is a consequence of information the format simply does not carry. The fix is not to be careful. The fix is to use a format that carries units: - Export 3MF for printing. Its attribute makes the question unanswerable-by-guessing.- Export STEP for interchange. Units are part of the file.- Reach for STL only under duress, and when you do, put the unit in the filename: . 3MF additionally solves the problem that an STL cannot represent multi-part plates, per-object print settings, modifier meshes, or colour. Most modern slicers use 3MF as their own project format for exactly this reason — a saved project is a 3MF containing the geometry and the settings that produced a known-good print. That is a far better thing to share than a naked STL. > Common pitfall: publishing only an STL for a functional part. The recipient cannot change a clearance, cannot adapt it to their printer's measured offsets, and cannot even be sure of the units. Publish STEP and 3MF; add the source (, , ) if you can. Mesh export settings — where Post 05's chord error is decided Every tessellator is controlled by two tolerances, whatever the UI calls them: - Chord / linear / deviation tolerance — the maximum distance between the flat facet and the true surface. Smaller ⇒ more triangles ⇒ closer to the real shape. This is the setting that controls hole undersizing.- Angular tolerance — the maximum angle between adjacent facet normals. Controls how many facets a curve gets regardless of its size, which matters on large-radius surfaces where the chord tolerance alone would produce visible flats. Naming across tools: | Tool | Chord tolerance | Angular tolerance ||---|---|---|| Fusion 360 (Refinement: custom) | Surface Deviation | Normal Deviation || SolidWorks | Deviation | Angle || Onshape | Chordal tolerance | Angular tolerance || FreeCAD (mesh export) | Surface deviation | Angular deviation || CadQuery | | || OpenSCAD | (minimum fragment size) | (minimum fragment angle); overrides both | The geometry of the error. For a circle of radius r approximated by an inscribed regular polygon of n sides, the maximum deviation (sagitta) is . Two consequences follow immediately: 1. The error grows as n falls — obvious, and the reason the default facet count is usually too low.2. For a fixed facet count, the error scales with r — so a coarse hurts big circles more in absolute terms, while a fixed chord tolerance produces the same absolute error at every size. Because the polygon is inscribed, an internal cylinder — a hole — is always printed smaller than nominal. That is Cause A from Post 05, and it is entirely eliminated by exporting at a tight chord tolerance. Cause B (the extrusion overlap on concave paths) is not — but there is no reason to add a self-inflicted error on top of an unavoidable one. Practical guidance: - Set chord tolerance to roughly an order of magnitude below the accuracy you care about. If you are chasing 0.05 mm, export at 0.005 mm or tighter. Triangle count is cheap; slicers handle multi-megabyte meshes without difficulty.- Do not use OpenSCAD's globally. A fixed gives a 3 mm hole a wastefully high facet count and a 100 mm cylinder a visibly faceted one. Set and instead and let the facet count adapt per feature. Use only as a local override on a specific object.- Preview the result. Import the exported mesh back and look at a hole edge-on. If you can see flats, the tolerance was too loose. > Trick: the fastest way to see whether the chord tolerance is right is to slice the exported mesh and look at the toolpath around a small hole in the slicer's preview. If the path is visibly a polygon rather than a circle, re-export. Mesh integrity A valid mesh for printing is manifold (watertight, "2-manifold"): every edge is shared by exactly two triangles, no more and no less; the surface is closed, with a well-defined inside and outside; and all normals point outward consistently. | Defect | What it means | Symptom ||---|---|---|| Non-manifold edge | an edge shared by 1, 3 or more faces | slicer cannot decide what is inside; missing or spurious walls || Hole / open boundary | a gap in the surface | the "solid" is not closed; slicer either repairs it or produces nonsense || Flipped normals | a face pointing inward | inverted regions; the slicer may print the negative of what you wanted || Self-intersection | the surface passes through itself | ambiguous interior; strange internal walls || Duplicate / coincident faces | two triangles in the same place | zero-thickness regions; unpredictable slicing || Zero-area / degenerate triangles | three collinear vertices | numerical failures in slicing || Disjoint shells | multiple separate closed volumes in one file | legitimate sometimes, a bug other times — decide deliberately | Repair tooling, roughly in order of how much you should want to need it: 1. Fix it upstream. A BREP kernel cannot produce a non-manifold solid. If your mesh is broken, either the source model was a mesh, or the tessellator failed, or a boolean produced a degenerate result. Fixing the source is the real fix.2. The slicer's own repair. PrusaSlicer, Cura, OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio all detect and auto-repair simple errors, and report what they found. Read the report rather than dismissing it.3. Blender — the 3D Print Toolbox add-on checks manifoldness, thin faces, sharp edges, overhangs and intersections, and offers targeted fixes. Free and effective.4. Meshmixer (legacy but still widely used), Netfabb, MeshLab — dedicated inspection and repair.5. — command-line, scriptable, good for CI checks on a repository of models. > Watch out: a slicer's "auto-repair succeeded" is not the same as "the model is correct". Repair heuristics guess at what you meant. If the model has a genuine 0.02 mm gap where two bodies were supposed to join, the repair will close it silently and you will never learn that your boolean failed. Chapter 5 — Modelling for what Post 05 measured The measurements from Post 05 are only useful if they are encoded somewhere durable. Three rules do most of the work. Rule 1 — Clearances are named variables, never literals Never type into a hole diameter for an M3 screw. Type , where both are parameters. The reasons compound: - Re-run the fit gauge after a nozzle change and update one number.- Print the same part in PETG instead of PLA and update one number.- Someone else on a different printer changes one number and it works for them.- Six months later you can see why the hole is 3.4, which is information the literal destroys. Keep a shared parameter block — a spreadsheet in the CAD document, an included file, or a Python module — that holds your machine's measured constants. Every model imports it. Rule 2 — Chamfer bottom edges, fillet everything else | Edge | Feature | Why ||---|---|---|| Bottom perimeter (touching the plate) | chamfer, 0.4–0.6 mm at 45° | self-supporting; cures elephant foot (Post 05); no support needed || Vertical edges | fillet, freely | prints perfectly; reduces stress concentration (Post 07) || Top edges | fillet or chamfer | both print acceptably; a fillet on a top edge is a shallow dome and is fine || Internal corners under load | fillet, generously | the cheapest strength upgrade available (Post 07) || Bottom edge as a fillet | avoid | the tangent point approaches vertical then goes past 45° — it droops and needs support | Rule 3 — Wall thickness is a multiple of extrusion width A wall the slicer cannot fill with an integer number of perimeters is a wall with a gap or a wall the slicer silently thickens. Both are bad, and both are avoidable. | Target | Expression | With a 0.4 mm nozzle ||---|---|---|| Thin cosmetic wall | | 0.8 mm || Standard wall | | 1.2 mm || Structural wall | to | 1.6–2.4 mm || Solid-through boss or rib | + | 2.4 mm+ | The same logic applies in Z: make Z dimensions multiples of layer height. A 6.05 mm boss at 0.2 mm layers becomes 6.0 or 6.2, whichever way the slicer rounds. A 6.0 mm boss is exactly 30 layers, every time. Additional printing-aware modelling habits worth building in: - Design overhangs to 45° or shallower where you can (Post 03), so support becomes optional rather than mandatory.- Model the fastener hole for the orientation you will print it in. A vertical-axis hole and a horizontal-axis hole are different features with different errors (Post 05).- Add a relief at the bottom of a blind pocket — a small chamfer or a slightly deeper pocket — so elephant foot and stray first-layer material do not prevent seating.- Never model a zero-clearance mating pair and hope. If two parts must touch exactly, one of them needs a clearance and the other needs a stop. Chapter 6 — Threads, fasteners, assemblies Five ways to get a thread, and when each is right | Method | Strength | Reusability | Cost | Use when ||---|---|---|---|---|| Printed thread | low — the thread crest is layer-bonded plastic and strips easily | poor; a few assembly cycles | free | large coarse threads (lids, caps, adjusters), light load, no repeated assembly || Heat-set insert | high — brass threads in a melted-in pocket | excellent | inserts + a soldering iron with the right tip | the default for a functional part with a machine screw; anything assembled and disassembled || Captive nut (hex pocket or slot) | high — a real steel nut carries the load | excellent | a nut, and a pocket in the model | when you can design a pocket, especially in a wall printed vertically || Tapping into printed plastic | moderate — real thread form cut into a solid boss | fair; better than a printed thread, worse than an insert | a tap | a boss thick enough to take it, no insert on hand || Self-tapping / thread-forming screw | moderate — screw cuts its own thread in a pilot hole | poor; degrades each cycle | a screw | quick assembly, one-time | Design notes for each: - Printed threads. Use a proper thread library rather than modelling a helix by hand — OpenSCAD's BOSL2 (, ), Fusion's built-in Thread feature with Modeled checked. Add clearance: the nominal thread pair will not assemble. Print with the axis vertical so the thread flanks are not overhangs. Prefer coarse pitches; anything finer than about M8 coarse is unlikely to be worth printing.- Heat-set inserts. Model the pocket per the insert manufacturer's datasheet — the required hole diameter and depth are specified, and guessing produces either a loose insert or a bulged boss. Give the boss enough surrounding wall (a wall thickness of at least the insert's radius is a reasonable rule) and add a small lead-in chamfer at the top so the insert self-centres. Install with a temperature-controlled iron and a proper insert tip, pressing slowly and straight — the plastic must melt and flow around the knurling, not be pushed aside. Check squareness before the plastic freezes.- Captive nuts. Model the hexagonal pocket with your or slightly more. If the pocket opens upward, the print will bridge over it — which works, but leaves a rough ceiling; if it opens sideways, the nut slides in cleanly. A tiny retaining bump or a slight interference on one flat stops the nut falling out during assembly. > Trick: design the heat-set-insert boss and the captive-nut pocket as reusable modules or components in your library, parameterised by screw size, so you build them once and never re-derive the dimensions. Assemblies, mating features and alignment For a printed assembly, alignment features are far more effective than tight tolerances. - Pin-and-socket. A pin on one part, a socket on the other, with your measured . Three pins overconstrain; two pins and one slot is the correct kinematic answer — one pin locates, the slot allows for the pitch error between them.- Tongue-and-groove / lip-and-recess. Aligns two shells, adds stiffness, and hides the parting line.- Dovetails and keyed profiles. Prevent assembly in the wrong orientation. Asymmetry is a feature: if a part can be assembled backwards, someone will.- Counterbores and countersinks. Model them explicitly; do not expect a screw head to sit flush against a printed surface.- Boss-and-recess with generous lead-ins. Every mating feature should have a chamfer at its entry so it self-centres rather than jamming on the first layer's elephant foot.- One adjustable dimension per chain. Post 05's stack-up rule, expressed in geometry: a slot instead of a hole somewhere in every chain of mating parts. Model the assembly in CAD before printing it. Parametric packages give you real joints and interference detection; even in code-CAD you can render both parts in place and inspect the interface visually. A five-minute check catches errors that cost a six-hour print. Scan-to-CAD and reverse engineering Reverse engineering an existing object — to make a bracket that fits a specific part, or to replace something discontinued — has two routes. Measure and remodel. Calipers, a straight edge, a radius gauge, a printed profile gauge, and a fresh parametric model. Slow, and it produces a clean, editable, parameterised solid. This is usually the right answer for anything mechanical. 3D scan. Photogrammetry, structured light, or laser. Produces a mesh, which you then use as a reference body to model against — not as the part itself. The mesh is noisy, and it has no flat planes, no true cylinders and no design intent. > Watch out: treat scanner accuracy claims sceptically, and apply Post 05's vocabulary. A scanner advertising "0.05 mm accuracy" is usually quoting a point accuracy under ideal conditions on an ideal target — often a single-shot figure that says nothing about the accumulated error across a full multi-scan alignment of a real object. Volumetric accuracy over a whole part is the number that matters and is rarely the number advertised. Dark, shiny, transparent and thin features scan badly or not at all. If a scan must produce a dimension you will rely on, verify that dimension with calipers. The workflow that works: scan for form, measure for dimensions. Use the scan to capture the organic shape you cannot measure, and override the critical dimensions with values you took by hand. 3D Printing Course — 05 · Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances
Created2026-05-22|Engineering 3D Printing
This is the post the rest of the series is built around. A CAD model contains a number — 20.000 mm — that is exact in the sense that a mathematical object is exact. The part that comes off the plate is not that number. It is a physical object with a size that depends on the filament batch, the flow calibration, the extrusion width, the first-layer squish, the cooling history, the motion system, the geometry itself, and the temperature of the room when you measured it. The gap between those two things is not a defect to be eliminated; it is the normal condition of manufacturing, and the entire discipline of tolerancing exists because of it. The goal of this post is not to hand you numbers. It is to give you the vocabulary to describe the problem precisely, an honest accounting of where the error comes from, and a reproducible procedure that produces numbers for your machine, your material and your profile — because those are the only numbers that are true. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances (this post) → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - State the difference between accuracy, precision (repeatability and reproducibility) and resolution, and explain why an advertised "0.05 mm resolution" says essentially nothing about accuracy.- Measure a printed part defensibly: right instrument, controlled force, controlled temperature, repeated readings, recorded uncertainty.- Build an error budget for a printed dimension and name which contributions are systematic and which are random.- Recognise and correct the standard systematic errors: elephant foot, hole undersizing, external-large/internal-small, and X/Y versus Z asymmetry.- Run a test-artefact procedure that produces a machine-and-material-specific offset table you fill in yourself.- Design fits using clearance/transition/interference as a concept, with a printed sweep gauge determining the actual value.- Choose between compensating in CAD and compensating in the slicer, and know which is correct when.- Design so that tolerance stack-up is absorbed by an adjustable dimension rather than demanded of every dimension. Chapter 1 — Saying what you mean Most confusion about printer accuracy is vocabulary confusion. These terms are not synonyms and the rest of the post keeps them strictly distinct. Terminology - Nominal size — the number in the CAD model. A design intent, not a prediction.- Actual size — what a specific physical feature measures, on a specific instrument, at a specific time and temperature.- Deviation (error) — actual minus nominal, signed. Sign matters: a hole 0.15 mm undersize and a hole 0.15 mm oversize are opposite problems with opposite fixes.- Accuracy (formally, trueness) — how close the mean of many measurements is to the true value. Accuracy is about the centre of the distribution.- Precision — how tightly repeated results cluster, independent of whether they cluster in the right place. Precision is about the spread.- Repeatability — spread when the same operator measures the same feature with the same instrument under the same conditions, or when the same machine prints the same part repeatedly without changes.- Reproducibility — spread when something legitimately changes: a different operator, a different instrument, a different day, a new spool of the same filament, the same model on a different printer.- Resolution — the smallest increment an instrument can display, or the smallest increment a machine can command. It is a property of the readout or the motion system, and it bounds nothing else.- Uncertainty — the interval within which the true value plausibly lies, given the instrument, the method and the operator. Always larger than the resolution.- Tolerance — the permitted deviation, decided by the designer from the function of the part. A tolerance is a requirement, not a measurement.- Systematic error (bias) — a repeatable, signed offset. Can be measured and compensated.- Random error — scatter with no fixed sign. Can be characterised statistically and reduced by averaging, but not compensated away. The four-quadrant picture, stated plainly | | Precise | Not precise ||---|---|---|| Accurate | every part right, every time — the goal | on average right, individually unpredictable || Not accurate | every part wrong by the same amount — compensable | every part wrong by a different amount — the hard case | The bottom-left quadrant is the most important thing in this post. A machine that is precise but not accurate is a good machine: the error is systematic, you measure it once, and you compensate for it. A machine that is imprecise cannot be fixed by compensation, because there is no single number to compensate by. So the first diagnostic question is never "is it accurate?" — it is "is it repeatable?" Why "0.05 mm resolution" is a meaningless accuracy claim Marketing copy quotes numbers like "0.0125 mm XY resolution". That figure is a motion increment: the linear distance corresponding to one microstep of a stepper motor, derived from the motor's step angle, the microstepping setting, and the belt pitch or lead screw pitch. It is arithmetic on the kinematics. It says nothing about: - whether the belt is tensioned identically in both directions (backlash),- whether the frame is square,- whether the extruder delivers the commanded volume of plastic (flow),- whether the extrusion width matches what the slicer assumed,- whether the filament is the diameter the slicer was told,- how much the part shrinks as it cools,- whether the motor actually reaches every commanded microstep under load, which at high microstepping ratios it frequently does not. A stepper motor's ability to command a 0.0125 mm move does not mean the toolhead goes there, and even if it did, the plastic that ends up on the plate is not the toolhead path. Treat advertised resolution as a specification of the controller, not of the part. > Watch out: the same confusion appears on the CAD side. A modeller displaying 20.0000 mm is showing you the precision of a floating-point representation, not a claim about anything physical. Digits in a CAD field are free; micrometres on a plate are not. Chapter 2 — The instrument is part of the experiment Before measuring error, establish that the measurement itself is not the largest source of error. On printed polymer parts, it very often is. Calipers versus micrometers | | Digital calipers | Micrometer ||---|---|---|| Typical display resolution | 0.01 mm | 0.001 mm || Realistic uncertainty in normal use | far worse than the display — commonly a few hundredths of a mm | roughly an order of magnitude better || Measuring force | uncontrolled, entirely operator-dependent | controlled by a ratchet or friction thimble || Reach | external, internal, depth, step | external only (for a standard outside micrometer) || Cost | low | moderate || Right for | almost everything in this post | verifying a critical dimension, or calibrating your calipers' bias | The decisive difference is measuring force. Calipers have no force control at all: how hard you squeeze determines the reading. On steel this barely matters. On a printed polymer part — a material with a modulus orders of magnitude below steel, and a surface made of rounded extrusion beads rather than a plane — it matters enormously. Squeezing calipers hard on a PLA cube can easily change the reading by more than the deviation you are trying to measure, and on TPU it is hopeless. A micrometer's ratchet applies a repeatable force. That is why a micrometer is more precise, not the extra digit. > Trick: to find out how much your grip is worth, measure the same feature ten times deliberately gently and ten times deliberately firmly. The difference between the two means is your personal measuring-force error. Everyone should know this number about themselves. It is usually larger than expected. The other things that move the reading - Zero. Close the jaws, check zero, re-zero, before every measurement session and again if you drop the tool. A caliper that drifted 0.03 mm off zero produces a beautifully repeatable, entirely wrong dataset.- Temperature. Polymers have a coefficient of thermal expansion roughly an order of magnitude larger than steel. A part measured warm off the plate and the same part measured cold are different sizes, and your steel calipers are not expanding with it. Let parts equilibrate to room temperature — a couple of hours for anything substantial — and record the room temperature with the data.- Where on the feature. Printed surfaces are not planes. A measurement across the middle of a face, across a corner, at the first layer, and at the top of a 40 mm pillar are four different measurements of the same nominal dimension. Decide a location convention and record it.- Seam and blob. The layer seam is a local bulge. Measuring across a seam gives a systematically larger reading. Either measure away from the seam and say so, or measure across it deliberately and say that.- Bead geometry. The outer surface is a stack of rounded beads, not a flat face. Calipers contact bead crowns; a flat contact averages differently than a knife-edge contact.- Internal features are harder. Caliper internal jaws are stubby and imprecise, and on a hole they measure a chord, not necessarily the diameter. Pin gauges (or, adequately, drill bit shanks of known size, or ground dowel pins) measure a hole far better: the largest pin that enters is a real, functional, repeatable answer. The measurement protocol The whole of the rest of this post depends on measurements taken like this: 1. Let the part reach room temperature. Record the temperature.2. Zero the instrument. Verify against a known reference if you have one (a gauge block, a ground dowel pin, a new drill bit shank).3. Deburr nothing and clean nothing that changes the dimension; do remove obvious stringing and the brim.4. Take at least five readings of each feature, re-seating the instrument each time. Rotate the part between readings where the feature allows.5. Record every reading, not just the one you like.6. Report the mean and the spread (range or standard deviation). The mean estimates the bias; the spread estimates your precision.7. If the spread is comparable to the deviation you are chasing, stop — improve the measurement before drawing any conclusion about the printer. > Common pitfall: measuring once, getting 19.86 on a 20 mm nominal, and concluding "my printer prints 0.14 mm small". One reading contains no information about its own reliability. It could be bias, it could be grip, it could be a seam, it could be the caliper's zero. Five readings tell you which. Chapter 3 — The error budget Here is the honest thesis of this post: the deviation of a printed dimension is a sum of contributions, most of which you can name, several of which you can measure, and a few of which you cannot control at all. Chapter 4 — The systematic errors, and their actual causes These four account for the large majority of real-world fit failures. Elephant foot Symptom: the bottom one to three layers bulge outward. A part that measures correctly at mid-height is oversize at the base, and it will not sit flat in a recess or slide into a slot. Cause: two things at once. The first layer is deliberately squashed into the plate for adhesion, which displaces material sideways. And the first layers cool against a heated bed that keeps them soft, under the weight and pressure of everything printed above. Fixes, in the order to try them: 1. Get the first-layer Z offset right (Post 01). Over-squish is the root cause; everything else is compensation.2. Chamfer the bottom edge in CAD — a 0.4–0.6 mm chamfer on the bottom perimeter. This is the most robust fix because it survives moving the model to a different printer, a different slicer or a different profile. It costs one CAD feature.3. Slicer elephant-foot compensation — shrinks the first layer's perimeter by a set amount. Effective, but it is a profile setting that does not travel with the model.4. Reduce bed temperature slightly if the material allows. Holes come out undersize Symptom: a 5 mm hole measures under 5 mm, and a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse. This is the single most reported dimensional complaint in FDM, and it has two independent causes that add. Cause A — polygonal chord error. The slicer works on a triangle mesh. A cylindrical hole in CAD becomes an N-sided polygon on export, and the toolpath follows that polygon. Because the mesh vertices sit on the true circle and the polygon edges cut inside it, the printed hole is inscribed in the nominal circle and is therefore smaller. The error grows as the facet count falls and as the hole gets smaller relative to the facet size. This error is created in CAD export, not in the printer — see Post 06 on chord/deviation tolerance. Cause B — extrusion overlap on a concave path. When the nozzle traverses a concave (inward-curving) path, the deposited bead's outer material is compressed toward the inside of the curve. The bead does not simply lie along the commanded centreline; it bulges into the hole. Small holes have tighter curvature, so the effect is larger — which is why a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse than a 12 mm one. This is a real physical effect of extruding a finite-width bead along a curve, and it cannot be fixed in CAD export. Fixes: 1. Export with a tight chord tolerance so the facets are fine (Post 06). This removes Cause A entirely and costs nothing but file size.2. Print, measure, and compensate. Because Cause B depends on hole size, the compensation is a curve, not a constant — hence the sweep in Chapter 5.3. For holes that must fit a fastener or a shaft, drill or ream to size after printing. A printed hole reamed with a standard drill or reamer is dramatically more accurate than any compensation scheme, and this is a legitimate professional answer, not a cop-out.4. Slicer hole compensation where available (Cura's Hole Horizontal Expansion, OrcaSlicer's X-Y hole compensation). Note that PrusaSlicer's XY size compensation is not hole-specific: it grows or shrinks all perimeters, so a positive value that enlarges the outside simultaneously shrinks holes. > Watch out: never design a hole at exactly the nominal shaft size and expect it to fit. A 3 mm hole for a 3 mm shaft will not accept the shaft on any FDM printer without help. Clearance is designed in, not hoped for — Chapter 6. External dimensions large, internal features small Symptom: the outside of the box is 20.15 on a 20 nominal, and the pocket inside it is 19.85 on a 20 nominal. Both errors are the same sign of material excess. Cause: any excess deposited material grows the part outward from the toolpath in both cases. On an external wall that means larger; on an internal wall it means the cavity is smaller. Over-extrusion, an extrusion width larger than the slicer assumed, or a too-hot first layer all produce this signature. This is the diagnostic signature of over-extrusion, and it is why both an external and an internal feature must be in the test artefact. Measuring only external features and compensating them makes internal features worse. X/Y and Z behave differently Z is not the same problem as X and Y and should never be lumped with them. | | X / Y | Z ||---|---|---|| Feature size determined by | extrusion width and toolpath geometry | layer height and the number of layers || Quantisation | effectively continuous | discrete — heights land on multiples of layer height || Typical accuracy | worse — the error budget above | often better, because Z steps are mechanically direct (lead screws) and there is no bead-width term || Error mechanism | flow, width, curvature, compensation | first-layer offset (a fixed error on every Z dimension), plus quantisation || Compensation | XY size compensation / hole compensation | design to layer-height multiples; correct the first-layer offset | The important asymmetry: your first-layer Z offset applies a constant error to every Z dimension in the part, in the same direction, regardless of height. Squish the first layer 0.05 mm extra and every Z dimension is 0.05 mm short. That is a systematic, easily measured, easily corrected error — and it is a completely different animal from the size-proportional errors in X and Y. > Trick: measure a tall feature's height and a short feature's height on the same print. If both are short by the same amount, it is first-layer offset. If the tall one is short by proportionally more, it is steps/mm or thermal contraction. The pattern of the error tells you the cause. Chapter 5 — The calibration-cube fallacy, and what to do instead Why the 20 mm cube proves almost nothing The 20 mm calibration cube is the most-printed object in the hobby and one of the least informative. Consider what it can and cannot tell you. What it does test: whether a 20 mm external dimension in X and in Y comes out near 20 mm, once. What it cannot tell you: - Anything about internal features. It has no holes. It therefore cannot detect the over-extrusion signature above, which is the most common real cause of fit failure.- Anything about size dependence. One nominal size cannot separate the offset term from the scale term of the error model. A cube that measures 20.10 could be a +0.10 mm offset or a +0.5 % scale error; those need completely different fixes and the cube cannot distinguish them.- Anything about repeatability. One cube is n = 1. It contains no information about spread.- Anything about fits. The actual engineering question is almost never "is a 20 mm face 20 mm?" It is "does this 3 mm pin go into this 3 mm hole with the right amount of friction?" The cube is silent on that.- Anything about your other materials or profiles. The result is specific to the filament, temperature, speed and layer height used. A printer can produce a perfect 20 mm cube and still fail to produce a hole a shaft will enter. Chapter 6 — Designing fits The concept, borrowed correctly Mechanical engineering has a mature vocabulary for how two mating features relate. ISO 286 ("Geometrical product specifications (GPS) — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes") defines it: a hole and a shaft each get a tolerance zone, described by a letter (position of the zone relative to nominal) and a grade number (width of the zone, IT01 through IT18). The classic designations — H7/g6, H7/p6 — come from that system, and fits fall into three classes: - Clearance fit — the hole is always larger than the shaft. There is always a gap. Parts assemble by hand and can move or rotate.- Transition fit — the tolerance zones overlap. Depending where each part lands within its zone, the assembly may be slightly loose or slightly tight. Used for accurate location with hand assembly.- Interference (press) fit — the shaft is always larger than the hole. Assembly requires force, heating or cooling, and the joint transmits torque by friction. Now the honest part: ISO 286 fit classes are not directly applicable to FDM printing. Borrow the three concepts; do not copy the values. The reasons are specific: 1. The IT grades assume machining processes with characterised, stable capability. FDM's capability is not that, and it is different for every machine, material and profile.2. The system assumes tolerance zones roughly symmetric about a predictable process centre. FDM's bias is strongly feature-dependent — external and internal features err in opposite directions, and small holes err differently from large ones.3. FDM surfaces are made of rounded beads with layer-scale texture. "Size" on such a surface is less well-defined than on a machined one, which undermines the measurement the system is built on.4. The material is compliant and viscoelastic. A polymer press fit relaxes over time (creep — Post 07); a steel one does not. So: use "I want a clearance fit here and an interference fit there" as design language. Then determine the actual clearance experimentally. The fit-test gauge The right tool is a printed gauge that sweeps clearance in known steps against a known reference. Use a real reference object rather than another printed part, so you are measuring one unknown instead of two: a ground dowel pin, a drill bit shank, an M3 screw, a 608 bearing, a specific connector. Write them into your CAD parameter file as named variables (Post 06) and stop guessing. Three things that change the answer and must be held constant or re-tested: - Material. PETG and PLA do not give the same clearances. Neither do two colours (Post 04).- Engagement depth. A 2 mm-deep hole and a 20 mm-deep hole with identical diameters behave completely differently. Test at a realistic depth.- Orientation. A hole printed with its axis vertical (a circle in the layer plane) and one printed horizontally (an arch, with bridging at the top) are different features with different errors. Test the orientation you will use. > Trick: design a deliberately adjustable interface where you can — a slot instead of a hole, a screw-clamped collar instead of a press fit, a shim pocket. One adjustable feature absorbs the whole tolerance stack of the assembly and turns a metrology problem into a five-second assembly step. Compensating in CAD versus in the slicer Both are legitimate. They belong in different places, and mixing them without noticing is how people end up double-compensating. | Compensate in CAD | Compensate in the slicer ||---|---|| The correction is a design decision: a functional clearance, a bottom chamfer, a wall thickness set to a multiple of extrusion width | The correction is a machine/process characteristic: your printer's systematic offset for this material and profile || Travels with the model to any printer, slicer or person | Lives in the profile; correctly re-applied to every model you print with that profile || Correct for: fit clearances, chamfers, thread reliefs, features sized to nozzle geometry | Correct for: global XY size compensation, hole compensation, elephant-foot compensation || Wrong for: your machine's private bias — you would bake a local defect into a shared file | Wrong for: functional clearances — the slicer setting applies to every feature, including the ones that were already fine | The rule: design intent goes in CAD, machine bias goes in the slicer. A clearance of 0.2 mm because a shaft must rotate is design intent — it belongs in the model and should be a named parameter. A −0.06 mm XY compensation because your extruder runs slightly hot is machine bias — it belongs in the profile, and it should not follow the model to someone else's printer. > Watch out: if you compensate in the slicer and then adjust the model because parts still do not fit, you now have two corrections that must be maintained together, and neither is documented. Pick one place per cause, and write down what you did. Chapter 7 — Stack-up, standards, and the argument Tolerance stack-up Errors add along an assembly path. Three parts stacked, each within ±0.15 mm, can be ±0.45 mm out at the end in the worst case. Design a mechanism as a chain of five tightly-toleranced printed features and you have designed something that will not assemble. Two ways to add up the contributions: - Worst case (arithmetic sum). Add all the tolerances. Guarantees assembly, and is conservative — it assumes every part is simultaneously at its extreme, which is unlikely.- Statistical (root-sum-square). If the individual errors are independent and roughly centred, the combined spread grows like the square root of the sum of squares — much smaller than the arithmetic sum. Valid only when the errors really are independent and centred, and FDM errors often are neither: a whole batch printed from one spool on one day shares the same bias, which is precisely the correlated case RSS is invalid for. For hobby and small-batch printing, the honest position is: use worst-case reasoning, and design so that the worst case is absorbed rather than resisted. Techniques that absorb stack-up, roughly in order of usefulness: 1. Make one dimension in the chain adjustable. A slot, an oversized clearance hole with a washer, a screw-adjusted stop, a shim. One adjustable feature is worth ten tight tolerances.2. Shorten the chain. Print two mating features as one part rather than three parts that must align. This is FDM's genuine superiority over machining — arbitrary geometry is free, so consolidate.3. Locate on a datum, not on an accumulation. Reference every feature to one face rather than each to the previous one, so errors do not accumulate down the chain.4. Use compliance deliberately. A printed snap-fit, a flexure, a slightly springy tab tolerates variation that a rigid interface will not.5. Post-process the one critical feature. Ream the bearing hole. Tap the thread. Face the mating surface flat with sandpaper on a granite tile. One machined feature on an otherwise printed part is cheap and effective. The closing argument Everything above converges on one position, and it is the same position the planned furniture-CAD series will argue about woodworking: CAD precision does not survive contact with the physical world. The number in the model is exact. The part is not, and the material is not, and the machine is not, and the measurement is not. Every physical process has a distribution of outcomes, and the designer's job is not to eliminate that distribution — that is impossible — but to design something that works across it. Three consequences follow, and they are the practical content of the whole post: 1. A design that only works if a tolerance is met is a fragile design. If the assembly requires a 0.1 mm clearance and the process delivers ±0.15 mm, it will fail, and no amount of care changes that. Redesign the interface — make it adjustable, make it compliant, or consolidate the parts — rather than demanding a capability the process does not have.2. Measure your own process, then design inside what it can do. The offset and scale you fit in Chapter 5, and the clearances you found in Chapter 6, are your process capability. They are the design constraint. Someone else's numbers are not data about your machine.3. Where precision is genuinely required, get it from a process that has it. Ream the hole. Tap the thread. Press in a heat-set insert. Use a bought bearing, a bought shaft, a bought fastener. FDM's strength is arbitrary geometry produced quickly and cheaply, not precision — and combining it with a precise process at the two or three points that need precision is a better engineering answer than fighting the printer for a capability it does not have. The most valuable skill in this entire course is not making an accurate print. It is knowing which dimensions need to be accurate — usually far fewer than beginners assume — and designing so the rest do not have to be. Chapter 1 — The polymer physics that predicts printing behaviour Almost everything a filament does on the machine follows from two thermal transitions and one question about molecular order. Terminology - Glass transition temperature (Tg) — the temperature range over which an amorphous polymer (or the amorphous fraction of a semi-crystalline one) changes from a rigid, glassy solid into a soft, rubbery one. It is not a phase change with latent heat; it is a change in molecular mobility. Tg is a range, and the number a datasheet reports depends on the measurement method (DSC, DMA) and the heating rate.- Melting temperature (Tm) — the temperature at which crystalline regions melt. Only semi-crystalline polymers have a true Tm. Purely amorphous polymers soften progressively and have no sharp melting point.- Amorphous polymer — chains arranged with no long-range order, frozen in a tangle. Examples used in printing: ABS, ASA, PC, PETG (largely amorphous by design), most photopolymer resins after cure.- Semi-crystalline polymer — contains ordered crystalline domains embedded in amorphous regions. Examples: PLA (slow-crystallising, usually printed nearly amorphous), the nylons (PA6, PA12), PP, PEEK.- Heat deflection temperature (HDT) — the temperature at which a standard test bar deflects a specified amount under a specified load. A load-dependent engineering figure, not a material constant; always quoted with its stress level (commonly 0.45 MPa or 1.8 MPa).- Crystallinity — the mass fraction of the polymer that is in ordered crystalline domains. It changes with cooling rate and with annealing, and it drives stiffness, chemical resistance, opacity and shrinkage. Why Tg, not Tm, sets the service temperature A printed part carries load through solid polymer. Above Tg the amorphous fraction becomes rubbery: modulus drops by orders of magnitude, and a part that was rigid at room temperature will sag, creep and lose dimensional stability long before anything melts. This is why a PLA phone mount fails in a car parked in the sun even though PLA does not melt until far higher: the interior of a closed car in direct sun readily reaches the low end of PLA's Tg range. Three consequences worth internalising: 1. Compare service temperature against Tg, with margin. Treat Tg as the ceiling, and derate further if the part is loaded — polymers creep faster as they approach Tg (Post 07).2. Tm is a printing parameter, not a service parameter. It tells you roughly where the extruder must operate. It tells you nothing useful about whether a bracket survives on an engine bay.3. HDT is closer to what you want but still not it. HDT is measured at a specific stress on a specific bar geometry, in a solid moulded specimen — not a 3-perimeter, 15 %-infill print with anisotropic layer bonding. > Watch out: the Tg values circulating in the hobby community for a given material vary by 10–20 °C between sources, because different grades, different additives and different measurement methods genuinely give different numbers. Use them as a family ranking (PLA < PETG < ABS/ASA < PC), and use the specific spool's technical datasheet for anything that matters. Chapter 3 — Warping, shrinkage and the enclosure question Warping is not a mystery and it is not a bed-adhesion problem in origin — bed adhesion is only where the symptom appears. The physics Every polymer contracts as it cools. In a printed part, that contraction happens unevenly in time: the layer being deposited is at nozzle temperature, the layer below is somewhere between, and the first layer has been sitting on a heated plate for an hour. Each new layer solidifies and then tries to contract while bonded to a layer beneath that has already finished contracting and will not move. The result is a stress field locked into the part, tensile in the newer material, and it grows with every layer. That locked-in stress has to go somewhere. It is relieved by: - Curling — the free edges of the part lift, which is geometrically the cheapest way to relieve it. Corners lift first because they have two free directions.- Delamination — a layer interface splits, usually on a tall thin part or at a stress raiser.- Bed release — the whole part peels off the plate. Two independent quantities drive the magnitude: the total contraction from nozzle to room temperature (roughly the coefficient of thermal expansion times the temperature drop, plus any crystallisation shrinkage) and the temperature gradient through the part while printing. Why ABS needs an enclosure and PLA does not Run the comparison: - PLA is printed around 200 °C and, being effectively amorphous as printed, contracts modestly. Its Tg is around 55–65 °C, so the part becomes rigid at a temperature only a little above the bed, meaning the stress-accumulating window is short and the accumulated stress is small. An enclosure adds nothing and can hurt: it raises the temperature around the heatsink and hotend, promoting heat creep and jams.- ABS is printed around 250 °C, has a Tg around 100–110 °C, and contracts substantially. Its Tg is far above room temperature, so the part spends a long stretch of its cooling path below Tg — rigid, storing stress rather than relaxing it. An enclosure holds the chamber high enough that the part stays near or above Tg — mobile, able to relax stress — for longer, and it removes the gradients caused by room draughts. That is the mechanism: the enclosure is a stress-relief oven, not a heater for the plastic. Consequences that follow directly: | Symptom | Mechanism | Fix in order of leverage ||---|---|---|| Corners lift on a large flat ABS part | high contraction plus draught-driven gradients | enclosure; raise chamber temperature; brim; reduce part footprint or split the part || Part releases mid-print, PLA | adhesion, not warp | clean the plate (IPA, then soap and water for oils); first-layer squish (Post 01) || Tall PC part delaminates near the top | chamber cools with height; interface bonding falls | enclosure with an actively warm chamber; raise nozzle temperature; reduce cooling || Nylon curls despite an enclosure | crystallisation shrinkage plus moisture | dry the filament (Chapter 4) before blaming warp || Fibre-filled version of the same polymer warps less | fibres restrain matrix contraction | expected — this is a genuine benefit of CF/GF grades | > Common pitfall: adding a brim or raft to fight ABS warp. It buys a little and hides the cause. The stress is still in the part; you have merely bonded it harder to the plate, which converts corner lift into internal stress or a delamination crack higher up. Fix the thermal environment. > Trick: you can see the locked-in stress. Print a thin ABS bar without an enclosure, let it cool fully, then cut it lengthwise with a sharp blade. The two halves spring apart. A bar printed in a warm enclosure moves much less. This is a five-minute experiment that makes the whole chapter concrete. Chapter 4 — Moisture, the invisible variable Which polymers care Hygroscopic means the polymer absorbs water from air into its bulk, not just onto its surface. In rough order of severity for printing filaments: | Severity | Materials | Practical consequence ||---|---|---|| Extreme | PVA, PA (nylon), PC | unprintable within hours to days of open-air exposure in a humid room || High | TPU, PETG, ASA | visible surface and strength degradation over days to weeks || Moderate | ABS, PLA | degrades over weeks to months; PLA additionally hydrolyses when hot and wet, permanently shortening chains || Low | dried, sealed, unopened spools of anything | the state you want to maintain | The hydrolysis point matters and is often missed: for PLA and the polyesters, water is not just a bubble source. At extrusion temperature, absorbed water chemically cleaves the polymer chains. Drying removes the water but does not undo the chain scission. Wet-then-dried PLA prints acceptably and is measurably weaker than it should be. A standard brass nozzle is soft; those particles cut it. Chapter 6 — Properties you set after the print: annealing, post-cure, pigment Annealing Annealing is holding a printed part at an elevated temperature — above Tg but below the melting/softening point — for a period, then cooling it slowly. For a semi-crystalline polymer printed too fast to crystallise (PLA is the archetype), it does three things at once: 1. Increases crystallinity. Chains gain mobility above Tg and organise into crystalline domains. Stiffness, heat resistance and chemical resistance rise. Annealed PLA can retain useful stiffness meaningfully above its as-printed service ceiling — how much depends on the grade, the additives (nucleating agents in "HT" PLA grades are there specifically to make this work) and the schedule.2. Relieves internal stress. The locked-in stress from Chapter 3 relaxes. Good for stability, and it is why the part changes shape.3. Improves interlayer bonding somewhat. Chains interdiffuse across layer interfaces given mobility and time, reducing the Z-direction weakness of Post 07 — reducing, not eliminating. The cost is dimensional change, and it is not small. Crystallisation densifies the polymer, so the part shrinks — and it shrinks anisotropically, because the print's own layer structure and residual stress field are anisotropic. Parts also sag under their own weight if unsupported above Tg. Practical annealing procedure, if you decide the part needs it: 1. Decide first whether it does. Annealing is worth it for heat-exposed and stiffness-critical PLA parts. It is not worth it for anything that has to fit something else, unless you are prepared to re-measure and iterate.2. Support the part fully — bury it in dry sand, salt or plaster, or lay it on a flat plate with nothing overhanging.3. Hold above Tg but below softening: a common starting window for PLA is roughly 80–110 °C. Longer at a lower temperature is safer than shorter at a higher one.4. Cool slowly in the oven. Quenching re-introduces the stress you just removed.5. Measure the same features before and after and record the change as a percentage. That percentage is your machine-plus-material-plus-schedule constant, and you scale the CAD model by it next time. It is not a number anyone can give you — Post 05 gives the measurement discipline. > Watch out: annealing an amorphous polymer (ABS, PETG, PC) does not increase crystallinity, because there is no crystalline phase to grow. It still relieves stress, which can be useful, and it still causes dimensional change. Do not expect the stiffness gain that PLA shows. 3D Printing Course — 03 · Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing
Created2026-05-06|Engineering 3D Printing
There is one decision in 3D printing that outweighs every slicer setting combined, and it is made in about two seconds by dragging a model around a build plate. Orientation simultaneously determines which direction the part is strong in, which surfaces come out smooth, how much support material is needed, how long the print takes, and how accurate the holes are — and those five objectives conflict with each other, so there is no orientation that wins on all of them. This post makes that trade-off explicit by working a real part through it, then covers the machinery of overhangs, bridging, holes and supports, and finishes with the design habits that let you avoid most of the problem in the first place. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing (this post) → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - Evaluate a part's orientation against five conflicting objectives and defend the choice you make.- Apply the anisotropy rule — load along layers, never across them — to a real bracket in three orientations.- State the 45-degree overhang guideline properly, derive where it comes from, and compute how it shifts with layer height and extrusion width.- Explain what makes a bridge succeed, and run a bridging test to find your machine's usable span.- Explain why horizontal holes come out undersized and egg-shaped, and apply the teardrop and chamfer fixes.- Choose between normal and tree supports, and set the Z-gap that decides whether supports snap off or fuse on.- Design overhangs out of a part rather than supporting them.- Apply the minimum wall, minimum feature, bottom-chamfer and text rules relative to your own extrusion width.- Split a part sensibly and join it with the right method for the material.- Design a print-in-place mechanism and know what determines its clearance. Chapter 1 — Orientation as the master decision Terminology - Build orientation — the rotation of the part relative to the build plate. Three rotational degrees of freedom, and every one of them matters.- Strength axis — the direction in which the part's layers run continuously, and therefore the direction it is strongest in.- Overhang — a downward-facing surface with nothing directly beneath it.- Bridge — an unsupported horizontal span with both ends anchored in solid material.- Support — sacrificial structure printed to hold up overhangs, then removed.- Interface layer — the dense top layers of a support, which form the surface the part rests on.- Z-gap / contact distance — vertical clearance between support top and part underside.- Self-supporting — geometry whose every downward face is shallow enough to print without support.- Scar — the rough surface left where support touched the part. The five conflicting objectives Every orientation is a bet across these: 1. Strength. The service load should run along the layers, not across them (post 00, Chapter 3).2. Surface quality. Vertical walls get layer lines; top faces get skin; downward faces get support scars; sloped faces get stepping.3. Support volume. More overhangs means more sacrificial material, more time, more cleanup, more scars.4. Print time. Roughly, taller means more layers means longer — but a tall thin part also has short layers with long minimum-layer-time waits, so it is not linear.5. Dimensional accuracy. XY features are accurate in a different way than Z features; holes behave completely differently depending on axis; the first layer is squashed. They conflict. The orientation that makes a bracket strongest usually also makes it tallest, slowest, and puts its bolt holes on their sides. Rank the objectives for this specific part before you rotate anything. For a functional bracket, strength and hole accuracy outrank surface finish. For a display model, the reverse. A worked part: the L-bracket Take a concrete part. An L-shaped bracket: two legs, 60 mm and 40 mm, each 4 mm thick and 30 mm wide, with two clearance holes through each leg. In service it is bolted to a wall by the short leg, and a load hangs from the end of the long leg, bending the bracket about the inside corner. Three orientations, all of which someone will suggest: Orientation A — flat on its back. The short leg lies on the plate, the long leg stands vertically. Layers are horizontal planes stacked up the long leg. Orientation B — L-profile flat on the plate. The L profile lies in the plane of the bed and the 30 mm width becomes the build height. Each layer is a complete L outline, 4 mm thick, repeated 30 mm tall. Orientation C — standing on the corner. The bracket balanced at 45°, corner down, both legs rising. | | A — flat on its back | B — L-profile flat | C — on the corner ||---|---|---|---|| Layer direction relative to the service load | tensile stress at the corner runs across layers | tensile stress runs along continuous roads that wrap the corner | stress at roughly 45° to layers || Strength under the design load | worst — a clean interlayer break at the inside corner is the predicted failure | best — the corner is a continuous loop of material every layer | intermediate || Support needed | none | none | yes, under the corner and the underside of both legs || Print time | short — 60 mm tall, big cross-sections | moderate — 30 mm tall, small L cross-sections, many layers of little material | longest — support plus a tall diagonal envelope || Bed footprint | small | large — the whole L profile | small || Hole axis | vertical through the short leg, horizontal through the long leg | horizontal through both legs | diagonal through both || Hole quality | mixed: one good, one egg-shaped | both need teardrop or chamfer treatment, or drilling | worst — diagonal holes are both overhung and stepped || Visible surface quality | good on the outer faces; layer lines up the long leg | layer lines wrap the profile; the two big flat faces are top-skin and bed-face | support scars on the underside of both legs || Adhesion risk | good — a big flat first layer | good — a long thin first layer, watch corner lift | poor — small contact area, needs a brim | The decision. For a bracket that carries nothing and just needs to look tidy on a shelf, orientation A is faster, uses less bed, and gives one good hole for free. That is the shape of the reasoning. It is not "which orientation is correct" but "which objective am I optimising, and what am I paying for it". > Trick: before rotating a part, write down in one sentence how it will be loaded in service. Half of all orientation mistakes are made by people who have not consciously answered that question. Chapter 2 — The anisotropy rule The rule: load should run along layers, never peel them apart. Everything in post 00, Chapter 3 reduces to this. Material along a road is continuous polymer. Material across a layer interface is held together only by the chains that diffused across during the moments the interface was hot. The interface is always the weaker path. The failure modes even look different, which makes diagnosis easy: | | In-plane failure | Interlayer failure ||---|---|---|| Fracture surface | ragged, drawn, fibrous | flat, follows a single layer line, often glassy || Warning before failure | visible yielding, whitening, deformation | usually none — it goes suddenly || Fracture path | wanders through the section | perfectly planar and horizontal | If you snap a printed part and the break is a flat horizontal plane, the part was oriented wrong. That is the whole diagnosis. Three orientations of the same bracket, mechanically Return to the L-bracket. The load bends the long leg about the inside corner. Bending puts the outside of the corner in tension. - Orientation A: the tensile stress at the outer corner is vertical, i.e. perpendicular to the horizontal layers. The load is directly peeling the interfaces apart, one at a time, starting at the outermost fibre where the stress is highest. Failure is a clean horizontal snap at the corner, at a load far below what the material could take.- Orientation B: the tensile stress runs around the L profile, which is exactly the direction the perimeter roads run. Continuous polymer carries the load. The interfaces are loaded in shear rather than tension, and shear is a far more favourable loading for a weld.- Orientation C: the stress is at roughly 45° to the interfaces, giving a mixed mode. Better than A, worse than B, and it costs support material and surface quality on top. Two corrections to the rule It is not "print everything standing up" or "print everything flat". The rule is about the load, not about the part. A hook loaded in its own plane wants that plane flat on the bed; a hook loaded out of its plane wants the opposite. Reason from the load path every time. Walls do more than infill. As post 02 noted, in bending, material far from the neutral axis dominates. A part in the right orientation with four walls and 15 % infill will usually beat the same part in the wrong orientation at 60 % infill. Orientation first, walls second, infill third. Let α be the overhang angle measured from vertical — 0° is a vertical wall, 90° is a flat horizontal ceiling. That is only the geometric limit. Quality degrades well before it, because the unsupported portion of a road droops under gravity and surface tension before it solidifies. How much it droops depends on: - Cooling. More airflow means faster solidification and better overhangs. This is the same trade against layer adhesion as always.- Speed. A slower overhang perimeter gives each road time to set before the next lands on it.- Material. PLA solidifies quickly and overhangs well; PETG stays soft longer; ABS and ASA in a hot enclosure are the worst case.- Extrusion width. A wider road has more supported area relative to its overhang. 45° is simply an angle that is comfortably inside every one of these limits for common settings, which is why it survived as folklore. > Watch out: slicers do not agree on which end of the angle they measure from. PrusaSlicer's support threshold is stated with 90° meaning vertical; Cura's overhang angle is measured such that 0° supports everything and 90° supports nothing. The two conventions are complementary, and 45 is the one value where they happen to agree — which is exactly why the confusion goes unnoticed until you type 60. Check your slicer's tooltip before trusting the number. Measuring your own overhang threshold Print a stepped overhang test model with faces at 30°, 40°, 45°, 50°, 55°, 60°, 65° and 70° from vertical, with your normal profile and material. Inspect the undersides in raking light and pick the steepest angle whose underside you would accept on a real part. Then set your slicer's support threshold a few degrees conservative of that value. Repeat per material. The answer for PLA is not the answer for PETG. Bridging A bridge is different from an overhang: it is a road spanning from one anchor to another with nothing beneath any of it. It works because the extrudate is laid under tension — the nozzle is moving away from an anchored end, drawing the strand taut — and cools enough to hold its own weight before it sags appreciably. What a successful bridge needs: 1. Both ends anchored in solid material. A bridge to nowhere is a string.2. Slicer detection. Slicers identify bridging regions and apply dedicated bridge settings: full cooling, a specific bridge speed, and often a reduced bridge flow so the strand is drawn thinner and tauter.3. Unidirectional first layer. The first bridging layer is printed as parallel lines aligned with the span, not at the usual infill angle. Slicers do this automatically; when a bridge fails oddly, check the preview to see whether the slicer detected it as a bridge at all.4. Cooling. Bridging is the one place where maximum fan is nearly always right, whatever the material. Span limits depend on material, cooling, speed and width, so measure rather than quote. Print a bridging test with steadily increasing spans, and record the span at which sag becomes unacceptable for each material you use. PLA with strong cooling bridges impressively; PETG less so; ABS or ASA in a warm enclosure least of all — which is one more reason enclosed materials benefit from support-free design. This is the good case, but it is not perfect: - Consistently undersized. Roads following a concave inner curve are compressed on their inner edge, so material bulges into the bore. Add die swell and elastic recovery and vertical holes come out reliably a little small. This is systematic and repeatable, which means it is correctable — post 05 covers the compensation.- Polygonal. The hole is only as round as the mesh's tessellation. A coarsely exported 5 mm hole can be a visible dodecagon.- Seamed. The perimeter loop's start/stop lands somewhere in the bore, leaving a small ridge. Painted seam placement helps. Horizontal holes (axis parallel to the bed) This is the problem case, and the mechanism is worth seeing clearly. Walk up a horizontal cylindrical bore layer by layer. At the widest point of the circle the bore wall is vertical. As you climb toward the crown, the wall angle sweeps continuously from vertical through 45° and on to horizontal at the very top. So the upper part of every horizontal hole is an increasingly severe overhang, and the last stretch before the crown is effectively an unsupported bridge across the bore. The results are consistent: - The top of the bore sags into the hole, so the hole is undersized at the top.- The cross-section becomes egg-shaped or D-shaped rather than circular.- The overhanging surface is rough, so a shaft binds even where the hole is nominally large enough.- The effect worsens with larger diameters (longer unsupported span) and with taller layers. The fixes, in order of preference 1. Reorient so the hole is vertical. Always consider this first. It is free and it is the best result. It is also why hole axes should be part of the orientation decision in Chapter 1, not an afterthought. 2. Teardrop the hole. Replace the top arc of the circle with two straight faces at 45° from vertical, meeting at a point — the classic teardrop profile. Every surface then satisfies the self-supporting criterion and nothing droops. Cost: the hole is no longer a true circle, so it suits clearance holes, cable passes and bolt shanks rather than precision bearing seats. 3. Chamfer the top of the hole. A less intrusive variant: replace only the top of the arc with a short flat roof and two 45° chamfers, giving a hexagon-topped bore. Preserves more of the circular profile than a full teardrop, still self-supporting, and the flat roof is a short, reliable bridge. 4. Print undersize and machine to size. Model the hole a little small, then drill or ream it. This is the accurate answer for anything that must actually be round and on-size, and it is the standard practice for bearing seats and dowel holes. 5. Leave it and rely on bridging. For large holes with a well-tuned bridging profile, the crown can be acceptable. Check the slicer preview to confirm the crown is being treated as a bridge. > Common pitfall: teardropping a hole that a bolt head or a bearing has to sit in. The teardrop's point is above the nominal circle, so the hole is larger than nominal at the top and a press fit will be loose there. Teardrops belong on clearance features, not on fits. Chapter 5 — Supports Normal versus tree/organic | | Normal (linear/grid) supports | Tree / organic supports ||---|---|---|| Structure | vertical columns or a grid extruded straight down from every overhang | branching trunks that grow up from the plate or the model and reach out to touch only the points that need it || Material use | high | much lower || Print time | high | lower || Removal | often difficult, especially in deep pockets; comes off in fragments | usually snaps away in a few large pieces || Rigidity | high — good for very large flat overhangs | lower — branches can flex or topple on wide flat ceilings || Compute time | fast | can be slow on complex parts || Best for | large flat overhangs, tall parts needing stabilisation | organic shapes, figurines, parts with scattered small overhangs | Tree supports originated in the Cura engine; PrusaSlicer added its organic support implementation in version 2.6, and the PrusaSlicer-lineage slicers carry it. Both types are worth having in your repertoire — tree supports are not a strict upgrade. Interface layers The top few layers of a support can be printed denser than the body — the interface. A dense interface gives the part's downward face something continuous to land on, so the underside is smoother. The trade is direct: more interface density and more interface layers means a better underside and a harder removal. Where the machine allows it, an interface printed in a different material solves this outright. Water-soluble PVA or BVOH dissolves away completely. Alternatively, an interface in a material that bonds poorly to the part material — PETG under PLA is the classic pairing, and vice versa — releases cleanly with no dissolution needed. The Z-gap: the parameter that decides everything The Z-gap (contact distance, top contact Z distance) is the vertical clearance left between the top of the support and the part's underside. It is the single parameter that determines whether supports snap off or fuse on. - Too large: the part's first layer over the support has nothing to sit on, and droops badly. You get an ugly underside and, at the extreme, a failed region.- Too small, or zero: the support welds to the part. Removal tears the surface, or is simply not possible without damaging the part. A common starting point is one layer height. The correct value is a material property in practice, because it depends on how aggressively the material bonds to itself — PETG self-bonds far more strongly than PLA and generally needs a larger gap for the same removability. Procedure: find your Z-gap 1. Model or download a small test block with a flat overhanging ledge, and put four copies on the plate.2. Use per-object settings (post 02) to give each copy a different Z-gap: 0, 0.5×, 1.0× and 1.5× layer height.3. Print in the material you care about, with your normal profile.4. Remove the supports from each and judge two things: how hard removal was, and how good the resulting underside is.5. Choose the largest gap that still gives an acceptable underside. Record it in your material profile.6. Repeat per material. Do not carry a PLA value into a PETG profile. There is a matching XY separation parameter controlling horizontal clearance between support and vertical part walls; the same logic applies, and it matters most for supports inside pockets and against tall walls. Support strategy options - Support on build plate only. Supports are generated only where they can reach the plate, so nothing ever touches the model's own surfaces except the underside of true overhangs above the plate. No scars on model faces. It fails whenever an overhang sits above the part itself.- Painted supports (enforcers and blockers). Paint directly on the model where you want support and where you forbid it. Almost always better than adjusting the global angle threshold, which is a blunt instrument that adds support everywhere the threshold catches.- Support blockers as modifier volumes. Drop a box over a region that must stay clean. The scar Supports always leave a mark. The interface never separates perfectly cleanly, so a supported face is rougher than an unsupported one and dimensionally less reliable. Three responses, in order of quality: 1. Design the overhang away (Chapter 6). Best answer.2. Orient so the scar lands on a face nobody sees or measures. Second best.3. Accept it and post-process — knife, file, sanding. Real, but it is manual labour on every copy. Chapter 6 — Support-free design Support is a workaround. The superior answer, wherever the part's function permits, is geometry that does not need it. Chamfers instead of overhangs Under a horizontal ledge, boss or shelf, add a 45° chamfer rather than leaving a square underside. The chamfer is self-supporting, prints cleanly and adds material exactly where a bending stress concentration would otherwise sit. Note the asymmetry with fillets: a fillet under a ledge is not a good substitute, because a fillet's surface becomes tangent to horizontal at its lower end — the worst possible overhang, right where the geometry starts. Chamfer downward-facing transitions; fillet upward-facing ones. Self-supporting geometry throughout Once you have measured your machine's overhang threshold (Chapter 3), design to it. Cones instead of cylinders on downward faces, pyramidal instead of rectangular bosses, sloped instead of flat undersides. On a part designed this way, the support setting is simply "off", and that is worth a great deal of cleanup time over a print run. Sacrificial bridge layers Over a large horizontal internal hole or pocket, a single sacrificial layer of bridging material gives the layers above something to build on. Many slicers can add this automatically for internal features; it can also be modelled deliberately as a thin membrane to be drilled out afterwards. Split the part If a part cannot be made support-free in any single orientation, cut it. Two halves, each oriented for its own loads and each printing support-free, glued or bolted together, will often beat one supported part on strength, surface and time simultaneously. See Chapter 7 for where to cut and how to join. Print in place Sometimes the correct answer to "how do I assemble this" is "don't". Print-in-place hinges, captive nuts, chain links and compliant mechanisms remove assembly entirely. They are clearance problems, and that is the topic of Chapter 7's last section. Put text and detail where the process can print it Embossed text on a top or vertical face prints legibly. Engraved text on a top face prints legibly. Text on a downward face is a support problem and will be a mess. Move it. Chapter 7 — Designing for the process Minimum wall thickness relative to extrusion width A wall is built from an integer number of roads. If your extrusion width is 0.45 mm, then: A wall of 1.0 mm asks for 2.22 roads. Classic perimeter generation lays two roads and dumps the remaining 0.10 mm into gap fill; variable-width extrusion (post 02) instead widens both roads to 0.50 mm, which is better but still a compromise. The rule: design walls as whole multiples of your extrusion width, and treat two roads as the practical structural minimum for anything that carries load or needs to survive handling. Single-road walls exist and are useful for vase-mode parts and light enclosures, but they are not structure. Note the dependency: this rule is tied to your nozzle and profile. If you design a 0.90 mm wall and later print with a 0.6 mm nozzle, the wall becomes an awkward 1.5 roads. Parametrise the wall thickness in CAD (post 06) rather than hard-coding it. Minimum feature size - Nothing narrower than one extrusion width can exist. A 0.3 mm rib on a 0.45 mm profile is simply not printed, or is printed at 0.45 mm and is therefore wrong.- Embossed or engraved detail should be at least two extrusion widths wide and at least two layers deep or tall to be legible. Below that it disappears into the surface texture.- Small holes close up. A 1 mm vertical hole in a 0.4 mm nozzle profile has room for barely one road around it; expect it to be undersized and rough, and plan to drill it. Chamfer the bottom edge Elephant foot (post 01) bulges the bottom edge outward. Slicers offer a compensation setting that shrinks the first layer inward, but the better fix lives in the model: put a small chamfer — typically a few tenths of a millimetre at 45° — on every bottom edge. The chamfer gives the squashed first layer somewhere to bulge into rather than past the nominal footprint. The part still sits flat, the base dimension is preserved, and the fix travels with the model into any slicer, on any printer, forever. This is one of the highest value-per-effort habits in the whole discipline. Text - Cap height of roughly 4–5 mm and above reads reliably; below that, legibility depends heavily on the font and on your extrusion width.- Stroke width should be at least two extrusion widths. Thin serif and script fonts do not survive; a bold sans-serif does.- Relief depth of at least two layers, both for embossed and engraved.- Embossed text generally reads better than engraved on FDM, because engraved text's fine channels get partially filled.- On a vertical face, vertical resolution of the glyph is limited by layer height, so curves and thin horizontals suffer. Thin unsupported pillars A tall, thin pillar is the classic failed feature, and there are three separate reasons: 1. It has no time to cool. Each layer is tiny, so the nozzle returns before the previous layer has solidified, and the pillar turns into a soft blob.2. It has no rigidity. The nozzle's drag and the travel moves knock it around.3. It is unstable. A tall narrow column has a small base and a high aspect ratio. Fixes, in order: taper it (a cone is stronger and cools better than a cylinder); enable minimum layer time or slow down for short layers; print several parts at once so each pillar has time to cool between visits; add a sacrificial cooling tower next to it; or reorient so the pillar lies down. Splitting and joining Where to cut. Choose a plane that (a) lets each half print support-free, (b) puts each half's layers in the right direction for its own loads, (c) does not pass through the region of highest stress, and (d) leaves a joint you can align. Cutting straight through a bending corner puts the joint exactly where the part is trying hardest to break. Alignment features. Never rely on a plain butt joint plus glue for alignment. Add: - Dowel pins — printed pins and matching holes, or bought steel/brass pins in reamed holes (more accurate).- Dovetails or keyed slots — self-aligning and carry shear load without relying on the adhesive.- Lip and groove — a step around the joint that locates the halves and increases bonded area.- A screw plus a heat-set insert — a mechanical joint that can be taken apart again. Real Analysis — 05 · Sequences of Functions, Uniform Convergence and Power Series
Created2026-05-04|Mathematics Real Analysis
Much of analysis builds functions as limits: $e^x$ as a power series, $\sin x$ as a Taylor series, the Weierstrass function of post 03 as a sum of cosines, solutions of differential equations as limits of approximations. The question is always the same: which properties survive the limit? Continuity, integrals and derivatives can all be lost under the obvious "point by point" notion of convergence. This post introduces the stronger notion that repairs most of this, uniform convergence, proves exactly what it preserves (continuity and integrals) and what it does not (derivatives, without an extra hypothesis), and then applies it to power series: radius of convergence, term-by-term calculus, Abel's theorem, and the difference between a Taylor series and the function it came from. It closes the core course with the Weierstrass approximation theorem and a map of where analysis goes next. > Series map: 00 The Real Numbers & Completeness → 01 Sequences & Series → 02 Topology of ℝ & Continuity → 03 Differentiation & Taylor's Theorem → 04 The Riemann Integral → 05 Uniform Convergence & Power Series (this post). Learning Outcome - Define pointwise and uniform convergence of a sequence of functions, and tell them apart with $x^n$ on $[0, 1]$.- Use the sup-norm test and the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence.- Prove that a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (the $\varepsilon/3$ argument).- Prove that integrals commute with uniform limits, and show with an example that pointwise limits break this.- State the theorem on limits of derivatives and give the counterexample that shows why it needs uniform convergence of the derivatives.- State and prove the Weierstrass M-test and use it on series of functions.- Compute the radius of convergence with the Cauchy–Hadamard formula and the ratio test, and check the endpoints.- Prove that a power series converges uniformly on every closed interval inside its radius, and differentiate and integrate it term by term.- State Abel's theorem and use it to show $1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \dots = \ln 2$.- Explain why a smooth function need not equal its Taylor series, and state the Weierstrass approximation theorem with the Bernstein-polynomial idea. Chapter 1 — Pointwise versus uniform convergence Terminology - Sequence of functions — functions $fn : A \to \mathbb{R}$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$, all on the same domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.- Pointwise convergence — $fn \to f$ pointwise on $A$ if for each fixed $x \in A$, the number sequence $fn(x)$ converges to $f(x)$. In quantifiers: for every $x \in A$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ (which may depend on both $x$ and $\varepsilon$) with $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $n \geq N$.- Uniform convergence — $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ (depending on $\varepsilon$ only) with $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $n \geq N$ and all $x \in A$.- Sup norm — for a bounded $g : A \to \mathbb{R}$, $\lVert g\rVert\infty = \sup{x \in A}\lvert g(x)\rvert$.- Epsilon-tube — the band of width $\varepsilon$ on each side of the graph of $f$. Uniform convergence says: from some $N$ on, the whole graph of every $fn$ lies inside the tube. The only difference between the two definitions is the order of "there is $N$" and "for all $x$". Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence; the converse fails. Sup-norm test. $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$ if and only if $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty \to 0$ (where $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty$ is finite for large $n$). This is the definition rewritten: "$\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $x$" gives $\sup \leq \varepsilon$, and $\sup < \varepsilon$ gives it for all $x$. The key example: x to the n on [0, 1] Let $fn(x) = x^n$ on $[0, 1]$. For $0 \leq x < 1$, $x^n \to 0$ (post 01); at $x = 1$, $1^n = 1$. So the pointwise limit is $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \leq x < 1, \\ 1 & x = 1. \end{cases}$$ Every $fn$ is continuous, and the limit is not. The convergence is not uniform: the point $xn = (1/2)^{1/n}$ lies in $[0, 1)$ and $fn(xn) - f(xn) = \frac12$, so $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty \geq \frac12$ for every $n$ (in fact the sup over $[0,1)$ is $1$). The "bad point" slides towards $1$: | $n$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 50 ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| $xn = (1/2)^{1/n}$ | $0.5$ | $0.7071$ | $0.8706$ | $0.9330$ | $0.9862$ | For each fixed $x < 1$ you eventually get close to $0$, but no single $N$ works for all $x$ at once. On a smaller domain the problem disappears. On $[0, \frac12]$, $\lVert x^n - 0\rVert\infty = (\frac12)^n \to 0$, so $x^n \to 0$ uniformly on $[0, \frac12]$, and indeed on $[0, r]$ for any $r < 1$. Damping the bad point. Multiply by $(1 - x)$: $kn(x) = x^n(1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$. The pointwise limit is $0$ everywhere (including $x = 1$, where $kn(1) = 0$). Setting $kn'(x) = x^{n-1}\bigl(n - (n+1)x\bigr) = 0$ gives the maximum at $x = \frac{n}{n+1}$, so $$\lVert kn\rVert\infty = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \frac{1}{n+1} < \frac{1}{n+1} \to 0.$$ So $kn \to 0$ uniformly on all of $[0, 1]$. For example, $\lVert k{10}\rVert\infty = \frac{10^{10}}{11^{11}} \approx 0.0350$. The factor $(1 - x)$ kills the values near $1$ exactly where $x^n$ misbehaved. A uniform example: smoothing the corner of the absolute value Let $gn(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 1/n^2}$ on $\mathbb{R}$. With $h = 1/n$, $$0 \leq \sqrt{x^2 + h^2} - \lvert x\rvert = \frac{h^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2} + \lvert x\rvert} \leq \frac{h^2}{h} = h,$$ with equality at $x = 0$. So $\lVert gn - \lvert x\rvert\rVert\infty = \frac1n \to 0$: uniform convergence to $\lvert x\rvert$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$. With $\varepsilon = 0.25$, every $gn$ with $n \geq 5$ lies inside the $\varepsilon$-tube around $\lvert x\rvert$ (its largest error is $\frac15 = 0.2$), while $g2$ (error $0.5$) does not. Every $gn$ is smooth, and the limit $\lvert x\rvert$ has a corner. Uniform convergence preserved continuity but not differentiability. Chapter 2 explains both facts. The Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence Theorem. A sequence $(fn)$ converges uniformly on $A$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ with $$\lvert fn(x) - fm(x)\rvert < \varepsilon \quad \text{for all } m, n \geq N \text{ and all } x \in A.$$ Proof. ($\Rightarrow$) If $\lvert fn - f\rvert < \varepsilon/2$ everywhere for $n \geq N$, then $\lvert fn - fm\rvert \leq \lvert fn - f\rvert + \lvert f - fm\rvert < \varepsilon$. ($\Leftarrow$) For each fixed $x$, $(fn(x))$ is a Cauchy sequence of reals, so it converges (completeness, post 01); call the limit $f(x)$. Given $\varepsilon$, take $N$ from the hypothesis. For $n \geq N$ and any $x$, let $m \to \infty$ in $\lvert fn(x) - fm(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ to get $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert \leq \varepsilon$. The same $N$ works for every $x$, so the convergence is uniform. $\blacksquare$ The value of the criterion: it proves uniform convergence without knowing the limit in advance. The M-test in Chapter 3 is built on it. Chapter 2 — What uniform convergence preserves Continuity Theorem. If each $fn$ is continuous at $c \in A$ and $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$, then $f$ is continuous at $c$. Proof (the $\varepsilon/3$ argument). Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $N$ with $\lvert fN(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon/3$ for all $x \in A$. Since $fN$ is continuous at $c$, choose $\delta$ with $\lvert fN(x) - fN(c)\rvert < \varepsilon/3$ for $\lvert x - c\rvert < \delta$. Then for such $x$, $$\lvert f(x) - f(c)\rvert \leq \lvert f(x) - fN(x)\rvert + \lvert fN(x) - fN(c)\rvert + \lvert fN(c) - f(c)\rvert < \frac\varepsilon3 + \frac\varepsilon3 + \frac\varepsilon3 = \varepsilon. \qquad \blacksquare$$ The first and third terms need the same $N$ at two different points, $x$ and $c$. That is exactly what uniformity supplies and pointwise convergence does not. The theorem also gives a quick proof that $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0, 1]$: the limit is discontinuous. A partial converse (Dini's theorem). If $K$ is compact, each $fn$ is continuous on $K$, $fn(x) \geq f{n+1}(x)$ for all $x$ and $n$, and $fn \to f$ pointwise with $f$ continuous, then the convergence is uniform. (Rudin, Theorem 7.13.) All four hypotheses are needed; $x^n$ on $[0, 1]$ fails only "the limit is continuous". Integrals Theorem. If each $fn$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$ and $fn \to f$ uniformly on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable and $$\inta^b f = \lim{n \to \infty} \inta^b fn.$$ Proof. Let $\varepsilonn = \lVert fn - f\rVert\infty$, which tends to $0$. Then $fn - \varepsilonn \leq f \leq fn + \varepsilonn$ on $[a, b]$ (so $f$ is bounded). Upper and lower sums respect these inequalities, so the upper and lower integrals of $f$ (post 04) satisfy $$\inta^b fn - \varepsilonn(b - a) \leq \underline{\inta^b} f \leq \overline{\inta^b} f \leq \inta^b fn + \varepsilonn(b - a).$$ The gap between the upper and lower integrals is at most $2\varepsilonn(b - a) \to 0$, so they are equal and $f$ is integrable. The same inequalities give $\left\lvert \inta^b f - \inta^b fn\right\rvert \leq \varepsilonn(b - a) \to 0$. $\blacksquare$ Pointwise convergence is not enough. Let $fn(x) = n x (1 - x^2)^n$ on $[0, 1]$. For $0 < x < 1$, $fn(x) \to 0$ (a geometric factor beats the factor $n$), and $fn(0) = fn(1) = 0$. So $fn \to 0$ pointwise. But $$\int0^1 n x (1 - x^2)^n\,dx = \left[-\frac{n}{2(n+1)}(1 - x^2)^{n+1}\right]0^1 = \frac{n}{2(n+1)} \to \frac12 \neq 0 = \int0^1 0\,dx.$$ The convergence is far from uniform: the maximum of $fn$, at $x = 1/\sqrt{2n + 1}$, grows without bound (about $1.34$ for $n = 10$ and $4.28$ for $n = 100$). A travelling spike keeps area $\approx \frac12$ while its base shrinks. Derivatives need more Uniform convergence of $fn$ says nothing about $fn'$. Two warnings: - $gn(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 1/n^2} \to \lvert x\rvert$ uniformly (Chapter 1): smooth functions, non-differentiable limit.- $hn(x) = \frac{\sin(n^2 x)}{n} \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ (the sup norm is $\frac1n$), but $hn'(x) = n\cos(n^2 x)$, and $hn'(0) = n \to \infty$. The derivatives do not converge to the derivative of the limit, which is $0$. The fix is to assume uniform convergence of the derivatives. Theorem (limits of derivatives). Let each $fn$ be differentiable on $[a, b]$. Suppose $(fn(x0))$ converges for some $x0 \in [a, b]$, and $(fn')$ converges uniformly on $[a, b]$. Then $(fn)$ converges uniformly on $[a, b]$ to a function $f$, and $f'(x) = \lim{n \to \infty} fn'(x)$ for every $x \in [a, b]$. (Rudin, Theorem 7.17.) Idea of proof (with the extra assumption that each $fn'$ is continuous). By FTC 2 (post 04), $fn(x) = fn(x0) + \int{x0}^x fn'$. The integrals converge uniformly by the integral theorem above, so $fn \to f(x) = f(x0) + \int{x0}^x g$ with $g = \lim fn'$, which is continuous as a uniform limit. FTC 1 gives $f' = g$. The general proof uses the Mean Value Theorem instead of integrals. > Watch out: "uniformly" in this theorem is about the derivatives. Uniform convergence of $fn$ alone never licenses swapping $\lim$ and $\frac{d}{dx}$. Chapter 3 — Series of functions and the Weierstrass M-test A series of functions $\sum{k=1}^{\infty} uk(x)$ converges pointwise or uniformly on $A$ when its partial sums $Sn(x) = \sum{k=1}^{n} uk(x)$ do. The three theorems of Chapter 2 become: | If the series converges uniformly on the interval and | then ||---|---|| each $uk$ is continuous | the sum is continuous || each $uk$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ | $\int \sum uk = \sum \int uk$ (integrate term by term) || each $uk$ is differentiable, $\sum uk'$ converges uniformly, $\sum uk(x0)$ converges | $(\sum uk)' = \sum uk'$ (differentiate term by term) | Theorem (Weierstrass M-test). Suppose $\lvert uk(x)\rvert \leq Mk$ for all $x \in A$ and all $k$, and $\sum Mk$ converges. Then $\sum uk$ converges uniformly (and absolutely) on $A$. Proof. For $n > m$ and every $x \in A$, $$\lvert Sn(x) - Sm(x)\rvert = \left\lvert \sum{k=m+1}^{n} uk(x)\right\rvert \leq \sum{k=m+1}^{n} Mk.$$ The right side does not depend on $x$, and it is small for large $m$ because $\sum Mk$ converges (Cauchy criterion for number series, post 01). So the partial sums satisfy the uniform Cauchy criterion of Chapter 1. $\blacksquare$ Worked example. $F(x) = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}$. Take $Mk = \frac{1}{k^2}$; $\sum \frac1{k^2}$ converges (its value is $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$). So the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $F$ is continuous. Term-by-term differentiation would give $\sum \frac{\cos(kx)}{k}$, which diverges at $x = 0$ (harmonic series). So the M-test gives continuity, and says nothing about differentiability. The same test proves the continuity of the Weierstrass function in post 03: $\lvert a^n\cos(b^n\pi x)\rvert \leq a^n$ with $\sum a^n$ a convergent geometric series. > Common pitfall: the M-test is sufficient, not necessary. $\sum{k \geq 1} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}$ (constant functions) converges uniformly, trivially, but no summable $Mk \geq \frac1k$ exists. Chapter 4 — Power series Terminology - Power series about $x0$ — a series $\sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak (x - x0)^k$ with real coefficients $ak$. Below, $x0 = 0$ for simplicity.- Radius of convergence — the number $R \in [0, \infty]$ such that the series converges absolutely for $\lvert x\rvert < R$ and diverges for $\lvert x\rvert > R$. At $x = \pm R$ anything can happen.- Limit superior — $\limsup{k} bk = \lim{n \to \infty} \sup{k \geq n} bk$, which always exists in $[-\infty, \infty]$ (monotone convergence, post 01). Radius of convergence Theorem (Cauchy–Hadamard). Let $\beta = \limsup{k \to \infty} \lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$. Then the radius of convergence is $R = 1/\beta$ (with $R = \infty$ if $\beta = 0$ and $R = 0$ if $\beta = \infty$). Proof. Apply the root test (post 01) to $\sum \lvert ak x^k\rvert$: $\limsup \lvert ak x^k\rvert^{1/k} = \beta\lvert x\rvert$. If $\beta\lvert x\rvert < 1$ the series converges absolutely; if $\beta\lvert x\rvert > 1$ the terms do not tend to $0$, so it diverges. $\blacksquare$ Ratio formula. If $\lim \left\lvert \frac{ak}{a{k+1}}\right\rvert$ exists (in $[0, \infty]$), it equals $R$. When it applies it is easier; when it does not, the root formula still works. | Series | $R$ | At $x = R$ | At $x = -R$ ||---|---|---|---|| $\sum x^k$ | $1$ | diverges | diverges || $\sum x^k / k$ (from $k = 1$) | $1$ | diverges (harmonic) | converges (alternating) || $\sum x^k / k^2$ (from $k = 1$) | $1$ | converges | converges || $\sum x^k / k!$ | $\infty$ | — | — || $\sum k!\,x^k$ | $0$ | — | — | The first three show that all endpoint behaviours occur with the same radius. When the ratio test fails. Let $ak = 2^k$ for even $k$ and $ak = 1$ for odd $k$. The ratios $\lvert ak / a{k+1}\rvert$ alternate between values tending to $0$ and to $\infty$, so they have no limit. But $\lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$ is $2$ for even $k$ and $1$ for odd $k$, so $\limsup = 2$ and $R = \frac12$. Uniform convergence inside the radius Theorem. If $0 < r < R$, the power series converges uniformly on $[-r, r]$. Consequently it converges uniformly on every compact subset of $(-R, R)$, and its sum is continuous on $(-R, R)$. Proof. Since $r < R$, the series converges absolutely at $x = r$, so $\sum \lvert ak\rvert r^k$ converges. On $[-r, r]$, $\lvert ak x^k\rvert \leq \lvert ak\rvert r^k = Mk$. Apply the M-test. A compact $K \subset (-R, R)$ is closed and bounded, so its max of $\lvert x\rvert$ is some $r < R$ and $K \subseteq [-r, r]$. Every $x \in (-R, R)$ lies in some $[-r, r]$, and continuity is local. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: the convergence need not be uniform on the whole open interval. For $\sum x^k$ on $(-1, 1)$ the tail after $x^n$ is $\sum{k > n} x^k = \frac{x^{n+1}}{1 - x}$, which is unbounded as $x \to 1^-$ for every $n$. Term-by-term differentiation and integration Theorem. Let $f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak x^k$ with radius $R > 0$. Then on $(-R, R)$: $$f'(x) = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} k\,ak x^{k-1}, \qquad \int0^x f(t)\,dt = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{ak}{k+1} x^{k+1},$$ and both new series also have radius $R$. Hence $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $(-R, R)$, and $ak = \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}$: a power series is the Taylor series of its own sum. Proof sketch. Since $k^{1/k} \to 1$, $\limsup (k\lvert ak\rvert)^{1/k} = \limsup \lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$, so the differentiated series has the same radius (similarly for the integrated one). On any $[-r, r]$ with $r < R$ the differentiated series converges uniformly, so the derivative theorem (Chapter 2) applies. Integration follows from the integral theorem. Repeating gives all derivatives, and evaluating the $k$-th derivative at $0$ leaves only $k!\,ak$. $\blacksquare$ Worked example 1. From $\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum{k \geq 0} x^k$ on $(-1, 1)$, differentiate: $\frac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = \sum{k \geq 1} k x^{k-1}$. Multiply by $x$ and put $x = \frac12$: $$\sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{2^k} = \frac{1/2}{(1 - 1/2)^2} = 2.$$ Worked example 2. From $\frac{1}{1 + t} = \sum{k \geq 0} (-1)^k t^k$ on $(-1, 1)$, integrate from $0$ to $x$: $$\ln(1 + x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \dots = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}x^k, \qquad \lvert x\rvert < 1.$$ Worked example 3. $E(x) = \sum{k \geq 0} \frac{x^k}{k!}$ has $R = \infty$. Differentiating term by term gives $E' = E$, with $E(0) = 1$. So $E(x) = e^x$ (the function $E(x)e^{-x}$ has zero derivative, hence is constant, by post 03). Abel's theorem at the edge The formula for $\ln(1 + x)$ was proved for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$. At $x = 1$ the series $1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \dots$ converges (alternating series test, post 01), but does it converge to $\ln 2$? Theorem (Abel). Suppose $\sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak$ converges. Then $f(x) = \sum ak x^k$ converges for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$ and $$\lim{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak.$$ Proof. Let $s = \sum ak$. Replacing $a0$ by $a0 - s$ changes $f$ by the constant $-s$ and makes the sum $0$, so assume $s = 0$. Let $sn = a0 + \dots + an$; then $sn \to 0$, so $(sn)$ is bounded and $\sum sk x^k$ converges for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$. Summation by parts ($ak = sk - s{k-1}$) gives, for $0 < x < 1$, $$f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak x^k = (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{\infty} sk x^k.$$ Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ with $\lvert sk\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $k > N$. Then $$\lvert f(x)\rvert \leq (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{N}\lvert sk\rvert + \varepsilon(1 - x)\sum{k > N} x^k \leq (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{N}\lvert sk\rvert + \varepsilon.$$ The first term tends to $0$ as $x \to 1^-$, so $\lvert f(x)\rvert < 2\varepsilon$ for $x$ close enough to $1$. Hence $f(x) \to 0 = s$. $\blacksquare$ Apply it to $ak = \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}$: the left side is $\lim{x \to 1^-}\ln(1 + x) = \ln 2$ by continuity of $\ln$. So $$1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \frac14 + \dots = \ln 2 \approx 0.693147.$$ The same argument with $\arctan x = \sum{k \geq 0} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}$ gives $1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots = \frac{\pi}{4}$. (These series converge slowly: the first 10 terms of the $\ln 2$ series sum to $0.6456$.) > Common pitfall: Abel's theorem needs the series at $x = 1$ to converge. For $ak = (-1)^k$, $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x} \to \frac12$ as $x \to 1^-$, but $\sum (-1)^k$ diverges; the limit $\frac12$ is not a sum in the ordinary sense. Chapter 5 — Taylor series, approximation, and what comes next Taylor series versus the function For a $C^\infty$ function $f$, the Taylor series at $0$ is $\sum \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k$. Two things can go wrong: 1. The series can have radius $0$ (there are $C^\infty$ functions like this).2. The series can converge, to a different function. Case 2 is the function of post 03: $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ with $f(0) = 0$. All derivatives at $0$ vanish, so its Taylor series is $0 + 0x + 0x^2 + \dots$, with radius $\infty$ and sum $0$. It equals $f$ only at $x = 0$. The link to Taylor's theorem is the remainder: the Taylor series of $f$ converges to $f(x)$ exactly when $Rn(x) \to 0$. For $e^x$ at any $x$, the Lagrange remainder satisfies $\lvert Rn(x)\rvert \leq e^{\lvert x\rvert}\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \to 0$, so $e^x$ equals its Taylor series everywhere. A function is real analytic at $x0$ when it equals a convergent power series on some interval around $x0$. By the term-by-term theorem, such a power series must be the Taylor series. So: analytic ⇒ $C^\infty$, but not conversely. The Weierstrass approximation theorem Not every continuous function is a power series, but every continuous function is uniformly close to a polynomial. Theorem (Weierstrass, 1885). Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a polynomial $p$ with $\lvert f(x) - p(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. Bernstein's constructive proof (idea). On $[0, 1]$, define $$Bn f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{n} f\!\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\binom{n}{k} x^k (1 - x)^{n-k}.$$ The weights $\binom{n}{k}x^k(1-x)^{n-k}$ are the binomial probabilities of $k$ successes in $n$ trials with success probability $x$. They add up to $1$ and concentrate near $k/n \approx x$ as $n$ grows, so $Bn f(x)$ is a weighted average of values of $f$ near $x$. Uniform continuity of $f$ (post 02) turns this into $\lVert Bn f - f\rVert\infty \to 0$. Worked example. For $f(x) = x^2$ a direct computation gives $$Bn f(x) = x^2 + \frac{x(1 - x)}{n},$$ so the error is $\frac{x(1-x)}{n}$, largest at $x = \frac12$, and $\lVert Bn f - f\rVert\infty = \frac{1}{4n}$. An error of at most $0.01$ needs $n \geq 25$. The convergence is uniform but slow; Bernstein polynomials are valued for their shape-preserving properties (they are the basis of Bézier curves), not their speed. Compare with the Weierstrass function of post 03: a continuous, nowhere-differentiable function can still be approximated uniformly, to any accuracy, by polynomials, which are as smooth as functions get. Uniform limits keep continuity and lose everything finer. A look ahead - Metric spaces. Uniform convergence is convergence in the metric $d(f, g) = \lVert f - g\rVert\infty$ on $C[a, b]$. Chapter 1's Cauchy criterion, together with the continuity theorem of Chapter 2, says this space is complete, and Weierstrass says polynomials are dense in it. The same words (complete, dense, compact) from posts 00–02 now describe spaces of functions.- Lebesgue integration. The spike example $n x(1 - x^2)^n$ shows that pointwise limits and integrals do not commute in general. Lebesgue's theory says exactly when they do (monotone and dominated convergence), without asking for uniformity.- Fourier series. Replacing powers $x^k$ by $\cos(kx)$ and $\sin(kx)$ gives Fourier series. Their convergence is subtler than for power series: the Fourier series of a discontinuous square wave converges pointwise at every point but never uniformly, which ties back to the continuity theorem of Chapter 2. The slicer is where almost all of your decisions actually live. The printer executes; the slicer decides. Yet most people use it as a box with a "quality" dropdown, which means the interesting settings — the ones that decide whether a part is strong, accurate, fast or pretty — are being chosen by a vendor who has never seen your part. Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes Terminology - Mesh — the triangle soup the slicer receives. Must be manifold (watertight, every edge shared by exactly two faces, consistent normals) for slicing to be well defined.- Layer polygon — the set of closed 2D contours produced by intersecting the mesh with a horizontal plane at one Z. Outer contours are the part; inner contours are holes.- Offset (inset) — shrinking a polygon inward by a fixed distance. Perimeter generation is repeated offsetting.- Perimeter / wall — a closed extrusion loop following the layer's contour.- Skin / solid layer — fully dense region on a top or bottom surface.- Sparse infill — the low-density interior lattice.- Toolpath — an ordered sequence of moves with extrusion amounts attached.- Seam — the point where a closed perimeter loop starts and ends.- Extrusion width — the intended width of one deposited road. A slicer parameter, not a nozzle property. The pipeline Two things are worth noticing about this list. Step 3 is why mesh quality matters. A plane–mesh intersection only yields closed loops if the mesh is closed. A hole in the mesh gives an open polyline, which the slicer must guess how to close — and its guess is where the weird stray extrusion in the middle of your part comes from. Fix the model, not the slicer settings. Step 5 is why "top layers" behaves unintuitively. Solid skin is not a property of the top face; it is a boolean result computed per layer. A shallow dome has a top surface at nearly every layer, so it gets skin over a large part of its height, and that is why gently sloped surfaces are slow and material-hungry. Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width Layer height versus nozzle diameter The widely used rule of thumb is a practical ceiling of roughly 75–80 % of nozzle diameter — about 0.30–0.32 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical constant, and it exists for a mechanical reason worth understanding. The flat annular face around the nozzle orifice acts as a shoe. It rides above the previous layer at the layer height and irons the emerging extrudate down into it, which is what creates the wide contact patch and the pressure that drives interdiffusion (post 00). As layer height approaches nozzle diameter, the shoe stops touching the extrudate at all: material simply falls out of the orifice as a round noodle, keys poorly to the layer below, and stacks unstably. Caveats in both directions: - High-flow hotends and internally-restructured nozzles change the melting side of the problem but not the shoe geometry. Some users run higher ratios successfully; verify on your own machine before designing around it.- Going very thin has its own floor. Below roughly a quarter of nozzle diameter, small Z errors and bed-mesh residuals become a large fraction of the layer, and print time grows without a proportional gain in quality. The first-layer exception The first layer routinely ignores the rule and runs thicker — commonly 0.2–0.3 mm even in fine profiles — for two reasons: a thicker layer swallows small bed irregularities and mesh residuals, and it produces a larger contact patch for adhesion. It is also frequently printed wider than other layers for the same reason. What layer height does and does not control | Layer height controls | Layer height does not control ||---|---|| Visible stepping on sloped and curved surfaces | detail in the XY plane — that is nozzle diameter and extrusion width || The Z resolution of features (a 0.15 mm step cannot exist in a 0.2 mm layer) | hole roundness or XY dimensional accuracy || Print time — roughly inversely, but not exactly | wall thickness || The number of layer interfaces per millimetre of height | whether a part is strong (interface count and interface quality pull in opposite directions; see below) || Overhang behaviour, indirectly (each layer's unsupported step scales with height) | support requirement | On strength, resist the confident claim in either direction. Thinner layers give more interfaces but each one is smaller, cools differently and reheats more often. The published results are material- and geometry-dependent, and the effect is generally much smaller than the effect of orientation (post 03) or of wall count. Spend your attention there instead. Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter This surprises people, and it is the single most useful conceptual unlock in the slicer. The width of a deposited road is set by how much material you push per unit of travel and how high the nozzle is, not by the orifice diameter. Push more, and the extrudate spreads sideways under the shoe until it is wider. Push less, and it is narrower. The orifice only limits how fast you can push it. Usable range is roughly 100–200 % of nozzle diameter. Below about 100 %, the road becomes unreliable and the nozzle drags. Above about 200 %, the extrudate escapes past the shoe's control and the surface becomes lumpy. How to use the decoupling deliberately: | Setting | Typical choice | Why ||---|---|---|| First layer | wider than default | more contact patch, better adhesion, more tolerance of bed variation || External perimeter | at or slightly below nozzle diameter | narrower roads follow corners more tightly and give better dimensional control on the visible surface || Internal perimeters | above nozzle diameter (often 110–120 %) | fewer, fatter roads for the same wall thickness — faster and fewer internal interfaces || Solid infill | above nozzle diameter | fewer passes to fill an area || Sparse infill | wider still | infill is invisible; width buys speed directly || Support interface | narrower | finer surface where the part rests on it | > Common pitfall: setting an extrusion width and forgetting that flow calibration (post 01) targets a specific width. Change your external perimeter width and your measured wall thickness changes, which looks like a flow error and is not. Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill Perimeter count Wall thickness ≈ perimeter count × extrusion width. Two perimeters at 0.45 mm gives a 0.9 mm wall; four gives 1.8 mm. Perimeter count is usually a better investment than infill density for stiffness and strength, and the reason is elementary beam mechanics: in bending, material's contribution to stiffness scales with the square of its distance from the neutral axis. The walls are the material furthest from the neutral axis. Infill in the middle of the section contributes comparatively little. Going from two walls to four on a bracket typically does more than doubling infill, and costs less time and material. Typical practice: 2 walls for decorative parts, 3–4 for general functional parts, 5+ for parts under real load or that need to be watertight or tapped. Perimeter order Two orders, both defensible: Inner first, external last (the usual default). The external perimeter is laid against already-solid inner walls, so it has something to lean on, and nothing is printed afterwards to disturb it. Generally better surface quality and better behaviour on overhanging walls. External first. The outer wall is laid before any inner wall can push it outward, which can improve dimensional accuracy on some machines. Costs surface quality and overhang reliability. Slicers in the PrusaSlicer lineage expose this as an "external perimeters first" toggle, and some offer a hybrid ordering. The honest guidance is that the effect size is machine-dependent: print a test part both ways, measure it with calipers and look at it, and decide for your machine. Post 05 returns to the accuracy side of this. Seams Every closed loop must start and stop somewhere. At that point the extruder transitions from zero pressure to steady pressure and back, and the result is a small defect — a blob, a notch, or both — stacked on every layer. Seam placement strategies: | Strategy | Result ||---|---|| Nearest | seam goes wherever travel is shortest; fastest, scattered, unpredictable || Aligned | seams stack into a single vertical line; ugly but predictable, and you can rotate it to the back || Rear | forced to the back of the plate || Random | scattered; hides the line at the cost of scattering small defects across the whole surface || Painted | you choose, per region, in the slicer — put it inside a corner where geometry hides it | Mechanically, a stack of aligned seams is a line of small stress concentrations and reduced wall continuity, so on a pressure-bearing or load-bearing part, do not let the seam line up with the region of highest stress. Reducing the seam artefact itself: tune pressure advance (post 01), and use the slicer's wipe and coasting options, which stop extruding slightly before the end of the loop and move the nozzle to smear the remainder. Some slicers additionally offer a scarf or ramped seam that spreads the transition over a Z ramp instead of a single point; where available, it is the most effective option for a visible cylindrical surface. Gap fill When a region is too narrow for another whole perimeter but too wide to ignore, classic perimeter generation leaves a gap and fills it with a short, thin extrusion. Gap fill is a common source of noise, vibration, blobs and stuttering motion, because it is many tiny moves with rapid flow changes. The modern answer is variable-width extrusion (Chapter 6), which mostly eliminates the problem by widening the perimeters themselves to close the gap. Chapter 5 — Skins and infill The thickness-not-count rule Solid layers are specified in most slicers as a count, which is a trap. What matters physically is total thickness of solid material. Halving your layer height silently halves your top skin. The result is pillowing: the top surface sags into the infill voids and shows holes and bumps. The rule: specify a thickness, then derive the count. Around 0.8–1.2 mm of top solid is a sensible general target; more if infill density is low, because thin skin has to bridge longer unsupported spans. Modern slicers have a "minimum shell thickness" setting that enforces this automatically — turn it on. Bottom skins can be thinner, because the build plate supports them. Monotonic ordering for the top solid layer is worth enabling where available: it forces solid infill lines to be laid in a consistent direction so each new road overlaps the previous one the same way, producing a visually uniform surface instead of a patchwork. Infill patterns, honestly First, what is reliably measurable at hobby level and what is not. Measurable, repeatable, and worth optimising: estimated and actual print time, material mass (weigh the part), whether the pattern self-intersects within a layer (which makes the nozzle run over its own deposits, adding noise and artefacts), and whether the pattern is 2D (identical every layer) or 3D (varies with Z, giving out-of-plane structure). Not reliably measurable without a test rig, standardised coupons and repeated samples: the small percentage strength differences between patterns that appear in blog comparisons. These figures are usually a single specimen, in a single load direction, on a single machine, and they do not generalise. Treat them as anecdote. | Pattern | Structure | Print speed | Material at equal density | Honest strength note ||---|---|---|---|---|| Rectilinear / lines | 2D, alternating direction per layer | fastest | lowest | no intersections within a layer; layers only touch at crossings, so poor out-of-plane contribution || Grid | 2D, both directions in the same layer | fast | low | nozzle crosses its own deposits at every intersection — can cause knocking and artefacts at high density || Triangles | 2D, three directions | moderate | moderate | good in-plane isotropy; more material at nodes || Honeycomb | 2D hexagonal | slow — many direction changes | moderate | high in-plane stiffness for its mass, paid for in time || Cubic / adaptive cubic | 3D, stacked cubes on a corner | moderate | moderate; adaptive saves a lot | contributes in all three axes; adaptive subdivides only near surfaces || Gyroid | 3D, smooth continuous non-intersecting surface | moderate | moderate | near-isotropic by construction; smooth continuous motion; the common default for functional parts, and good for flexibles because it compresses evenly || Lightning | tree-like, grows only where a top surface needs support | very fast | very low | contributes essentially nothing structurally — that is the point. For decorative parts and hollow bodies || Concentric | follows the contour | moderate | moderate | used for flexible parts, where following the wall keeps the part uniformly compliant | Density Strength does not scale linearly with infill density; the returns diminish, and the time cost does not. The efficient move is almost always: add walls before you add infill, for the second-moment-of-area reason in Chapter 4. Typical practice: - Decorative: 5–15 %, or lightning.- General functional: 15–25 % with 3–4 walls.- Loaded parts: 30–50 % with 5+ walls — and read post 07 before assuming that is enough.- 100 %: rarely correct. Over-packing solid infill causes over-extrusion, dimensional growth and worse surfaces. If you need a solid part, increase walls until they meet in the middle. Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features Per-feature speeds Slicers set a different speed for external perimeters, internal perimeters, solid infill, sparse infill, top solid, bridges, gap fill and travel. The important asymmetry: Outer-wall speed dominates surface quality, and costs very little total time. The external perimeter is the only extrusion anyone sees; it is where ringing lands, where pressure-advance error is visible, and where corner rounding shows. It is also a small fraction of total extruded volume on most parts. Halving outer-wall speed typically adds a few percent to print time and produces a visibly better part. This is the best quality-per-minute trade in the whole slicer. Conversely, sparse infill speed is nearly free to raise — until it hits the volumetric flow ceiling you measured in post 01. Acceleration is the real print-time variable On small parts the toolhead rarely reaches its commanded speed at all. It accelerates over half its length and decelerates over the other half, so its peak speed is = = = 200 mm/s, and its average is lower still. Consequences: - Raising the speed setting on a part made of short segments changes nothing. Raising acceleration does.- Raising acceleration excites resonance, which is what input shaping (post 01) exists to manage. The two settings are coupled.- Jerk (Marlin's classic model), junction deviation and Klipper's square corner velocity all describe the same thing: how much speed is retained through a direction change. Raising it speeds up detailed parts and adds ringing.- Minimum layer time / "slow down for short layers" forces small cross-sections to take long enough to solidify. It is a cooling setting wearing a speed setting's clothes. Cooling Set from material, as measured in post 01. Briefly: PLA wants a lot, PETG a moderate amount, ABS/ASA very little in an enclosure, nylon and PC little. Cooling buys overhang and detail quality and pays in layer adhesion; that trade never goes away. The quality features Ironing. After the top solid layer is complete, the nozzle sweeps the surface again at very low flow, remelting and smoothing it. Produces a genuinely flat, near-glossy top face. Costs time proportional to top-surface area, and can push a bead of material to the edges if flow is too high. Only applies to flat, horizontal top surfaces. Adaptive layer height. Layer height varies with local surface slope — thin layers where the surface is shallow (where stepping is visible), thick where it is vertical (where stepping is invisible). Excellent on organic and curved shapes, useless on prisms, and it complicates any setting that is expressed per layer. Some slicers additionally let you paint a height range by hand. Fuzzy skin. The outer wall is deliberately jittered in XY by a small random amount, giving a matte, textured surface. It hides layer lines, hides seams, and improves grip on handles. Costs a little time and a little dimensional precision on the fuzzed surface — never fuzz a mating face. Better slicers let you paint it onto selected surfaces only. Variable-width extrusion (Arachne). The perimeter generator that, instead of laying fixed-width loops and dumping the remainder into gap fill, varies each loop's width to fill the available space exactly. Thin walls, tapering ribs, embossed text and small features all improve substantially. It originated in the Cura engine and became the default perimeter generator in PrusaSlicer from version 2.5; OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio, both of PrusaSlicer lineage, carry it too. The classic fixed-width generator remains selectable and is occasionally preferred when you want perfectly uniform road width for its own sake. Modifiers and per-region settings The most under-used capability in every modern slicer. You are not limited to one setting per part. - Per-object settings — different infill, walls or supports for each object on the plate.- Height ranges — different settings for a Z band, e.g. more solid layers around a threaded region.- Modifier meshes — drop a box or cylinder into the scene and any setting inside its volume is overridden. The canonical use is 100 % infill only inside a screw boss, or extra walls only around a bearing seat.- Negative volumes — subtract geometry at slice time without touching the CAD model. Useful for a quick clearance test.- Support blockers and enforcers — force supports on or off in a region rather than fighting the global threshold.- Painted seams and painted supports — direct manual control on the model surface. > Trick: a modifier mesh is the correct answer almost every time you are tempted to raise a global setting for the sake of one feature. Raising global infill from 20 % to 60 % because one boss needs to be solid costs you the whole part's print time; a modifier box costs you the boss. Brims, rafts and skirts | Feature | What it is | Use when ||---|---|---|| Skirt | one or more loops around the part, not touching it | always — it purges the nozzle and gives you a free look at the first layer before the part starts || Brim | a flat collar attached to the part's first layer | small footprints, tall thin parts, warp-prone materials, sharp corners that lift. 3D Printing Course — 01 · First Print and Calibration

Almost every dramatic 3D printing failure is a boring first-layer failure that was allowed to continue for six hours. Post 00 established what the machine does physically; this post makes it do it repeatably. The organising idea is that calibration is not a ritual you perform once and forget, but a short ordered sequence of measurements — each one produces a number you write down, and each one must be done before the next because the next depends on it. Copying settings from a forum post skips the measurement and therefore skips the calibration entirely. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration (this post) → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - State the first-layer doctrine and explain mechanically why the first layer determines most print outcomes.- Tram a bed, set a Z-offset by the paper method and by feeler gauge, and explain what mesh bed levelling does and does not fix.- Compare probe technologies (inductive, BLTouch-style, eddy current, strain gauge) by what each actually senses.- Choose a build surface and a cleaning regime, and explain why a fingerprint is an adhesion failure.- Read a first-layer test print and say precisely whether the nozzle is too high, too low, or correct.- Execute the calibration sequence in the correct order, taking a real measurement at each step: rotation distance/e-steps, temperature, flow, retraction, pressure advance, maximum volumetric flow, cooling.- Explain which settings belong to the printer and which belong to the spool.- Diagnose the common first-week failures from their visual signature. Chapter 1 — The first layer: doctrine, tramming and Z-offset Terminology - Tramming — physically adjusting the bed so its plane is parallel to the plane the nozzle travels in. Often loosely called "levelling", which is misleading: gravity is irrelevant, parallelism is everything.- Z-offset — the distance between the point the printer calls Z = 0 and the point where the nozzle actually touches the bed. A single scalar, and the most consequential number on the machine.- Nozzle-to-bed gap — the physical clearance while printing the first layer. Deliberately smaller than the first-layer height, so that material is squashed.- Squish — the intentional compression of the first layer. It is what converts a round extrudate into a flat road with a wide contact patch.- Mesh bed levelling — probing a grid of points and compensating for bed shape by modulating Z during the print.- Probe repeatability — the spread of readings when the probe measures the same point many times. The floor on how good your mesh can be.- Elephant foot — the outward bulge at the bottom of a part caused by excessive first-layer squish, excessive bed temperature, or both. Why the first layer decides everything Three separate mechanisms, all rooted in the same event: 1. Adhesion is contact area. A squashed road has a wide flat contact patch against the plate; an unsquashed one touches along a line. Adhesion force scales with that contact patch, so a nozzle 50 µm too high can halve it.2. The first layer is the datum for every layer above it. Layer 2 is deposited onto layer 1 at a fixed Z step. If layer 1 is thinner than commanded, layer 2 is deposited into empty space and the error propagates upward as under-extrusion that never recovers. If layer 1 is thicker than commanded, layer 2 collides with it.3. Warping starts at the corners of the first layer. As upper layers cool and contract, they pull inward on the part. That force is resisted only by the bond between first layer and plate. A weak first layer does not resist it, so the corner lifts, and once a corner lifts the nozzle starts hitting the raised edge. The operational rule: watch the first layer finish before you leave the room. Nothing else in this hobby has a better ratio of attention spent to failures avoided. Step 1: mechanical prerequisites, before any of this Levelling compensates for shape. It cannot compensate for movement. Before touching Z-offset: - Check that the bed does not rock. Press each corner; if it moves, springs are too loose or the mounts are worn.- Check belt tension (X and Y): a belt should feel taut, and plucking it should give a defined note rather than a dull thud. Loose belts show up later as ringing and dimensional error that no calibration will fix.- Check that the gantry is square and, on dual-Z machines, that both Z motors are synchronised — Marlin's and Klipper's / exist for exactly this.- Check the nozzle is clean and cold-pulled free of debris. A blob of burnt plastic on the nozzle tip makes every subsequent measurement meaningless.- Heat everything to printing temperature before probing. Beds change shape by a measurable amount between cold and 60 °C; probes drift with temperature. Probing cold and printing hot is a common self-inflicted wound. Step 2: tram the bed Even with automatic levelling, the bed should be physically parallel to within a few tenths of a millimetre. On a four-screw bed the classic procedure is manual and works: 1. Heat bed and nozzle to the temperatures you will print at.2. Home all axes, then disable the steppers' Z compensation ( on Marlin, or simply skip mesh loading).3. Move the nozzle over screw 1, drop to Z = 0, adjust the screw until a sheet of paper drags with light, consistent friction.4. Repeat for screws 2, 3, 4, then repeat the whole cycle at least twice — adjusting one corner moves the others.5. Check the centre. If the centre is markedly high or low relative to all four corners, the bed is dished or crowned; that is what mesh compensation is for. Some machines use screw-tilt-calibrate helpers ( in Klipper, in Marlin) that probe and tell you how far and which way to turn each screw. Use them if available — they replace a subjective feel with a number. Step 3: set the Z-offset Two methods, both legitimate: The paper method. A sheet of ordinary office paper is roughly 0.1 mm thick, which is conveniently close to a good first-layer gap. Lower Z in 0.02–0.05 mm steps until the paper can still be moved but drags noticeably. The weakness is that "drags noticeably" is subjective, that paper thickness varies by brand, and that the paper compresses under the nozzle. A feeler gauge. A 0.1 mm feeler gauge is a known, incompressible thickness. Same procedure, but the result is a number you can reproduce next month. If you intend to be systematic, buy a feeler gauge set. Live adjustment during the first layer. The most practical method on a machine that supports baby-stepping: start a large first-layer test, and adjust Z live while watching the extrusion until it looks correct (Chapter 3), then save. This is the method that converges fastest because it optimises the thing you actually care about — the printed result — rather than a proxy. > Trick: record your Z-offset in your notes with the build plate it belongs to. Every plate has a different thickness, and swapping a smooth PEI sheet for a textured one changes the correct offset by an amount you will otherwise rediscover by scraping the plate. Step 4: mesh bed levelling, and its limits A probe measures the bed at a grid of points and builds a height map. During printing, the firmware adds a small Z correction as a function of X and Y so the nozzle follows the bed's actual shape. Probe technologies differ in what they physically sense: | Probe type | Senses | Strengths | Limitations ||---|---|---|---|| Inductive (proximity sensor) | proximity of a conductive/ferrous target | no moving parts, fast, cheap | only works over a metal bed; readings drift with the sensor's own temperature; senses the metal plate, not the print surface on top of it || BLTouch / CRTouch style | mechanical contact of a deployed pin | works over any surface material, well understood | deploying mechanism is a moving part that eventually fails; has an X/Y offset from the nozzle, so it cannot probe the bed edges || Eddy current | distance to a conductive plate, continuously | very fast, can scan while moving, no deployment | needs temperature compensation and calibration against a known reference; conductive plate required || Strain gauge / load cell | the force when the nozzle itself touches the bed | zero X/Y offset because the probe point is the nozzle; senses the real print surface | sensitive to what is on the nozzle tip; needs a clean nozzle every time | Whatever the type, measure its repeatability: in Klipper, in Marlin. Probe the same point ten times and look at the standard deviation. If repeatability is worse than a few tens of micrometres, your mesh is noise and you should fix that before trusting it. What auto-levelling does not fix This list saves more time than anything else in the chapter. - It does not set your Z-offset. A mesh describes the bed's shape; the offset describes the gap. You still set the gap yourself.- It does not fix a tilted bed. Compensation is applied by moving Z during motion, which skews the part's geometry and, on a badly tilted bed, can exceed the compensation range or run the Z motor at absurd speeds. Tram first, compensate second.- It does not fix a bed that moves. If the bed rocks, the mesh you measured is not the bed you print on.- It does not fix a warped plate beyond mesh resolution. A sharp local dent between probe points is invisible to a 5×5 grid.- It does not fix a dirty plate. Grease under the first layer defeats any Z value.- It does not fix an unsquare gantry. If X sags in the middle, the correction changes as Z increases, and a mesh probed at Z ≈ 0 does not know that.- It does not persist automatically. Check whether your start G-code actually loads the mesh ( / ). A probed-but-not-loaded mesh is a common silent failure. Chapter 2 — Build surfaces and adhesion The surfaces | Surface | Adhesion mechanism | Grip | Release | Best for | Watch out ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| Smooth PEI (spring steel) | chemical/polar interaction with the hot polymer | strong | good once cool, by flexing the sheet | PLA, PETG with a release agent, glossy first-layer finish | PETG can bond hard enough to tear the PEI off; use a glue-stick release layer || Textured PEI (powder-coated) | mechanical keying into the texture | strong but distributed | excellent — parts often pop free as the bed cools | the general-purpose default; PLA, PETG, TPU, ABS/ASA | texture is imprinted into the part's bottom face; hides first-layer imperfections, which cuts both ways when diagnosing || Glass | mostly adhesive-assisted; very flat | weak bare, strong with glue/hairspray | excellent as glass cools | perfectly flat bottom faces | heavy, can chip; needs an adhesive for most materials || Garolite / FR4 | mechanical + surface chemistry | strong for nylon specifically | good | nylon (PA), which sticks to garolite and to little else | mediocre for PLA || Adhesive sheets (PET/BuildTak-style) | tack | strong | varies | budget option, replaceable | consumable; gouges easily | Cleaning: soap and water beats IPA Isopropyl alcohol is a fine solvent for the last print's release agent, but it is a poor degreaser for skin oils — it dissolves them and then, as it flashes off, redeposits a thin film across the whole plate. Washing the removable sheet in a sink with warm water and ordinary dish soap, then drying with a clean paper towel, physically carries the oil away. Practical regime: - Every few prints: wipe with IPA (99 % if you can get it; the water in 70 % dries slowly).- When adhesion drops, or every 10–20 prints: soap and water wash in the sink.- Never: aggressive solvents, abrasive scourers, or metal tools on PEI. Scrape stuck plastic with a plastic scraper, or reheat the bed and let the plastic release. Why fingerprints matter PEI's adhesion is chemical. Skin oil is a non-polar film sitting between the polymer and the plate — it is a release agent, and a very effective one. A single fingerprint in the middle of the plate produces a print that adheres everywhere except a palm-sized region, and lifts there. Handle the sheet by its edges. This is not fussiness; it is the most common cause of "my bed suddenly stopped working". Chapter 3 — Reading a first-layer test print Print a large single-layer patch — most slicers can do this, and dedicated first-layer test models are common. Look at it in raking light and, if in doubt, put a caliper on it. Too high (gap too large) - Individual extrusion lines are visibly separate, with valleys between them; you can see the plate through the gaps.- Each road is round-topped rather than flat-topped.- Poor adhesion: the patch can be lifted with a fingernail, or curls at the corners.- The surface feels ribbed.- Measured thickness is approximately the commanded first-layer height (no squish) or slightly less. Too low (gap too small) - Roads are pressed so wide they overlap, and excess material is pushed up as ridges between the lines.- Surface looks translucent, burnished or scratched; on textured plates the texture is smeared.- Audible scraping, and a visible line on the plate.- Extrusion becomes inconsistent because back-pressure is fighting the extruder — sometimes with under-extrusion appearing several centimetres later.- Downstream: pronounced elephant foot on real parts.- Measured thickness is noticeably below the commanded first-layer height. Correct - Adjacent roads have merged into a continuous sheet with no gaps and no raised ridges.- Line boundaries are faintly visible but the surface is flat.- The patch is uniformly matte (textured plate) or uniformly glossy (smooth plate) — uniformity is the signal, not the finish itself.- The patch peels off the cooled plate in one piece with moderate effort and shows a clean, evenly-textured underside.- Caliper reading is close to, and slightly under, the commanded first-layer height. > Common pitfall: judging the first layer at the centre of the plate only. Check all four corners and the centre. Uniformly wrong is a Z-offset problem; wrong in one region only is a tram/mesh problem. They have completely different fixes. Chapter 4 — The calibration sequence Do these in order. Each step's result depends on all the previous ones, so a step done out of order will have to be redone. 4.0 — Prerequisites Mechanical checks from Chapter 1, a trammed bed, a clean plate, dry filament, and a known-good vendor profile as the starting point. Do not begin with a profile you found on the internet. 4.1 — Extruder rotation distance / e-steps What it fixes: the machine delivering a different length of filament than it was asked for. Everything downstream is scaled by this error, so it is first. Procedure: 1. Heat the nozzle to printing temperature (the extruder must be able to actually push).2. Put the extruder in relative mode and disable any modifiers.3. Mark the filament at a known distance above the extruder inlet — 120 mm is convenient because it leaves 20 mm after a 100 mm extrusion.4. Command a slow 100 mm extrusion. Slow matters: fast extrusion can skip or grind and will corrupt the measurement.5. Measure the remaining distance from the inlet to the mark with calipers. Actual extruded = 120 − remaining.6. Repeat three times and average. Applying the result: Note the inversion: e-steps are steps per millimetre, rotation distance is millimetres per rotation, so the correction goes the opposite way. Getting this backwards doubles your error, which is an obvious enough result that you will notice. Acceptance: repeat the test after applying. Actual extruded should land within roughly a millimetre of 100 mm. If it does not converge, the drive gear is slipping, the tension is wrong, or the filament path has a restriction. Scope: this is a per-printer value. It does not change with filament brand. 4.2 — Temperature tower What it fixes: picking a nozzle temperature by evidence rather than by the number printed on the spool. Procedure: print a tower whose temperature drops (or rises) by 5 °C per block, using either a pre-built model with embedded temperature changes or a slicer height-triggered custom G-code ( at the layer where each block starts). What you evaluate on each block, and what each tells you: | Observation | Meaning ||---|---|| Layer separation when you try to snap the block | too cold — insufficient interdiffusion (post 00, Chapter 3) || Stringing between features | too hot, or a retraction problem (isolate later, not now) || Blobbing, drooping overhangs, glossy over-melted surface | too hot || Poor bridging, curled bridge strands | too cold, or too little cooling || Rough, matte, under-fused surface | too cold | Decision rule: do not just pick the prettiest block. Pick for the property the part needs. Functional part → the hottest temperature that does not string, droop or degrade, because layer bonding improves with temperature. Visual part → the coolest temperature that still fuses cleanly. Write the choice and the reason down. Scope: per material, per spool. Pigment loading alone shifts the optimum; a black and a white PLA from the same manufacturer regularly want different temperatures. 4.3 — Flow / extrusion multiplier What it fixes: the difference between the volume the slicer thinks it deposited and the volume actually deposited. Causes include filament diameter variation, nozzle wear, and material-specific die swell. Procedure (single-wall cube): 1. Slice a simple cube (or the standard single-wall calibration cube) with one perimeter, zero top layers, zero infill, and no gap fill. Vase mode also works.2. Note the extrusion width the slicer used for that wall — this is the target, and it is not the nozzle diameter (post 02 explains why).3. Print it at your chosen temperature.4. Measure the wall thickness with calipers at eight or more places — different heights, different faces, avoiding the seam and the corners. Use light, consistent pressure; plastic deforms under a caliper jaw.5. Average the measurements. Applying the result: Acceptance: reprint and remeasure. The spread between your individual measurements tells you how much precision you actually have — if measurements vary by more than a couple of hundredths of a millimetre, your measurement technique or the machine is the limiting factor, not the flow number. Modern slicers ship guided flow calibrations (OrcaSlicer's two-pass flow-rate test, for example) that use a printed surface-quality judgement instead of a caliper. They are faster and less prone to caliper-pressure error; the single-wall method remains the one that produces a number you can defend. Scope: per spool. This is the setting most worth re-checking on every new roll. 4.4 — Retraction What it fixes: oozing during travel moves, which shows as stringing, blobs and zits. Procedure: print a retraction tower or a two-pillar stringing test, stepping one variable per tower. Tune in this order: distance, then speed, then extra restart distance / wipe / Z-hop if still needed. Typical starting ranges — these depend on melt-zone volume, extruder architecture and Bowden tube length, so treat them as where to begin the tower, not as answers: | Setup | Retraction distance, typical starting range ||---|---|| Direct drive | roughly 0.5–2 mm || Bowden | roughly 3–7 mm, longer with a longer tube | Acceptance: the shortest retraction distance that eliminates strings. Over-retraction is not free: it pulls molten material back into the cold zone where it can jam, grinds a groove into the filament on repeated retractions at the same point, and causes gaps at the start of each extrusion. > Watch out: stringing has three plausible causes — too little retraction, too high a temperature, and wet filament. Wet filament strings and pops audibly and will defeat every retraction value you try. If you hear crackling from the nozzle, dry the filament before tuning anything. Scope: per material. 4.5 — Pressure advance / linear advance What it fixes: the lag between commanded flow and actual flow caused by pressure building and releasing in the melt zone. Symptoms are bulges after a deceleration and thin, starved lines after an acceleration — most visible at corners and at the start of each line. Klipper calls it pressure advance; Marlin calls it linear advance with a K factor. Same physics, different scaling, so the numbers are not interchangeable. Procedure (Klipper): use the tuning-tower mechanism, which ramps the parameter continuously with height: Print the standard tuning tower object, then find the height where corners are sharpest and line width is most uniform through the corner. The value is — with the factor above, a good band at 8 mm means a pressure advance of 0.04. Procedure (Marlin): use the K-factor test pattern (Marlin publishes a generator) and set with . Read the pattern for the K where the fast-to-slow transition shows no bulge and the slow-to-fast transition shows no gap. Reported typical values span a wide range and depend strongly on architecture — direct-drive setups sit far lower than Bowden ones, because Bowden systems store much more compliance. Do not carry a number across from a different machine; run the tower. Scope: per material (and re-check if you change hotend or extruder). 4.6 — Maximum volumetric flow What it fixes: the ceiling on print speed. Above it, the hotend cannot melt filament fast enough and the print silently under-extrudes. Procedure: print a flow-ramp test that increases volumetric flow with height — OrcaSlicer's "Max Volumetric Speed" test does this directly, and equivalent speed towers exist for other slicers. A typical sweep starts around 5 mm³/s and climbs in small steps. Reading it: find the height where the surface changes character — extrusion becomes visibly inconsistent, the finish shifts from even to rough or unevenly glossy, or the wall thins. That height's flow is the point of failure, not the working limit. Applying the result: set your profile's maximum volumetric speed 10–20 % below the failure point. The test is a best case: it does not include retractions, sharp corners, small layers or an already-hot enclosure. Dependency: this number moves with nozzle temperature. If you later raise the temperature, the ceiling rises too — and if you re-run 4.2, re-run this. Scope: per material, per hotend, per nozzle. 4.7 — Cooling What it fixes: overhang quality, bridging, small-feature definition — traded directly against layer adhesion (post 00, Chapter 3). Procedure: print an overhang/bridging test at several fan settings, or a small tower where fan speed steps with height. Also set minimum layer time (or "slow down for short layers"), which forces small cross-sections to print slowly enough to solidify. Starting points by material, all subject to measurement: | Material | Part cooling, typical starting point | Reason ||---|---|---|| PLA | high, often near maximum | crystallises quickly; benefits from fast solidification; layer adhesion stays adequate || PETG | moderate | needs some cooling for overhangs, but too much visibly weakens layer bonding || ABS / ASA | minimal or none, in an enclosure | high shrinkage; rapid cooling causes warping and delamination || TPU | low to moderate | slow-flowing; cooling helps definition but the material is tolerant || Nylon (PA) | low | strong, hygroscopic, and very sensitive to rapid cooling || PC | low | high glass transition; needs a warm environment to bond | > Trick: cooling is the one setting where "more is better" is actively wrong for functional parts. If a bracket keeps snapping along a layer line, drop the fan before you change anything else. Scope: per material. Chapter 5 — Discipline: per spool, and one variable at a time Why calibration is per spool, not per printer Split your settings into two buckets: | Belongs to the printer | Belongs to the spool / material ||---|---|| e-steps / rotation distance | nozzle temperature || XY and Z steps per mm | bed temperature || input shaper frequencies | flow / extrusion multiplier || bed mesh | retraction distance and speed || probe offsets | pressure advance || kinematic limits (max accel, jerk/square corner velocity) | maximum volumetric flow || — | cooling and minimum layer time | The right-hand column moves because filament varies. Diameter tolerance differs between manufacturers and between batches. Pigments and fillers change melt viscosity, thermal conductivity and optimum temperature. Moisture content changes everything at once. Even two spools of nominally identical material from one vendor can want a different flow number. Practically: create a slicer filament profile per spool type you buy, not per material category, and put the measured numbers in it. The tuning-order rule Never chase two variables at once. If you change temperature and flow together and the result improves, you have learned nothing you can reuse. The sequence in Chapter 4 is ordered by dependency: rotation distance scales all extrusion, temperature changes flow behaviour and the flow ceiling, flow changes how corners look, pressure advance is judged on corner appearance, and cooling changes both bonding and overhang quality. Keep a notebook — a text file is fine. For each change record: date, printer, spool, the setting changed, the old value, the new value, the test used, and the observation. Six months later this file is the only thing that distinguishes "my printer got worse" from "I changed something in March". Desktop 3D printing looks like a solved problem from the outside: load a file, press print, collect a part. Chapter 1 — Additive versus subtractive Terminology - Additive manufacturing (AM) — building a part by adding material, usually in layers, from a digital model. The formal ISO/ASTM term; "3D printing" is the colloquial synonym.- Subtractive manufacturing — starting from a solid blank and removing material: milling, turning, drilling, grinding, laser and waterjet cutting.- Formative manufacturing — forcing material into a shape without adding or removing it: injection moulding, casting, forging, sheet bending.- Layer — one planar slice of the part, deposited or cured in a single pass. The layer is the fundamental quantum of almost all AM processes.- Anisotropy — direction-dependent material properties. A part that is strong along X and weak along Z is anisotropic.- Build volume — the maximum envelope the machine can print inside, usually quoted as X × Y × Z in millimetres. What additive is genuinely good at The honest list is shorter than the marketing list: 1. Geometric complexity is free. A lattice, an internal channel that curves, a topology-optimised bracket — all cost the same per gram as a plain block. On a mill, every feature costs a setup, a tool and a toolpath.2. Quantity one is cheap. There is no tooling. The marginal cost of the first part equals the marginal cost of the thousandth, which is exactly the property injection moulding does not have.3. Iteration latency is hours, not weeks. The design loop closes the same day. For fixtures, jigs and prototypes this is the entire value proposition.4. Internal voids and enclosed geometry. A hollow shell with internal ribs is trivial additively and often impossible subtractively without splitting the part.5. On-demand spares. A discontinued knob or bracket becomes a file rather than a supply-chain problem. What subtractive is still better at 1. Tolerance and surface finish out of the machine. A milled feature can hold tolerances an order of magnitude tighter than a hobby FDM part, with a surface that needs no post-processing.2. Isotropic material properties. Stock aluminium or acetal has the same strength in every direction because it was never built in layers.3. Certified material provenance. You can buy a billet with a mill certificate. Filament data sheets are far weaker evidence.4. Volume economics. Above a few hundred identical parts, moulding or machining wins on cost per part, usually decisively.5. Flatness, roundness and bore quality. A reamed hole is round. A printed hole is a polygon approximation with a seam in it — post 03 and post 05 spend real time on this. | Criterion | Additive (desktop) | Subtractive (CNC) | Formative (injection moulding) ||---|---|---|---|| Tooling cost | none | fixturing only | high, dominates low volumes || Cost at quantity 1 | lowest | moderate | prohibitive || Cost at quantity 10 000 | worst | moderate | lowest || Geometric complexity | nearly free | expensive | limited by draft and ejection || Internal channels | easy | hard or impossible | needs cores/inserts || Achievable tolerance | loosest | tightest | tight and repeatable || Material isotropy | poor (FDM) to good (SLS) | excellent | excellent || Lead time for a change | hours | days | weeks (retool) | > Trick: the question is never "can I print this?" but "is printing the cheapest path to a correct part?" Chapter 2 — The process families Four families cover essentially everything a reader of this course will encounter. Only the first two are realistically owned at home; the third is bought as a service; the fourth is named for completeness. FDM / FFF — fused deposition modelling A thermoplastic filament is pushed through a heated nozzle and deposited as a road of molten polymer that fuses to its neighbours. "FDM" was a Stratasys trademark; "FFF" (fused filament fabrication) is the vendor-neutral name for the identical process. This is the overwhelming majority of desktop machines and the default assumption of this course unless stated otherwise. Resin — SLA and MSLA A photopolymer resin is cured layer by layer with ultraviolet light. SLA steers a laser spot across the surface; MSLA (masked stereolithography) shines a UV LED array through an LCD panel that masks the whole layer image at once, so exposure time is independent of how much area is being cured. Every affordable desktop resin printer today is MSLA. SLS — selective laser sintering A laser fuses regions of a bed of polymer powder (usually nylon PA12). Unfused powder supports the part, so overhangs and captive geometry need no support structures. Industrial and service-bureau territory; a handful of "benchtop" SLS machines exist but the powder handling is a serious commitment. Metal processes — one line Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF/SLM/DMLS), binder jetting and directed energy deposition all exist and all produce genuinely useful metal parts, but they are capital equipment with inert-gas, powder-safety and post-machining requirements that put them entirely outside the hobby envelope — buy metal parts as a service, do not plan to own the machine. Resolution is the smallest step the machine can command. Accuracy is how close the resulting dimension is to the model. They are unrelated, and post 05 is entirely about the second one. Chapter 3 — The physics of FDM Terminology - Melt zone — the region inside the hotend where the filament is above its melting or softening point and behaves as a viscous fluid.- Heatbreak — the deliberately thin-walled, low-conductivity section between the heatsink and the heater block that keeps the melt zone short and well defined.- Heat creep — failure mode where the melt zone migrates upward past the heatbreak, softening filament in the cold section and jamming it.- Extrudate — the material that has left the nozzle.- Road / extrusion — one deposited line of extrudate. Its cross-section is roughly a rectangle with rounded ends, width × layer height.- Weld / interface — the boundary between two roads, either side by side within a layer or stacked between layers.- Volumetric flow — the rate at which the hotend can melt and deliver material, in mm³/s. The real speed limit of the machine. The filament is a plunger The extruder gears grip the filament and push it into the hotend. Solid filament above the melt zone acts as a piston; the molten polymer below it is the working fluid. This is a positive-displacement pump, and the consequences are worth stating explicitly: - Flow is commanded by distance, not by pressure. The firmware asks for "extrude 2.31 mm of filament", and the volume delivered is that length times the filament cross-section. If the filament diameter is not what the slicer assumed, every extrusion in the file is wrong by the same ratio.- The system is compliant. Molten polymer compresses slightly, the filament itself buckles slightly, the PTFE liner or Bowden tube stretches. Pressure therefore lags the commanded flow — which is exactly the problem that pressure advance / linear advance exists to correct (post 01).- The grip is the weak link. If the drive gear cannot push hard enough, it grinds a flat into the filament and stops gripping. Under-extrusion that gets progressively worse over minutes is nearly always this. Melting, and why volumetric flow is the real limit Heat has to travel from the heater block into the filament core by conduction, and polymers are poor thermal conductors. The maximum flow the hotend supports is set by how much filament it can bring to temperature per second — which depends on melt-zone length, nozzle temperature, and the material's own thermal properties. This is why the same hotend will happily run PLA far faster than PETG, and why raising nozzle temperature raises attainable flow. Practically: your printer does not have a "maximum speed" in mm/s. It has a maximum volumetric flow in mm³/s, and speed follows from it. If that number exceeds what the hotend can melt, the print under-extrudes no matter what the slicer says. Post 01 gives the measurement procedure for finding your own machine's ceiling; do not trust a headline figure from a product page, because it is measured with a specific material at a specific temperature that is probably not yours. Layer bonding is polymer chain diffusion This is the single most important piece of physics in the course. When a hot road is laid onto a cooler road below it, the two do not glue together and they do not melt into a homogeneous mass. What happens is interdiffusion: polymer chains near the interface, if they are above the glass transition and mobile enough, wriggle across the boundary by reptation — snake-like motion along the tube formed by neighbouring chains — and entangle with chains on the other side. The strength of the weld is a function of how many chains crossed and how far, which is a function of interface temperature and time above that temperature. The classic treatment is Wool and O'Connor's crack-healing theory, and it maps onto FDM almost directly. The engineering consequences follow immediately: - Hotter nozzle → stronger layer bond, up to the point where the material degrades or the part slumps. This is a real, repeatable lever.- More cooling → weaker layer bond. Part cooling fans buy overhang quality and surface detail and pay for it in Z strength. Post 04 and post 07 return to this trade.- Smaller layer height → more interfaces per millimetre, but each interface is also smaller and the part reheats more often. The net effect on strength is material-dependent and genuinely contested; do not repeat a blanket claim in either direction.- An enclosure raises ambient temperature, which slows cooling at the interface and improves bonding. This is most of why ABS, ASA and PC want an enclosure.- Long print times per layer let the previous layer cool further. A tall thin part and a tall wide part with identical settings do not have identical layer adhesion. Anisotropy, stated properly A load applied along the roads is carried by polymer chains that run continuously through the extrudate — essentially the bulk strength of the material, minus voids. A load applied across the layers is carried only by the chains that diffused across each weld interface, plus whatever mechanical interlock the road geometry provides. The Z direction is therefore always weaker, and the failure mode is different too: in-plane failure tends to be ductile yielding, while interlayer failure tends to be a clean, brittle-looking separation along a layer line. How much weaker? Published figures for Z-direction tensile strength as a fraction of in-plane strength scatter enormously — across materials, nozzle temperatures, layer heights, cooling settings, enclosure conditions and test-coupon geometry. Treat any single percentage you read as a statement about that study's conditions, not about your printer. What is robust, and what you should carry forward, is the ordering: in-plane strong, interlayer weak, and the gap is large enough to change the design. > Common pitfall: printing a hook, a lever or a bracket flat on the bed because it "fits better that way", so that the working load pulls the layers apart. This is the most common structural failure in hobby printing and it is entirely an orientation decision. Post 03 works the example through in detail. Chapter 4 — Printer anatomy The extruder: direct drive versus Bowden The extruder is the geared drive that grips and pushes the filament. Two architectures: Direct drive puts the extruder motor on the moving toolhead, immediately above the hotend. The filament path from gear to melt zone is short — typically tens of millimetres. Bowden puts the motor on the frame and pushes filament through a PTFE tube, often several hundred millimetres, to a hotend that carries no motor. | | Direct drive | Bowden ||---|---|---|| Toolhead mass | higher | lower || Achievable acceleration before ringing | lower | higher || Retraction distance needed | short (roughly a couple of millimetres) | long (often several times more) || Responsiveness to flow changes | good | sluggish; more pressure-advance compensation needed || Flexible filament (TPU) | workable | difficult to painful || Maintenance access | motor in the hot, moving area | motor accessible, tube is a wear item | Neither is "better". Direct drive trades acceleration for control; Bowden trades control for acceleration. Modern direct-drive extruders have got light enough that the historical acceleration argument is much weaker than it was. The hotend Stacked from cold to hot: heatsink (with a fan that must never be switched off while hot), heatbreak, heater block carrying a cartridge heater and a thermistor, and the nozzle. The heatbreak's whole job is to make the transition from cold to molten as short as possible. A long, sloppy transition zone means a long column of soft filament that behaves like a spring, degrading extrusion control, and it invites heat creep. Two hotend classes matter: - PTFE-lined: a PTFE tube runs all the way down to the nozzle. Cheap, forgiving, excellent for PLA and PETG. The ceiling is the PTFE itself, which begins to degrade at elevated temperature and releases decomposition products; the widely used practical limit is around 240–250 °C, and going above it is both a part-quality and an air-quality problem.- All-metal: no PTFE in the hot zone, so high-temperature materials become possible. In exchange the melt zone is less well constrained and heat creep is a more real risk, particularly with materials that soften gradually. The nozzle - Diameter sets the minimum feature width and, with layer height, the volumetric flow available. 0.4 mm is the default for good reasons: it balances detail against speed. 0.2 mm buys detail and costs enormous print time; 0.6 and 0.8 mm buy speed and strength on functional parts and cost fine detail.- Material matters as soon as you print composites. Brass conducts heat well and is soft; any filament loaded with carbon fibre, glass fibre, metal powder or glow-in-the-dark phosphor is abrasive and will wear a brass nozzle out of round in hours. Hardened steel, tool steel or a carbide/ruby-tipped nozzle is required for those, at a small cost in thermal conductivity.- Wear is invisible until it is not. A worn nozzle prints wider than commanded and loses corner definition. If dimensional behaviour drifts over months, measure the nozzle orifice or simply replace it — nozzles are consumables. The bed and adhesion surfaces The bed must be flat, at a controlled temperature, and chemically willing to hold the first layer and then let it go. Common surfaces, covered properly in post 01: - Smooth PEI — strong, chemically-driven adhesion; glossy first-layer finish; can hold some materials (PETG especially) hard enough to damage the sheet without a release agent.- Textured PEI — mechanical keying rather than pure chemical grip; matte first layer that hides imperfections; releases much more willingly as the bed cools. The forgiving default.- Glass — extremely flat, usually needs an adhesive (glue stick, hairspray); prints release as the glass cools.- Garolite / FR4 — the traditional answer for nylon, which sticks to it well and to most other things poorly. Removable spring-steel sheets on a magnetic base have become the norm because flexing the sheet is the single most reliable release mechanism. Motion systems | System | How it moves | Consequences ||---|---|---|| Bedslinger (Cartesian i3) | bed moves in Y, gantry carries X, Z lifts the gantry | simple, cheap, easy to service. Moving mass in Y includes the part, so ringing and accuracy get worse as the print gets taller and heavier. Tall thin parts are its weakness. || CoreXY | two frame-mounted motors drive both X and Y through a crossed belt path; the bed only moves in Z | constant, low moving mass regardless of part size; supports much higher acceleration; the natural choice for fast machines. Belt path is longer and belt tension/squareness matter more. || Delta | three vertical towers with arms meeting at an effector | very low moving mass, very high speed potential, tall cylindrical build volume. Position is a trigonometric function of three carriages, so calibration is fussy and accuracy varies across the bed. | Ringing (also called ghosting) is the ripple you see downstream of a sharp corner: the toolhead's acceleration step excites a mechanical resonance in the frame and gantry, and the vibration is printed into the surface. It is a function of moving mass, frame stiffness, belt tension and acceleration. The modern fix is input shaping — the firmware pre-shapes the motion command to cancel the dominant resonance, with the resonant frequency measured using an accelerometer. Klipper implements this as ; Marlin has its own input-shaping implementation in recent versions. Either way it is a measurement, not a preset: the frequency belongs to your machine. Chapter 5 — The resin process, and its safety requirements How MSLA actually works 1. The build plate descends into a vat of liquid photopolymer until the gap between plate (or the last cured layer) and the transparent film at the bottom equals one layer height.2. A UV LED array beneath the vat illuminates an LCD panel. The panel is driven as a mask: pixels for the current cross-section are transparent, everything else is opaque.3. The resin in the illuminated gap crosslinks and cures for the programmed exposure time. Because the whole layer is exposed at once, exposure time is independent of layer area — a full-plate layer takes the same time as a single tiny part.4. The plate lifts, peeling the cured layer off the film, then returns for the next layer. Cure chemistry is free-radical or cationic photopolymerisation initiated by UV, on desktop machines typically around the 405 nm range. The correct exposure time is a property of the resin, the panel and the layer height together, so it must be found by test — printed exposure-test models are the standard method, and every resin change means a new test. Lift and peel forces — the real difficulty The cured layer is stuck to two things: the previous layer (which is what you want) and the release film at the bottom of the vat (which you do not). Separating it requires overcoming adhesion and the suction created as resin has to flow in behind the retreating part. Peel force scales with the cross-sectional area being separated, which is why: - Large flat cross-sections parallel to the plate are the worst case, and are why parts are usually tilted rather than laid flat.- Hollowing a model with adequate drain holes dramatically reduces both peel force and resin consumption. Without drain holes, a hollow shell becomes a suction cup and can pull itself off the plate or deform.- Slow lift speed, a lift height that clears the part, and a light-off delay (a pause before exposure that lets resin flow back into the gap) all buy reliability at the cost of print time.- Failures show up as layers torn off and left stuck to the film, a part detached from the supports, or a "layer shift" that is really a partial delamination. Bottom layers get a much longer exposure to key the part to the plate. Over-exposing them causes elephant foot in exactly the same way that a too-low first layer does on FDM. Post-processing is part of the process, not an afterthought Drain the part, wash it in isopropyl alcohol (or water for water-washable resins) to remove uncured surface resin, dry it, then post-cure it under UV. Post-cure is not cosmetic — mechanical properties and, to a small degree, dimensions change between the green state and the fully cured state. Over-curing makes many resins more brittle. Safety — the non-negotiable part Uncured photopolymer resin is a skin and respiratory irritant, and formulations commonly contain sensitisers: substances where repeated exposure can trigger an allergic response that becomes progressively worse and can be effectively permanent. This is not a "be a bit careful" hazard; it is a "the third exposure can be much worse than the first" hazard, and it is the reason experienced resin users are strict about it. The working rules: - Nitrile gloves, always. Not latex — latex is not an adequate barrier for these chemistries. Change them when contaminated; do not touch door handles, phones or keyboards while gloved.- Eye protection when handling the vat, decanting resin, or working with a wash tank.- Ventilation. A dedicated ventilated space, extracted outdoors where possible. A closed cupboard in a bedroom is not ventilation. Enclosures with carbon filters reduce odour more reliably than they reduce hazard.- Skin contact → soap and warm water immediately. Do not wash resin off with IPA or any other solvent; solvents carry the resin into the skin rather than off it.- Read the SDS for the specific resin. Formulations differ substantially between vendors and even between product lines from one vendor, and the safety data sheet is the authoritative document.- Waste disposal. Never pour resin, resin-contaminated IPA or wash water down a drain. Cure liquid waste to a solid under UV or sunlight, then dispose of the solid according to local regulations. Contaminated IPA can be cured, the solids filtered out, and the IPA reused. Paper towels, gloves and failed prints are all contaminated waste until cured.- Keep it out of food areas and away from children and pets, permanently. > Watch out: the largest exposure risk with resin is not printing; it is the wash and cleanup step, where hands are near liquid resin and solvent for extended periods. Set that station up properly before the first print, not after the first mess. Chapter 6 — The pipeline: CAD → mesh → slicer → G-code → printer Every post in this series lives at one stage of this chain. Knowing which stage a problem belongs to is most of debugging. Stage by stage CAD holds exact geometry. A cylinder is a mathematical cylinder. Post 06 covers CAD for printing properly. Mesh export is lossy and irreversible. The solid is tessellated into triangles, and the tessellation tolerance you choose at export decides how faceted your curves are. Export too coarse and a 20 mm hole visibly becomes a polygon; export absurdly fine and you get a slow, enormous file for no benefit. - STL stores nothing but a bag of triangles. No units, no colour, no materials, no assembly structure, no metadata. The unit ambiguity is why models occasionally arrive at 1/25.4 scale.- 3MF is a modern zipped-XML format that carries units, transforms, colours, materials, multiple objects and slicer metadata, and is far less prone to invalid geometry. Prefer 3MF wherever both ends support it — which today is essentially all mainstream CAD and all mainstream slicers.- STEP is a true solid format; several slicers now import it directly and tessellate internally, which removes the export-tolerance decision from your hands but also removes a failure mode. Slicing turns the mesh into toolpaths and is the subject of post 02. This is where the overwhelming majority of your decisions actually live. G-code is a flat, human-readable list of machine commands. A one-line taste: Resin printers are the exception: they consume a stack of sliced images plus per-layer exposure and motion parameters, in vendor container formats (, , and friends) rather than G-code. Some FDM vendors also ship binary G-code containers to cut file size and parse time. Firmware parses the file, does look-ahead motion planning and acceleration limiting, runs the heater control loops and the safety interlocks. Post 01 gives an orientation to the firmware landscape. > Trick: when something is wrong, identify the stage before you change a setting. Wrong shape → CAD or mesh. Right shape, wrong toolpaths → slicer. Right toolpaths, wrong physical result → printer, material or calibration. Changing slicer settings to fix a mesh problem is the most common wasted afternoon in this hobby. Chapter 7 — Capabilities, limits, and how to buy a machine Can, reliably, with a tuned machine - Functional brackets, mounts, enclosures, spacers, adapters and cable management.- Jigs and fixtures — arguably the single highest-value application, because a fixture only has to be right once.- Replacement parts for things that are otherwise scrap: knobs, clips, feet, gears in low-load applications.- Prototypes for form, fit and ergonomics, same day.- Geometry that cannot be machined: internal channels, lattices, captive assemblies, print-in-place mechanisms.- Short-run production of tens of parts, where tooling would never pay for itself. Cannot, or not without much more work than expected - Hold a tolerance out of the box. Dimensional accuracy is achievable, but it is an outcome of calibration and compensation, not a default. Post 05.- Survive heat. PLA loses stiffness not far above room temperature and creeps under sustained load; a PLA part in a parked car or clamped under permanent tension is a slow failure. Material choice fixes this; post 04.- Be watertight or pressure-tight by default. Achievable with wall-count, material and sealing strategy, but never assume it.- Be food-safe. Layer crevices harbour bacteria, and neither the filament additives nor the brass nozzle are automatically food-contact rated. Treat "food safe" as an engineering claim requiring evidence.- Be optically clear. "Transparent" filaments give translucent parts with visible layer structure without significant post-processing.- Print large engineering-plastic parts without warping. ABS, ASA, PC and nylon shrink as they cool; large flat parts in these need an enclosure and good design, and sometimes still fail.- Beat injection moulding on cost at volume. At a few hundred identical parts, run the numbers again.- Run unattended without thought. Mains-powered heaters and a moving machine deserve a smoke alarm in the room, thermal-runaway protection enabled in firmware, and a hard look at anything you are tempted to leave running overnight. Buying criteria, not model names Specific model recommendations date within months. Criteria do not. Score candidates against these: 1. Build volume you actually need. Most parts most people print fit in a 200 mm cube. Paying for a larger machine you use twice is a worse deal than paying for a better small one.2. Enclosure — required or not? If ABS, ASA, PC or nylon are on the roadmap, an enclosure is close to mandatory. If it is PLA and PETG only, an enclosure is a nice-to-have that mostly buys quiet.3. Hotend class and maximum temperature. All-metal and a genuine 280–300 °C capability keep engineering materials available. PTFE-lined caps you around 240–250 °C.4. Extruder architecture. Direct drive if TPU or fine retraction control matters; either is fine otherwise.5. Motion system. CoreXY if speed and tall parts matter; bedslinger if simplicity, serviceability and price matter.6. Bed levelling and probe type. Some form of automatic mesh probing is close to standard now; what varies is probe technology and whether the machine can also tram the bed physically. Post 01 covers what auto-levelling does and does not fix.7. Firmware and profile openness. Can you flash your own firmware? Are slicer profiles open and editable? Can you use a third-party slicer? A closed ecosystem is convenient right up to the day you need something it does not do.8. Consumables lock-in. Does it require proprietary filament, proprietary build plates, or a cloud account to print? Check whether local printing works with no network.9. Spare parts and documentation. Can you buy a nozzle, a hotend, a belt, a mainboard? Is there a wiring diagram? This is what separates a five-year machine from a two-year one.10. Community size. The practical value of a large user base is that your specific failure has already been diagnosed by someone, in public, with photos.11. Safety features. Thermal runaway protection, sane mains wiring for a heated bed, a thermistor that is mechanically captive.12. Total cost. Add spare nozzles, extra build plates, filament, a dry box, and — for resin — gloves, IPA, a wash/cure station and ventilation. Resin's sticker price is the smaller half of its cost. > Common pitfall: buying for the maximum-speed number in the marketing material. Maximum speed is quoted at a flow rate and a quality level nobody prints at. The number that predicts your experience is reliable volumetric flow with the material you actually use, and no vendor prints that on the box.
