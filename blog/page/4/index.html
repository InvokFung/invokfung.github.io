Billiards & Snooker — 03 · Tip and Power
Created2026-07-16|Sports Billiards
The aim decides where the object ball goes. The tip spot and the power decide what the white does. This lesson covers the five tip spots, the safe zone, keeping the cue level, five power levels, how the white slides and then rolls, and the straight-pot family: stop, follow, screw, stun run-through, long-range screw and the drag shot. > Series map. ★ Basics: 01 Cue action and elevation → 02 Aiming and potting → 03 Tip and power (this post) → 04 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles · ★★★ Advanced: 09 Escaping snookers → 10 Multi-ball shots → 11 Safety → 12 Position play · ★★★★ Hard: 13 Break-building → 14 Special shots · Reference: 15 Rules, tactics and practice. Learning Outcome - Know the five basic tip spots and keep the tip inside the safe zone.- Keep the cue level for low shots, and see why.- Pick a power from 1 to 5 by how far the white would roll.- Know where the white stops sliding and starts rolling, for each tip height.- On a straight pot: stop, follow, screw and stun run-through on purpose.- Screw from long range, and play a drag shot to arrive slowly from far away. Most pots need nothing else. 2 · Stay inside the safe zone Keep the tip inside the green circle, about half-way from the centre to the edge. Further out, the tip slides off the ball (a miscue) and the white goes anywhere. > Trick: chalk before every shot. Most miscues are a dry tip, not a bad spot. 3 · Low shots: keep the cue level To hit low, lower the whole cue (post 01, tip height) and keep it nearly flat, about 3° ✓. Raising the butt to reach the bottom of the white ✗ makes the tip strike down into the ball. > Watch out: a raised cue plus even a little side makes the white curve (swerve, post 14). On low shots that curve is a common, hidden cause of missed pots. Chapter 1 — The debugging loop Most wasted debugging time comes from changing things at random. The fix is a loop borrowed from the scientific method: 1. Observe. What exactly happened? Copy the full error text. Note the input, the time, the version.2. Hypothesize. Make one specific guess: "one order item has no key".3. Test one change. Design a check that proves or kills the guess. Change one thing only.4. Record. Write down what you tried and what happened. Then loop. A tiny log in a text file is enough. It stops you testing the same idea twice, and it becomes your bug report or commit message: > Watch out: if you change two things and the bug vanishes, you learn nothing. You do not know which change fixed it, or if one of them only hid it. Chapter 4 — The TLS 1.3 handshake TLS (Transport Layer Security) gives three things: confidentiality (only the two ends can read), integrity (changes on the way are detected), and authentication (you reached the real ). TLS 1.3 was defined in RFC 8446 (August 2018). In July 2026 the IETF published RFC 9846, which obsoletes RFC 8446. It calls itself a minor update that keeps the version number 1.3 and stays backward compatible. It also obsoletes RFC 5246, the TLS 1.2 spec. 1. ClientHello. The client guesses which key-exchange group the server accepts and sends a public key for it at once. This guess is what saves a round trip compared with TLS 1.2.2. ServerHello. The server sends its own public key. Both sides now run ECDHE (Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman, Ephemeral): each mixes its private key with the other's public key and gets the same shared secret. Someone watching the wire cannot compute it.3. Keys are derived from that secret. Everything after ServerHello is encrypted, including the certificate.4. CertificateVerify. The server signs a hash of all handshake messages so far with the private key of its certificate. This proves it owns the certificate, not just a copy.5. Finished. Each side sends a MAC over the whole transcript. If an attacker changed any earlier message, the MACs do not match and the connection dies.6. The client sends its HTTP request together with its Finished. Total: one round trip (1-RTT). The key pairs are ephemeral: new for each connection, then thrown away. So stealing the server's certificate key later does not decrypt recorded old sessions.
