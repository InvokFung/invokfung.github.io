Chapter 1 — What Python is (and isn't)

Terminology

- Interpreter — the program ( or ) that reads and executes Python code line by line.- REPL — Read-Eval-Print Loop: an interactive shell where each line you type is executed immediately.- Script — a  file containing Python code, run with .- Module — a  file imported by another file.- Package — a directory of modules with an .- PEP — Python Enhancement Proposal. The language's design documents. PEP 8 is the style guide everyone follows.

Python vs other languages (one paragraph)

Python is interpreted (no compile step), dynamically typed (you don't declare types, though you can hint them), and garbage-collected (no manual memory management). The trade-off vs C++/Rust: Python is 10–100× slower in raw computation but 5–10× faster to write. For data work, web back-ends, scripts, automation, and ML, the slowness almost never matters.

> Trick: don't compare languages by speed in isolation. Compare by time-to-correct-program. Python wins almost every "write something useful by Friday" race.

Chapter 1 — The full scale + double-stop arsenal

Three-octave scales: every key

By Grade 8 you must own all 12 major and all 12 minor (× 2 = harmonic + melodic) scales — three octaves each.

Practice rotation (4-week cycle)

| Week | Major keys | Minor keys ||------|------------|------------|| 1 | C, G, D, A, E | A, E, B, F♯, C♯ || 2 | B, F♯, C♯, D♭, A♭ | G♯, D♯, A♯, B♭, F || 3 | E♭, B♭, F | C, G, D || 4 | Review weakest 4 keys | Review weakest 4 keys |

15 minutes scales per day, rotating focus across the cycle.

Double stops at Grade 8

| Interval | Difficulty | Key example ||----------|------------|--------------|| 3rds | Moderate (Grade 6+) | G, D, A major || 6ths | Moderate | G, D, A major || Octaves | Hard | G, D major || 10ths | Very hard | Selected keys, single octave || Fingered octaves | Very hard | Heifetz scale system |

10ths require an enormous left-hand stretch (1st + 4th finger on adjacent strings, two octaves and a third apart). Most adult learners take 1–2 years to play 10ths reliably.

> Trick: don't force 10ths into pieces if they're not in the syllabus. Practise as a stretch exercise (open-palm yoga for the violin hand). When the syllabus demands them, you'll already have the reach.

Chapter 2 — Virtuoso bowing toolkit

By Grade 8 every technique is in active use. Quick reference:

| Bowing | Description | Typical use ||--------|-------------|--------------|| Détaché | Smooth separate strokes | Most Classical/Romantic passages || Legato / slur | Connected, one bow | Lyrical lines || Staccato | Sharp short, on string | Articulated passages || Slurred staccato | Multiple stops, one direction | Long lines with articulation || Spiccato | Bow lifts between notes | Light, dancing passages || Sautillé | Very fast spiccato | Fast 16th-note passages || Ricochet | Multiple bounces per drop | Showpieces, cadenzas || Martelé | Hammered, percussive | Accents, dramatic openings || Flying staccato | Lifts each note, one direction | Showpieces || Col legno battuto | Tap with the bow stick | Modern repertoire, Mars (Holst) || Col legno tratto | Draw with the wood | Eerie effects, rare || Sul ponticello | Near the bridge | Glassy colour || Sul tasto | Over the fingerboard | Soft, dreamy colour || Pizzicato | Plucked | Common; Bartók pizz snaps string against fingerboard || Jeté | "Thrown" — multiple notes from a single bounce-then-draw | Showpieces || Tremolo | Rapid bow oscillation on one note | Suspense, orchestral effect |

A piece using nearly all of them

Sarasate Zigeunerweisen uses détaché, legato, spiccato, sautillé, ricochet, harmonics, pizzicato, and flying staccato in one 9-minute work. Reference repertoire for technique synthesis.

Chapter 3 — Vibrato as expression

By Grade 8, vibrato is not "on" or "off" — it's a continuous parameter.

The four vibrato variables

1. Width — narrow (subtle warmth) vs wide (passionate Romantic).2. Speed — slow (sustained nobility) vs fast (intense, virtuoso fire).3. Continuous vs delayed — start the vibrato instantly, or let the note begin straight then add vibrato.4. Pulse — strict steady vibrato vs phrased vibrato (slows and speeds with the phrase).

Vibrato style per period

| Period | Vibrato preference ||--------|---------------------|| Baroque | Sparse, narrow, often as ornament (varies by historical performance practice) || Classical | Selective; on long notes mostly || Romantic | Continuous, wide, warm || 20th-c / Modern | Variable; sometimes deliberately senza vibrato || Folk / world | Narrow, fast, intermittent |

> Trick: listen to one violinist play the same piece from different periods (e.g., Hilary Hahn plays both Bach and Tchaikovsky). Notice how her vibrato choice changes. Internalise the period-appropriate sound by daily listening.

Chapter 6 — Performance polish

Programme planning (typical Grade 8 recital)

| Slot | Choice | Length ||------|--------|--------|| 1 | Baroque (Bach, Handel) | 5–8 min || 2 | Classical / early Romantic (Mozart, Beethoven) | 8–12 min || 3 | Late Romantic / Modern (Brahms, Ravel, Bartók) | 5–10 min || 4 | Virtuoso showpiece | 4–8 min |

Variety across periods is graded.

Stage presentation

- Tuning ritual: walk onstage, tune visibly, take a breath, begin.- Eye contact with pianist (if accompanied) before nodding to start.- Bow direction visible to audience.- Recovery: if you make a mistake, don't react. Continue. Audience usually misses small errors.

Stage anxiety management

- Daily mock performance for 30 days before exam. Play full programme top-to-bottom every day.- Performance-day routine: same warm-up, same meal timing, same clothing dress rehearsal.- Breathing: 4 seconds in, 6 seconds out, before walking onstage. Lowers heart rate.- Visualisation: mentally walk through the recital from arrival to bow, daily for 2 weeks before exam.

> Where I got stuck: I prepared technically but didn't rehearse the experience of performing. First exam: hands shook on the bow. Fix: 4 weeks of daily mock recitals — in front of family, friends, even an empty room with a recording running. By exam day, the room was less intimidating because I'd "performed" 30 times already.

Chapter 7 — Stylistic understanding

Grade 8 exams ask: Why is this Baroque ornament played this way? You should know:

Baroque (1600–1750)

- Trills start on upper note, on the beat.- Less vibrato; vibrato as occasional ornament.- Strong terraced dynamics (sudden f to p, not gradual).- Rubato sparingly; mostly steady tempo.- Détaché with subtle weight changes for phrase shape.

Classical (1750–1820)

- Balanced phrases, clear structure.- Vibrato on long notes only.- Dynamic gradients (cresc, dim) more frequent.- Détaché with elegance; spiccato light.

Romantic (1820–1900)

- Vibrato continuous.- Wide dynamic range; rubato substantial.- Long lyrical lines.- Full use of register; expressive shifts welcomed.

Modern (1900+)

- Technique-heavy.- May use extended techniques.- Senza vibrato on certain passages.- Irregular metres + atonal pitch language possible.

How to internalise style

Listen to one piece per period weekly, with score in hand. Mark performance choices (vibrato, rubato, bowing). Compare across recordings. Over a year, style sensitivity becomes second nature.

- Grades 1–2: getting the hands to work at all.- Grades 3–4: first shift, first concerto, first real expression.- Grade 5: the bottleneck (vibrato + theory paper).- Grades 6–7: double stops, advanced bowings, full repertoire.- Grade 8: synthesis — every technique in service of musical interpretation.

Grade 8 isn't an endpoint; it's a license to start the real study — the chamber music, the recital tours, the deeper Bach partitas, the modern works. Many adult learners find Grade 8 is where the violin finally feels like a voice rather than a project.

> Final trick: keep listening. Daily, lifelong. Every great violinist I admire spends more time listening than playing. Hearing shapes the hand. Hearing shapes the bow. Hearing makes you a musician — not just a violinist.

Chapter 1 — Harmonics: the bell-like tones

A harmonic is a high, pure, bell-like tone produced by lightly touching the string at specific nodes (rather than pressing down).

Natural harmonics

Found at division points of the open string:

| Touch point on string | Resulting harmonic | Pitch ||-----------------------|--------------------|-------|| 1/2 (octave node) | 1st overtone | Open string + octave || 1/3 node | 2nd overtone | Octave + 5th || 1/4 node | 3rd overtone | Two octaves above open || 1/5 node | 4th overtone | Two octaves + maj 3rd |

The 1/2 node on the A string is found by lightly touching with your 4th finger at the position where the 4th finger would normally play A5 (one octave above open A). The sound: a soft, ringing high A — an octave higher than expected.

> Trick: harmonics need almost no finger pressure — just a feather touch. If you press, you get a muted scratchy note. If you brush, you get the pure overtone. Practise the touch on open A: bow long stroke, feather-touch where the 4th finger sits in 1st position. The ringing high A appears almost magically.

Artificial harmonics

Press one finger firmly (the "stopping finger") and touch with another finger 1/4 above (the "harmonic finger"). The pitch produced is two octaves above the stopped pitch.

Notation: diamond-shape note head for the touched note, plus regular note for the stopped note.

Used famously in: Tchaikovsky Violin Concerto, Paganini Caprices, Ravel Tzigane.

Chapter 2 — All positions: fluency target

By Grade 7, you've learned half, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th positions. Now: 8th position and fluency across all of them.

8th position

On the A string: 1=B5, 2=C♯6, 3=D6, 4=E6 (right at the top of the violin's standard range for most pieces).

The left hand pivots: thumb shifts to a different angle, hand approaches the bridge.

The fluency goal

You should be able to:1. Shift to any position without looking.2. Find the correct first finger pitch within a quarter-tone, on the first try.3. Choose appropriate position for expression (tone colour) — not just to reach a note.

> Trick: practise a single piece entirely in 1st position, then again entirely in 3rd, then entirely in 5th (skipping notes you can't reach). The same melody in different positions sounds different — different string vibrations, different timbre. Pick the position that suits the music.

Position landmarks (physical)

| Position | Hand landmark ||----------|---------------|| 1st | Index touches the neck near the nut || 3rd | Thumb at the curve of the neck || 5th | Hand at the neck-body joint || 7th | Hand over the body curve || 8th+ | Thumb leaves the neck, sits on the shoulder of the violin |

Chapter 3 — Advanced bowings

Martelé ("hammered")

A short, percussive stroke with a strong attack and a dead stop. Bow pressure increased before the stroke begins, released through the stroke, stops abruptly.

Used for: marching feel, accented passages, opening of dramatic phrases.

Ricochet ("rebound, thrown")

Multiple notes from a single thrown bow. The bow is dropped from above, bounces 2–4 times naturally, lifted before it stops.

Used in: Mendelssohn Concerto last movement, Paganini Caprices, Sarasate showpieces.

Sautillé ("springing")

Very fast spiccato — so fast that the bow no longer fully lifts off; the wood "springs" rapidly. Played in the middle of the bow with a loose grip.

Used in: Vivaldi Four Seasons (Summer storm), Beethoven Concerto 1st mvt, Mendelssohn Concerto 1st mvt.

> Where I got stuck: I couldn't transition from spiccato to sautillé. The hand wanted to "control" each bounce. Fix: extreme tempo experiment. Start a tremolo (very fast back-and-forth on one note). Gradually add finger changes. The wrist learns the bouncing speed first, then the fingers add pitches.

Chapter 4 — Octave double stops

Two notes an octave apart, played simultaneously. Fingering: 1st finger on lower string + 4th finger on upper string, 3 strings apart (e.g., 1 on G + 4 on D for an octave).

Octaves require:- 1st and 4th fingers to be exactly in tune with each other.- 3rd and 4th fingers stretched apart maximally.

Practice

Slow tuner work. Tune 1st finger first; freeze it. Add 4th finger; adjust until the octave rings (no beats). Then bow the double stop.

Practise on every string pair: G+D, D+A, A+E.

> Trick: octaves are the second-hardest interval to tune (after major 7ths). Beats are very audible; an octave that's even slightly off sounds painful. Practise daily for 5 minutes. Octave fluency takes 6 months minimum.

Chapter 7 — Memorisation strategy

By Grade 7, you should perform from memory. Strategy:

1. Analyse the structure first. Break the piece into sections (A-B-A, exposition-development-recap, theme + variations, etc.). Label each section.2. Memorise section by section — not start-to-finish.3. Memorise the hardest section first. Mental energy is highest at the start of practice; spend it on the toughest bars.4. Practise from any starting point. Open the score randomly; you should be able to start there from memory. This builds resilience for performance anxiety.5. Mental practice. Without the violin, mentally walk through the piece — every shift, every bow direction, every fingering. 10 minutes a day. Increases memory reliability dramatically.6. Slow performance practice. Once memorised, play the whole piece at 60% tempo from memory. Catches weak spots.

> Where I got stuck: I memorised by repetition only — fingers learned the piece, but my brain didn't. Under stress (an audition), my fingers blanked. Fix: 10 minutes of mental practice daily. It feels useless at first, but after a month, memory failure rate dropped 80%.

Chapter 1 — Double stops: the violin singing in two voices

A double stop is two strings bowed simultaneously, each producing a different pitch. The bow must contact both strings at once.

Bow angle for double stops

The bow normally tilts to one side to contact one string. For double stops, the bow lies flat on both strings at the same angle. Bow arm height adjusts to suit.

Easy double stops: open string + stopped string

Examples:- Open D + 3rd finger A on D = A4 + D5 (a 4th)- Open A + 1st finger B on E = B5 + open A (depends; usually combined notes)

These are the entry-level double stops; you've been hearing them in pieces since Grade 3 (as the drone effect).

Stopped double stops: 3rds

Two fingers on adjacent strings, both stopped. For a 3rd in 1st position:

To get a 3rd, you need adjacent fingers giving notes 3 letters apart. In 3rd position on A + E:

The Grade 6 syllabus typically asks for a one-octave scale in 3rds in keys like G major.

6ths

Two-finger spread; the two fingers are usually on different strings, one slightly behind the other.

> Where I got stuck: my fingers couldn't independently stop adjacent strings at different positions. Fix: very slow practice with a tuner on each note separately, then together. 5 minutes per day, 6 weeks, before any scale in 3rds was reliable.

Chapter 2 — Chromatic scale

12 semitones in one octave, played either with traditional fingering or sliding-finger chromatic.

Traditional fingering (G chromatic ascending, one octave on G + D string)

The same finger plays two consecutive semitones by sliding (1 plays G♯ then A; 2 plays A♯ then B). The finger slides up about ½ a finger-width.

Galamian's chromatic fingering

Uses a different finger pattern that avoids slides. Faster but harder to learn.

> Trick: the slide-finger chromatic is more idiomatic and what most pieces expect. Practise it very slowly with metronome. The "click" of the finger sliding between two semitones should be inaudible — the bow disguises it.

Chapter 3 — Flying staccato (staccato volante)

A series of bouncing staccato notes all on one bow direction, the bow lifting between each.

Distinct from slurred staccato (which stays on the string). Flying staccato lifts.

How to learn it

1. Solid up-bow staccato first (easier direction). 4 notes per bow at slow tempo.2. Each note: bow stops and lifts a millimetre, then drops for the next note.3. Build to 8 notes per bow.4. Reverse to down-bow staccato.

Used famously in: Sarasate Zigeunerweisen, Wieniawski concertos, encore showpieces.

> Trick: the wrist + fingers produce the "stop+lift" motion; the arm stays steady. If your arm is jerking, you're forcing it. Relax, slow down.

Chapter 4 — Higher positions: 6th, 7th, half position

Half position

The hand sits below 1st position — 1st finger plays where the open string normally sounds (or a semitone above). Used for low chromatic passages.

6th and 7th positions

6th pos on A: 1=G♯5.7th pos on A: 1=A5 (an octave above open A).

In 7th position, the hand sits over the body of the violin. Thumb adjusts to a different position; left hand has limited mobility. Used mostly for very high passages in concertos and showpieces.

> Where I got stuck: my left wrist collapsed in 7th position — the angle felt wrong. Fix: explicitly lift the left elbow forward (more underneath the violin) when shifting above 5th. The hand pivots more from underneath.

Chapter 5 — Ornaments executed idiomatically

Grade 6 demands playing, not just identifying, ornaments.

Trill (tr or ⟵⟶)

Rapid alternation between the written note and the note a step above. In Baroque: starts on the upper note. In Classical/Romantic: starts on the written note.

The 2nd finger trills (lift-drop) while bow sustains. Trill speed: 6–10 oscillations per second. Practise slowly first, build speed.

Mordent (𝆚)

Quick three-note ornament: written note → step below (or above) → written note.

Turn (∾)

Four-note ornament: above-note → written → below-note → written.

Appoggiatura (small note before main note)

Takes half the value of the following note in Classical music.

Acciaccatura (small note with slash through stem)

A "crush" note — played as fast as possible, then immediately to the main note.

Chapter 1 — Vibrato: the expression

Vibrato is the small, rapid pitch oscillation that adds warmth and life to sustained notes. Without it, classical violin tone sounds naked.

Three vibrato types

| Type | Source | Speed | When ||------|--------|-------|------|| Wrist vibrato | Wrist rocks back and forth | Medium | Most repertoire || Arm vibrato | Whole forearm rocks | Slow + wide | Romantic, big tone || Finger vibrato | Finger joint flexes | Fast + narrow | Rare; modern works |

Wrist is the standard first vibrato taught at Grade 5.

Learning wrist vibrato — step by step

1. Air vibrato — without violin. Hold left arm up as if holding violin. Wave hand at the wrist, palm-up to palm-down motion, for 30 sec. This is the motion.2. Vibrato on knee. Sit. Place finger on knee. Rock wrist gently, finger stays in contact, pad rolls. Build motor pattern.3. Vibrato on violin neck (no string). Finger on the neck, not string. Rock wrist.4. Vibrato on string with finger anchored. 3rd finger on A (= D5). Bow long open D. Rock wrist; finger should roll (not slide). Pitch should wobble below the pitch and back to it.5. Slow metronome vibrato. 4 wobbles per beat at 60 bpm = 4 Hz. Build to 6 Hz over 6 months.6. Apply to scales. Vibrate every sustained note in a slow scale.

> Where I got stuck: my wrist locked. Vibrato felt impossible for 3 months. Fix: spend 5 minutes per day on the "knee drill" — no violin, no string, just the wrist motion. The brain learns motion separately from instrument. Once the wrist could rock freely, transferring to the violin took one week.

Pitch direction

Vibrato should wobble below the in-tune pitch back up to it. Never above. The brain hears the upper limit of the oscillation as "the note".

Where to start using it

Don't vibrate every note. Start with:- Long notes (half-notes, whole-notes).- Phrase peak notes.- Final note of a phrase.

By Grade 6, you'll vibrate almost every note that's longer than a quaver. At Grade 5, just the obvious ones.

Chapter 2 — Shifting to 4th and 5th positions

4th position

On the A string: 1=E5, 2=F♯5, 3=G♯5, 4=A5.

Note: 4 in 4th position = A5 = open E next door. Useful for staying on one string.

5th position

1=F♯5, 2=G♯5, 3=A5, 4=B5.

5th position is where the violin's neck meets the body. The thumb position changes — you'll start using the "shoulder" of the violin as a reference.

The shift map (3-octave G major)

To play G major across 3 octaves, you traverse:- G string: 1st position G-A-B-C- D string: 1st pos D-E-F♯-G- A string: 1st pos A-B-C♯-D- E string: 1st pos E-F♯-G- Shift to 3rd pos on E: A-B-C- Shift to 5th pos on E: D-E- (or shift higher: 7th position D-E-F♯-G — depends on edition)

> Trick: learn the shift route as a fixed sequence. Mark "III" or "V" above the music. Practise just the route (no rhythm) until the shifts are automatic; then add timing.

Silent shift drill (essential at Grade 5+)

1. Bow open A.2. Slide 1st finger up to D5 (3rd pos).3. Continue sliding to F♯5 (5th pos).4. Back down to D5, then to 1st position B.5. Repeat 10×, very slowly. No glissando audible.

Daily. The shifts smoothen over weeks.

Chapter 4 — Triads: all four qualities

Grade 5 theory requires identification of:

| Triad | Intervals (from root) | Sound ||-------|------------------------|-------|| Major | Maj 3rd + Min 3rd | Bright || Minor | Min 3rd + Maj 3rd | Sad || Diminished | Min 3rd + Min 3rd | Tense, unstable || Augmented | Maj 3rd + Maj 3rd | Suspenseful, dreamy |

Building each from C

- C major: C–E–G (M3 + m3)- C minor: C–E♭–G (m3 + M3)- C diminished: C–E♭–G♭ (m3 + m3)- C augmented: C–E–G♯ (M3 + M3)

Inversions

A triad has 3 notes; any of them can be in the bass:

| Position | Bass note | Example (C major) ||----------|-----------|--------------------|| Root | Root | C–E–G || First inversion | 3rd | E–G–C || Second inversion | 5th | G–C–E |

Notation: figured bass uses ⁶ (1st inv) and ⁶⁄₄ (2nd inv). At Grade 5 you must identify these from a score.

Chapter 5 — Tone colours

Grade 5 introduces colour markings in scores. Common ones:

| Marking | Italian | Effect ||---------|---------|--------|| sul G | "on the G string" | Forces phrase onto G string for rich, dark tone || sul D / A / E | "on the D/A/E string" | Same idea, different colour || sul ponticello | "near the bridge" | Glassy, eerie, metallic || sul tasto | "over the fingerboard" | Soft, flute-like, dreamy || pizzicato | "plucked" | Plucked string || col legno | "with the wood" | Tap with the bow stick |

Practise: D major scale sul ponticello, then sul tasto. The difference is dramatic. Composers exploit this.

Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills

Grade 5 aural is a separate exam paper in some syllabi or a section of the practical. It includes:

- Sing or play back a 4–8 bar phrase after one hearing.- Identify modulations to dominant or relative minor.- Identify cadences in context.- Identify chord progressions (basic I-IV-V-I).- Sight-sing a longer phrase with leaps.

Chapter 8 — The Grade 5 Theory prerequisite

In the ABRSM system, you cannot enter the Grade 6, 7, or 8 practical exam without first passing Grade 5 Theory. This is the bottleneck.

Grade 5 theory paper covers

- All intervals (size + quality, simple + compound).- All triads (4 qualities + inversions).- All cadences.- Time signatures including 5/4, 7/8 (irregular).- Transposition (writing music in a new key).- Score reading (alto + tenor clefs introduction; full score interpretation).- Composition: 8-bar melody using given opening.- Ornament identification (trill, mordent, turn, appoggiatura, acciaccatura).

Preparation strategy

1. Buy ABRSM Music Theory in Practice — Grade 5 workbook.2. Work through 1 chapter per week.3. Take 2 past papers per month for the last 3 months.4. Aim for 90% on practice papers before booking the exam.

Average study time: 6 months alongside violin practice.

Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine

3rd position — the workhorse

3rd position is the second most used position after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd.

Finding 3rd position

On the A string:1. Play open A.2. Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).3. Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5.

Other strings analogous:- G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.- D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.- E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D.

The shift motion

Three components, in this order:1. Release finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).2. Thumb and hand travel together up the neck.3. Press the new finger at the target.

The finger that leads the shift is called the guide finger. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1.

> Trick: practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a "scrape", the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide.

2nd position

Less common than 3rd. The hand sits halfway between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F.

2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits just above 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches.

How to choose position

| Reason | Position to use ||--------|------------------|| Reach high notes | Higher position || Stay on same string (tone colour) | Whatever position keeps you there || Avoid awkward string crossing | The position that smooths the line || Score marks it | Composer chose; obey || Difficult fingering pattern | Try 2nd position |

Chapter 2 — Melodic minor in playing

Grade 3 introduced melodic minor on paper. Grade 4 requires playing.

D melodic minor (one or two octaves)

Ascending: D E F G A B C♯ D — raise 6 (B) and 7 (C♯).Descending: D C B♭ A G F E D — back to natural.

Fingering (one octave on D + A):

That B♭ on descent / B♮ on ascent on the same string is the tricky part. Same finger (1), different position.

> Where I got stuck: my hand could not switch B♮ ↔ B♭ in one scale. Fix: bow only the A string portion (B♭ down, B♮ up) until the finger shape change became automatic. Don't add the rest of the scale until that bar is reliable.

All Grade 4 minors

| Minor | Form | Octaves ||-------|------|---------|| D minor | Harmonic + Melodic | 2 || G minor | Harmonic + Melodic | 1 (some 2) || A minor | Harmonic + Melodic | 2 || E minor | Harmonic + Melodic | 1 or 2 |

Six combinations to practise. Allocate one minor per day, rotate.

Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce

Spiccato is the foundation of all advanced bowing. The bow lifts off the string between notes — not because you lifted it, but because the bow's elasticity bounces it.

How spiccato works

The bow is flexible. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the "sweet spot" (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note.

Spiccato practice progression

1. Drop test. Hold the bow over the A string, let it fall from 1 cm above, watch it bounce. Find the bow position where bounce is most controlled (usually 4–6 inches above the middle, near the balance point).2. One bounce per drop. Drop, lift, drop. Slow.3. Two bounces per drop without re-lifting. The bow bounces itself.4. Continuous bouncing on open string at metronome 60.5. Add scale notes. D major scale, spiccato.6. Speed up gradually to 100, then 120.

> Trick: spiccato fails when you grip the bow. Relax thumb + pinky; let the bow do the work. If your wrist hurts after spiccato practice, you're forcing it. Stop, shake out, restart looser.

Détaché vs spiccato vs staccato — quick distinction

| Bowing | Bow contact | Sound ||--------|-------------|-------|| Détaché | On string | Smooth separation || Spiccato | Bounces off | Light, dancing || Staccato | On string, sharp stop | Short, dry || Slurred staccato | On string, multiple stops one direction | Pulsed long line |

Chapter 4 — New keys: E major, A♭ major

E major (4 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯)

Fingering: normal "high" positions everywhere. Coming after the flat keys, E major feels easy because the violin's open strings (E, A, D) are all natural to the key.

A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭)

The hardest playable key at Grade 4. Two "low" fingers on most strings; A♭ itself (instead of open A) creates an unusual hand position.

Most editions require A♭ for one octave only at Grade 4. Two-octave A♭ is Grade 5+.

> Trick: don't memorise A♭ note-by-note. Memorise it as a modified D major shape, with low 1 on the D string and a low everywhere else. Pattern recognition over note recall.

Chapter 5 — All four cadences (theory)

Grade 4 paper requires recognition of all four cadences:

| Cadence | Chords | Sound | Example ||---------|--------|-------|---------|| Perfect (Authentic) | V → I | "Full stop" | End of Twinkle || Plagal ("Amen") | IV → I | "Settled, churchy" | "Amen" at hymn end || Imperfect (Half) | any → V | "Hanging question" | Mid-phrase pause || Interrupted | V → vi (instead of I) | "Surprise" | Romantic deception |

Identify by ear in aural: play tonic chord + the cadence chord. Listen to the resolution.

> Trick: sing "Happy Birthday" — the last two chords are V → I, perfect. Sing the church "Amen" at the end of any hymn — that's IV → I, plagal. Pre-load these references; any cadence question becomes "which of my references does this match?".

Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills

Grade 4 adds:

- Sing back a 4-bar phrase with one or two leaps.- Identify cadences (perfect, plagal, imperfect, interrupted).- Identify features in a passage (modulation? change of texture?).- Sight-sing a simple 4-bar phrase (Grade 4+ feature).

Sight-singing strategy

1. Look at the time signature + key signature.2. Hum the tonic note (the examiner gives it).3. Trace the contour with your finger silently.4. Sing slowly with the rhythm correct.5. Don't worry about perfect pitch — relative shape matters more than absolute tuning at Grade 4.
