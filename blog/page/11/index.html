PostgreSQL Course — 03 · Indexes and the Query Planner
Created2025-10-05|Database PostgreSQL
An index is a redundant, automatically maintained data structure that lets the server find rows without reading the whole table. Adding the right one turns a forty-second query into a two-millisecond one; adding six wrong ones slows every write, wastes gigabytes, and leaves the slow query exactly as slow. The skill worth acquiring is not "add an index" — it is reading well enough to know which index, and to recognise the cases where the planner's sequential scan is genuinely the right answer. This post covers B-tree mechanics, all six index access methods, the leading-column rule, index-only scans, and the operational side: concurrent builds, bloat, and finding indexes nobody uses. Structure A PostgreSQL B-tree is a Lehman–Yao B-link tree, built from 8 KB pages: Numbers worth internalising: - Each page holds on the order of a few hundred entries for typical key widths, so fanout is a few hundred.- Three levels therefore index roughly a hundred million rows; four levels index far more. Depth grows logarithmically and stays small.- A single-row lookup costs about depth + 1 page reads: one per level down, plus one heap page fetch — and the upper levels are almost always cached.- The maximum index entry is about one third of a page (roughly 2704 bytes at the default 8 KB ). Chapter 2 — The six index types B-tree The default and the right answer for perhaps 90% of indexes: any type with a total order, equality and range predicates, sorting, uniqueness. Hash Equality only. WAL-logged and crash-safe since PostgreSQL 10 (before that they were not replicated and had to be rebuilt after a crash, which is why old advice says "never use hash indexes"). Narrower than a B-tree for long keys, since it stores a 32-bit hash rather than the value. In exchange it cannot do ranges, cannot sort, cannot be unique, and cannot be multicolumn. The niche is genuinely narrow: very long keys, equality-only access, no ordering requirement. Benchmark against a B-tree on your own data before committing. GIN — Generalized Inverted Index For values that contain multiple searchable items: array elements, keys and values, full-text lexemes, trigrams. GIN reads fast and writes slowly, because one row update can touch many index entries. The mechanism buffers changes in a pending list and flushes them in batches — good for write throughput, but it means a query may have to scan the unflushed pending list, producing occasional latency spikes. Tune with or set for read-latency-sensitive workloads. GiST — Generalized Search Tree A framework for indexing things without a natural total order: geometry, ranges, nearest-neighbour search, and the exclusion constraints from Post 01. GiST is lossy: it returns candidate rows that the executor must recheck. That is why a GiST plan shows a . SP-GiST — Space-Partitioned GiST For naturally hierarchical, non-balanced partitionings: quadtrees, k-d trees, radix trees. In practice: / containment, points, and text prefix search where a radix tree beats a B-tree on space. BRIN — Block Range INdex Stores a summary (by default min/max) for each range of heap pages — 128 pages by default. Consequently it is minuscule: an index over a billion-row table can be a few megabytes. BRIN only works when the physical row order correlates with the indexed value — append-only time-series data, log tables, tables loaded in key order. If rows are scattered, every block range spans the whole value domain and the index eliminates nothing. Check before building: PostgreSQL 14 added opclasses (tolerant of a few outliers that would otherwise ruin a range's summary) and opclasses (equality on uncorrelated columns). When a sequential scan is correct This bears stating plainly, because a great deal of wasted tuning effort comes from trying to force an index scan that would be slower. A sequential scan is the right plan when: 1. The predicate matches a large fraction of the table — beyond roughly 5–10%, random heap fetches through an index exceed the cost of reading everything sequentially. The exact crossover depends on and physical correlation.2. The table is small. Under a few hundred pages, one sequential read beats index descent plus heap fetch. The planner knows this and is right.3. No index covers the predicate, and creating one is not justified by the query's frequency.4. The query is an aggregate over most of the table — genuinely must look at everything (or at least at the whole visibility map, via a parallel index-only scan).5. Parallelism applies. A parallel sequential scan across four workers can beat a single-threaded index scan outright. To diagnose — never to fix in production code: If the forced index plan is slower, the planner was right and the investigation is over. If it is much faster, you have found a cost-model or statistics problem — which is Post 04's subject. Linear Algebra — 05 · Inner Products, the Spectral Theorem and the SVD
Created2025-09-17|Mathematics Linear Algebra
Linear Algebra 02 defined a vector space with only addition and scaling: an abstract vector space has no lengths and no angles until you add them. This post adds them with an inner product — the dot product of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the model — and three big results follow. Orthogonal projection gives the best approximation from a subspace, which is the whole theory of least squares. The spectral theorem says every real symmetric matrix is diagonal in an orthonormal basis — the eigen-story of Linear Algebra 04 with no defective cases and perpendicular eigenvectors. And the singular value decomposition extends that to every matrix, square or not: any linear map is a rotation, a stretch along perpendicular axes, and another rotation. The SVD gives the best low-rank approximation, the pseudoinverse and principal component analysis. Chapter 1 — Inner products and norms Terminology - Inner product on a real vector space $V$ — a function $\langle u, v\rangle \in \mathbb{R}$ that is symmetric, linear in each argument, and positive definite (axioms below).- Inner product space — a vector space together with an inner product.- Norm $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$ — the length of $v$. A unit vector has norm 1.- Orthogonal — $\langle u, v\rangle = 0$, written $u \perp v$.- Orthonormal list — pairwise orthogonal unit vectors.- Orthogonal complement $W^\perp = \{v : \langle v, w\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\}$.- Orthogonal matrix — a square real matrix $Q$ with $Q^TQ = I$. Axioms and examples An inner product on a real vector space satisfies, for all $u, v, w$ and scalars $c$: 1. Symmetry: $\langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle$.2. Linearity: $\langle u + cv, w\rangle = \langle u, w\rangle + c\langle v, w\rangle$ (and so, by symmetry, linear in the second slot too).3. Positive definiteness: $\langle v, v\rangle \ge 0$, with equality only for $v = 0$. Examples: - Dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$: $\langle x, y\rangle = x^Ty = \sum xi yi$. This is the default in this post.- Weighted dot product: $\langle x, y\rangle = \sum wi xi yi$ with all $wi > 0$. More generally $\langle x, y\rangle = x^TMy$ for any symmetric positive definite $M$ (Chapter 6).- Functions: on $C[0,1]$, $\langle f, g\rangle = \int0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt$. For example $\langle 1, t - \tfrac12\rangle = \int0^1 (t - \tfrac12)\,dt = 0$: the constant 1 and $t - \frac12$ are orthogonal, and $\lVert t - \tfrac12\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{12}$.- Matrices: $\langle A, B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(A^TB) = \sum{i,j} a{ij}b{ij}$ (the Frobenius inner product). Over $\mathbb{C}$ the symmetry axiom becomes $\langle u, v\rangle = \overline{\langle v, u\rangle}$ and the standard product is $\sum xi \overline{yi}$; everything below has a complex version with $A^T$ replaced by the conjugate transpose $A^$. Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality Theorem 1 (Cauchy–Schwarz). $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert$, with equality if and only if $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent. Proof. If $v = 0$ both sides are 0. Otherwise, for every real $t$, $$0 \le \lVert u - tv\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 - 2t\langle u, v\rangle + t^2\lVert v\rVert^2.$$ A quadratic in $t$ with positive leading coefficient that is never negative has at most one real root, so its discriminant is $\le 0$: $4\langle u, v\rangle^2 - 4\lVert u\rVert^2\lVert v\rVert^2 \le 0$. Equality means the discriminant is 0, so for some $t$ the quadratic is 0, that is $u = tv$; conversely $u = tv$ gives equality directly. ∎ Cauchy–Schwarz makes angles possible: define $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ by $\cos\theta = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert}$, a number in $[-1, 1]$. Example: $u = (1, 2, 2)$, $v = (3, 0, 4)$ have $u \cdot v = 11$, $\lVert u\rVert = 3$, $\lVert v\rVert = 5$, so $\cos\theta = 11/15$ and $\theta \approx 42.8°$. Theorem 2 (triangle inequality). $\lVert u + v\rVert \le \lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert$. Proof. $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \le \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert + \lVert v\rVert^2 = (\lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert)^2$, using Cauchy–Schwarz in the middle. Take square roots. ∎ The same expansion gives Pythagoras: if $u \perp v$ then $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2$. Chapter 2 — Orthogonality and projection Orthonormal bases Proposition 3. An orthogonal list of nonzero vectors is linearly independent. Proof. If $\sum ci vi = 0$, take the inner product with $vj$: every term except $cj\langle vj, vj\rangle$ vanishes, so $cj\lVert vj\rVert^2 = 0$ and $cj = 0$. ∎ The same computation shows why orthonormal bases are so convenient. If $q1, \dots, qn$ is an orthonormal basis, then for every $v$: $$v = \sum{i=1}^n \langle v, qi\rangle\, qi, \qquad \lVert v\rVert^2 = \sum{i=1}^n \langle v, qi\rangle^2.$$ Coordinates are just inner products — no linear system to solve. The second formula is Parseval's identity. Orthogonal projection onto a subspace Let $W$ be a subspace with orthonormal basis $q1, \dots, qk$. The orthogonal projection of $v$ onto $W$ is $$PW v = \sum{i=1}^{k} \langle v, qi\rangle\, qi.$$ The residual $v - PW v$ is orthogonal to every $qj$ (take the inner product and use orthonormality), hence to all of $W$. So every $v$ splits uniquely as $v = PW v + (v - PW v)$ with the first part in $W$ and the second in $W^\perp$, and $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = \dim V$. Theorem 4 (best approximation). $PW v$ is the unique closest point of $W$ to $v$: for every $w \in W$ with $w \neq PW v$, $$\lVert v - w\rVert > \lVert v - PW v\rVert.$$ Proof. Write $v - w = (v - PW v) + (PW v - w)$. The first part is in $W^\perp$, the second in $W$, so by Pythagoras $\lVert v - w\rVert^2 = \lVert v - PW v\rVert^2 + \lVert PW v - w\rVert^2$, and the last term is positive when $w \neq PW v$. ∎ In matrix form. If $Q$ is the $n \times k$ matrix with orthonormal columns $qi$, then $Q^TQ = Ik$ and the projection matrix is $P = QQ^T$. If the columns of $A$ are any basis of $W$ (not orthonormal), then $$P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T.$$ Every projection matrix satisfies $P^2 = P$ and $P^T = P$. Projection onto a single line through $a$ is $\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}$; for example the projection of $b = (3, 0, 3)$ onto the line through $a = (1, 2, 2)$ is $\frac{a \cdot b}{a \cdot a}a = \frac{9}{9}a = (1, 2, 2)$, with residual $(2, -2, 1) \perp a$. > Common pitfall: $\sum \langle v, qi\rangle qi$ is the projection only when the $qi$ are orthonormal. With a non-orthogonal basis, adding up the one-dimensional projections double-counts, and the result is not the closest point. Use $A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$, or orthonormalise first. Chapter 3 — Gram–Schmidt and QR The algorithm Given independent vectors $v1, \dots, vn$, Gram–Schmidt produces an orthonormal list $q1, \dots, qn$ with the same span at every stage: $\operatorname{span}(q1, \dots, qj) = \operatorname{span}(v1, \dots, vj)$ for each $j$. At step $j$, subtract from $vj$ its projection onto the span of the earlier $q$'s, then normalise: $$wj = vj - \sum{i < j} \langle vj, qi\rangle\, qi, \qquad qj = \frac{wj}{\lVert wj\rVert}.$$ $wj \neq 0$ because $vj$ is not in the span of $v1, \dots, v{j-1}$. So every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. Worked example. $v1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $v2 = (1, 0, 1)$, $v3 = (0, 1, 1)$. - $q1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)$.- $\langle v2, q1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so $w2 = (1, 0, 1) - \frac12(1, 1, 0) = (\frac12, -\frac12, 1)$, with $\lVert w2\rVert = \sqrt{3/2}$. Then $q2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, -1, 2)$.- $\langle v3, q1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\langle v3, q2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}$, so $w3 = (0, 1, 1) - \frac12(1, 1, 0) - \frac16(1, -1, 2) = (-\frac23, \frac23, \frac23)$, and $q3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}(-1, 1, 1)$. Check: $q1 \cdot q2 = \frac{1 - 1 + 0}{\sqrt{12}} = 0$, $q1 \cdot q3 = \frac{-1 + 1 + 0}{\sqrt6} = 0$, $q2 \cdot q3 = \frac{-1 - 1 + 2}{\sqrt{18}} = 0$. QR factorisation Gram–Schmidt, written as matrices, is a factorisation. Each $vj$ is a combination of $q1, \dots, qj$ only, with coefficients $r{ij} = \langle vj, qi\rangle$ ($i < j$) and $r{jj} = \lVert wj\rVert$. So $$A = QR,$$ with $Q$ having orthonormal columns and $R$ upper triangular with positive diagonal. For the example, $R = Q^TA$: $$R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} & 1/\sqrt6 \\ 0 & 0 & 2/\sqrt3 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1.4142 & 0.7071 & 0.7071 \\ 0 & 1.2247 & 0.4082 \\ 0 & 0 & 1.1547 \end{pmatrix}.$$ > Watch out: Classical Gram–Schmidt loses orthogonality in floating point when the columns are nearly dependent. Libraries compute QR with Householder reflections (or use modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts each projection from the updated vector). The factorisation is the same; the arithmetic is more stable. Chapter 4 — Least squares The problem An overdetermined system $Ax = b$ ($A$ is $m \times n$ with $m > n$) usually has no solution: $b$ is not in the column space $C(A)$. The least-squares solution $\hat x$ minimises $\lVert b - Ax\rVert$. By Theorem 4, $A\hat x$ must be the projection of $b$ onto $C(A)$, so the residual $e = b - A\hat x$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$: $A^Te = 0$. That gives the normal equations $$A^TA\,\hat x = A^Tb.$$ If the columns of $A$ are independent, $A^TA$ is invertible (if $A^TAx = 0$ then $x^TA^TAx = \lVert Ax\rVert^2 = 0$, so $Ax = 0$, so $x = 0$), and $\hat x = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb$ is unique. Worked example — fitting a line Fit $y = c0 + c1 x$ to the points $(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2), (4, 5)$. Each point gives one equation $c0 + c1 xi = yi$: $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^Tb = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix}.$$ $\det(A^TA) = 120 - 100 = 20$, so $$\hat c = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} 30 & -10 \\ -10 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} -10 \\ 24 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 \\ 1.2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ The best line is $y = -0.5 + 1.2x$. The fitted values are $(0.7, 1.9, 3.1, 4.3)$ and the residuals $e = (0.3, 0.1, -1.1, 0.7)$. Checks: $A^Te = 0$, that is, the residuals sum to 0 and $\sum xi ei = 0.3 + 0.2 - 3.3 + 2.8 = 0$. The minimum sum of squared errors is $0.09 + 0.01 + 1.21 + 0.49 = 1.8$; no other line does better. Least squares with QR If $A = QR$ (reduced: $Q$ is $m \times n$), the normal equations $R^TQ^TQR\hat x = R^TQ^Tb$ simplify to $$R\hat x = Q^Tb,$$ solved by back substitution. This is the method used in practice. Forming $A^TA$ squares the condition number (Chapter 7), which can destroy half the significant digits; QR avoids it. Chapter 5 — Orthogonal matrices and the spectral theorem Orthogonal matrices A square $Q$ with $Q^TQ = I$ has orthonormal columns and $Q^{-1} = Q^T$. It preserves inner products, lengths and angles: $(Qx)\cdot(Qy) = x^TQ^TQy = x \cdot y$. Taking determinants, $\det(Q)^2 = 1$, so $\det Q = \pm 1$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ the orthogonal matrices are exactly the rotations ($\det = 1$) and the reflections ($\det = -1$) of Linear Algebra 03. Products and inverses of orthogonal matrices are orthogonal. A change of basis to an orthonormal basis uses an orthogonal $P$, so $P^{-1}AP = P^TAP$: no inverse to compute. The spectral theorem Theorem 5 (spectral theorem, real symmetric case). If $A$ is a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix ($A^T = A$), then: 1. all eigenvalues of $A$ are real;2. eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal;3. $A$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal. Conversely, any $Q\Lambda Q^T$ is symmetric, so a real matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable if and only if it is symmetric. Proof sketch. The key identity is $\langle Ax, y\rangle = x^TA^Ty = x^TAy = \langle x, Ay\rangle$. (1) Let $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \in \mathbb{C}^n$ nonzero. The number $\bar v^TAv$ equals its own complex conjugate (conjugate it, use that $A$ is real, then transpose the $1 \times 1$ result and use $A^T = A$), so it is real. It also equals $\lambda\,\bar v^Tv = \lambda\sum\lvert vi\rvert^2$, and $\sum\lvert vi\rvert^2 > 0$ is real. So $\lambda$ is real. (2) If $Av1 = \lambda1 v1$ and $Av2 = \lambda2 v2$, then $\lambda1\langle v1, v2\rangle = \langle Av1, v2\rangle = \langle v1, Av2\rangle = \lambda2\langle v1, v2\rangle$, so $(\lambda1 - \lambda2)\langle v1, v2\rangle = 0$. (3) Induction on $n$. By (1) there is a real eigenvalue $\lambda1$ with a unit eigenvector $q1$. The complement $W = q1^\perp$ is mapped into itself: if $w \perp q1$ then $\langle Aw, q1\rangle = \langle w, Aq1\rangle = \lambda1\langle w, q1\rangle = 0$. On the $(n-1)$-dimensional space $W$, $A$ still satisfies $\langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle$, so by induction $W$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $q2, \dots, qn$. Together with $q1$ this is an orthonormal eigenbasis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. ∎ Worked example. $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ from Linear Algebra 04 has eigenvalues $4, 1, 1$. The eigenvector $(1,1,1)$ for 4 is automatically orthogonal to the eigenplane $x + y + z = 0$ for 1. Inside that plane, Linear Algebra 04 used $(-1, 1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, 1)$, which are not orthogonal to each other — Gram–Schmidt fixes that. An orthonormal choice is $$q1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1), \quad q2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1, 0), \quad q3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, 1, -2), \qquad Q^TBQ = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1, 1).$$ The spectral decomposition writes $A$ as a sum of rank-one projections: $$A = \lambda1 q1q1^T + \lambda2 q2q2^T + \dots + \lambdan qnqn^T.$$ For $B$: $B = 4q1q1^T + q2q2^T + q3q3^T$. Since $q2q2^T + q3q3^T = I - q1q1^T$, this is $B = I + 3q1q1^T$. > Trick: For symmetric matrices, eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are orthogonal for free. You only need Gram–Schmidt inside an eigenspace of dimension 2 or more. Chapter 6 — Positive definite matrices and quadratic forms Four equivalent tests A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite if $x^TAx > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$, and positive semidefinite if $x^TAx \ge 0$ for every $x$. Theorem 6. For a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent: 1. $A$ is positive definite;2. every eigenvalue of $A$ is positive;3. every leading principal minor (the determinant of the top-left $k \times k$ block, $k = 1, \dots, n$) is positive (Sylvester's criterion);4. elimination without row swaps succeeds with every pivot positive;5. $A = R^TR$ for some invertible $R$ — for example the Cholesky factor, upper triangular with positive diagonal. Proof of 1 ⇔ 2. Write $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ and put $y = Q^Tx$ (a change to the eigenvector basis; $y \neq 0$ exactly when $x \neq 0$). Then $$x^TAx = y^T\Lambda y = \lambda1 y1^2 + \dots + \lambdan yn^2,$$ which is positive for all $y \neq 0$ exactly when every $\lambdai > 0$. For 5 ⇒ 1: $x^TR^TRx = \lVert Rx\rVert^2 > 0$ when $x \neq 0$ because $R$ is invertible. The links to 3 and 4 go through the LU factorisation of Linear Algebra 01: the $k$-th pivot equals the ratio of the $k$-th and $(k-1)$-th leading minors. ∎ Worked example. $$K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ - Leading minors: $2$, $4 - 1 = 3$, $\det K = 4$. All positive.- Pivots: $2$, $\frac32$, $\frac43$ — the ratios $\frac21, \frac32, \frac43$ of consecutive minors.- Eigenvalues: $2 - \sqrt2 \approx 0.586$, $2$, $2 + \sqrt2 \approx 3.414$.- The quadratic form is a sum of squares: $x^TKx = 2x^2 - 2xy + 2y^2 - 2yz + 2z^2 = x^2 + (x - y)^2 + (y - z)^2 + z^2$, which is $0$ only when $x = y = z = 0$. All four tests agree: $K$ is positive definite. (This matrix is the second-difference matrix that appears when a differential equation $-u'' = f$ is discretised.) > Common pitfall: Positive entries do not make a matrix positive definite, and negative entries do not prevent it. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ has all entries positive but determinant $-1$, so its eigenvalues are $2 \pm \sqrt5$, one of them negative; $K$ above has negative entries and is positive definite. Quadratic forms and principal axes A quadratic form is $q(x) = x^TAx$ with $A$ symmetric (any cross term $bxy$ is split evenly between the two off-diagonal entries). The spectral theorem rotates the axes so the cross terms disappear. Worked example. $q(x, y) = 5x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2$ has $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with trace 7 and determinant 6, so eigenvalues $6$ and $1$. Unit eigenvectors: $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)$ for 6 and $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1, 2)$ for 1. In the rotated coordinates $u = \frac{2x + y}{\sqrt5}$, $v = \frac{-x + 2y}{\sqrt5}$, $$q = 6u^2 + v^2.$$ (Check at $(x, y) = (1, 0)$: $u = 2/\sqrt5$, $v = -1/\sqrt5$, and $6 \cdot \frac45 + \frac15 = 5 = q(1, 0)$.) The level curve $q = 6$ is the ellipse $u^2 + v^2/6 = 1$: semi-axis $1$ along $(2, 1)$ and $\sqrt6$ along $(-1, 2)$. The principal axes are the eigenvectors, and the semi-axes are $\sqrt{c/\lambdai}$ for the level $q = c$. In calculus the same idea classifies critical points: a positive definite Hessian means a local minimum. Chapter 7 — The singular value decomposition The theorem Diagonalization needs a square matrix, and even then it can fail or need complex numbers. The SVD always works. Theorem 7 (SVD). Every real $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ can be written as $$A = U\Sigma V^T,$$ where $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) are orthogonal and $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ with diagonal entries $\sigma1 \ge \sigma2 \ge \dots \ge \sigmar > 0$ and zeros elsewhere. The $\sigmai$ are the singular values (uniquely determined by $A$); the columns $vi$ of $V$ and $ui$ of $U$ are the right and left singular vectors, and $Avi = \sigmai ui$. Proof. $A^TA$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite ($x^TA^TAx = \lVert Ax\rVert^2 \ge 0$), so by the spectral theorem it has an orthonormal eigenbasis $v1, \dots, vn$ with eigenvalues $\lambda1 \ge \dots \ge \lambdan \ge 0$. Exactly $r$ of them are positive, because $A^TA$ has the same null space as $A$ (shown in Chapter 4), hence rank $r$. Put $\sigmai = \sqrt{\lambdai}$ and, for $i \le r$, $ui = Avi/\sigmai$. These are orthonormal: $$ui \cdot uj = \frac{vi^TA^TAvj}{\sigmai\sigmaj} = \frac{\lambdaj\, vi \cdot vj}{\sigmai\sigmaj} = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \neq j. \end{cases}$$ Extend $u1, \dots, ur$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$. Then $Avi = \sigmai ui$ for $i \le r$ and $Avi = 0$ for $i > r$, which is $AV = U\Sigma$, that is $A = U\Sigma V^T$. ∎ Dropping the zero parts gives the compact form $A = \sum{i=1}^{r}\sigmai ui vi^T$, a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices. The SVD also hands you orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces of Linear Algebra 02: $v1, \dots, vr$ for the row space, $v{r+1}, \dots, vn$ for the null space, $u1, \dots, ur$ for the column space, and $u{r+1}, \dots, um$ for the left null space. Worked example $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}.$$ $A^TA$ has trace 50 and determinant $625 - 400 = 225$, so eigenvalues $45$ and $5$, with unit eigenvectors $v1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $v2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)$. The singular values are $\sigma1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.708$ and $\sigma2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$. Then $$u1 = \frac{Av1}{\sigma1} = \frac{(3, 9)/\sqrt2}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3), \qquad u2 = \frac{Av2}{\sigma2} = \frac{(-3, 1)/\sqrt2}{\sqrt5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(-3, 1),$$ $$A = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}{U} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 3\sqrt5 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt5 \end{pmatrix}}{\Sigma} \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}{V^T}.$$ Checks: $\sigma1\sigma2 = 15 = \det A$ (here $\det U = \det V = 1$), and $\sigma1^2 + \sigma2^2 = 50 = 9 + 0 + 16 + 25$, the sum of the squared entries. > Watch out: The singular values of $A$ are not its eigenvalues. This $A$ is triangular with eigenvalues 3 and 5, but singular values $6.708$ and $2.236$. For a symmetric matrix the singular values are the absolute values of the eigenvalues (more generally this holds for normal matrices, those with $A^TA = AA^T$), and for a positive semidefinite matrix they are exactly the eigenvalues. Geometry: rotate, stretch, rotate Read $A = U\Sigma V^T$ from right to left as three steps applied to the unit circle: 1. $V^T$ is orthogonal: it rotates (or reflects) the plane, carrying $v1, v2$ to $e1, e2$. Here $V^T$ is rotation by $-45°$. The circle stays a circle.2. $\Sigma$ stretches along the axes by $\sigma1$ and $\sigma2$. The circle becomes an axis-aligned ellipse with semi-axes $6.708$ and $2.236$.3. $U$ rotates again, carrying $e1, e2$ to $u1, u2$. In general $U$ and $V$ may include a reflection; both can be chosen as rotations exactly when $\det A \ge 0$ (for square $A$). Norms and conditioning - Spectral norm: $\lVert A\rVert2 = \max{\lVert x\rVert = 1}\lVert Ax\rVert = \sigma1$ — the longest semi-axis.- Frobenius norm: $\lVert A\rVertF = \sqrt{\sum a{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sigma1^2 + \dots + \sigmar^2}$ (orthogonal matrices do not change it).- Condition number of an invertible $A$: $\kappa(A) = \sigma1/\sigman$, the ratio of the longest to the shortest semi-axis. For the example $\kappa = 3\sqrt5/\sqrt5 = 3$. Solving $Ax = b$ can amplify relative errors in $b$ by up to $\kappa$; since $\sigmai(A^TA) = \sigmai^2$, forming the normal equations squares it. This, and not the size of $\det A$, measures closeness to singularity (Linear Algebra 03, Chapter 7). Chapter 8 — Using the SVD Low-rank approximation Theorem 8 (Eckart–Young, statement). Let $A = \sum{i=1}^r \sigmai ui vi^T$ and, for $k < r$, $Ak = \sum{i=1}^k \sigmai ui vi^T$. Among all matrices $B$ of rank at most $k$, $$\min{\operatorname{rank} B \le k}\lVert A - B\rVert2 = \lVert A - Ak\rVert2 = \sigma{k+1}, \qquad \min{\operatorname{rank} B \le k}\lVert A - B\rVertF = \lVert A - Ak\rVertF = \sqrt{\sigma{k+1}^2 + \dots + \sigmar^2}.$$ Keep the $k$ largest singular triples and drop the rest; nothing of rank $k$ does better. For the example, $$A1 = 3\sqrt5\, u1v1^T = \frac{3}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 1.5 \\ 4.5 & 4.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A - A1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix},$$ and $\lVert A - A1\rVertF^2 = 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5 = \sigma2^2$, as the theorem says. Storage: a rank-$k$ approximation of an $m \times n$ matrix needs $k(m + n + 1)$ numbers instead of $mn$. For a $1000 \times 1000$ greyscale image and $k = 50$, that is $100\,050$ numbers instead of $1\,000\,000$, about 10%. Whether the result looks acceptable depends on how fast the singular values decay. The pseudoinverse The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of $A = U\Sigma V^T$ is $$A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^T,$$ where $\Sigma^+$ is $n \times m$ with $1/\sigmai$ in place of each nonzero $\sigmai$. It inverts $A$ on the row space and sends the left null space to 0. $x^+ = A^+b$ is the least-squares solution of $Ax = b$ with the smallest norm — well defined even when $A$ is rank-deficient and the normal equations have infinitely many solutions. If $A$ has independent columns, $A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ and $A^+b$ is the $\hat x$ of Chapter 4; for the line fit, $A^+b = (-0.5, 1.2)$. If $A$ is invertible, $A^+ = A^{-1}$. Example. $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2\,uu^T$ with $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, so $A^+ = \frac12 uu^T = \frac14\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. For $b = (1, 3)$ (not in the column space), $x^+ = A^+b = (1, 1)$. Every $x$ with $x1 + x2 = 2$ gives the same smallest residual (because $Ax = (2, 2)$, the projection of $b$), and $(1, 1)$ is the shortest of them. Principal component analysis Put $N$ data points in $\mathbb{R}^p$ as the rows of a matrix and subtract the mean of each column, giving a centred $N \times p$ matrix $X$. The sample covariance matrix is $C = \frac{1}{N - 1}X^TX$. If $X = U\Sigma V^T$ then $C = V\frac{\Sigma^T\Sigma}{N - 1}V^T$, so: - the principal directions are the right singular vectors $vi$ (eigenvectors of $C$);- the variance along $vi$ is $\sigmai^2/(N - 1)$;- projecting the data onto $v1, \dots, vk$ is the best $k$-dimensional summary in the least-squares sense (Eckart–Young applied to $X$). Worked example. The four points $(3, 3), (-3, -3), (1, -1), (-1, 1)$ already have mean zero. $X^TX = \begin{pmatrix} 20 & 16 \\ 16 & 20\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 36 and 4 with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so the singular values of $X$ are 6 and 2. The first principal direction is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ with variance $36/3 = 12$; the second has variance $4/3$. The first component explains $36/(36 + 4) = 90\%$ of the total variance. > Common pitfall: Forgetting to centre the data. Without subtracting the mean, the first "principal component" mostly points at the mean itself. Also scale the columns first when they are in different units, or the largest-unit variable dominates.
