Violin Notes — 03 · Grade 3 New Keys, Shifting Prep & Slurred Staccato
Created2026-05-04|Music
Grade 3 ABRSM violin is the gateway between "beginner who can play tunes" and "real student". Two new flat keys appear (B♭ and E♭), the three minor scales must be distinguished, a hint of position shifting is introduced, and bowings begin to diversify (slurred staccato, hooked bowing). The repertoire shifts toward Baroque dances and early Classical pieces. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 (this post) → 04 Grade 4 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play two-octave scales of G, D, A, B♭ majors and one-octave E♭ major.- Play D, G, A natural and harmonic minors (one or two octaves as syllabus).- Distinguish the three minor forms by ear and on paper.- Use slurred staccato and hooked bowing.- Prepare for 3rd position shifting (theoretical introduction).- Identify interval quality (major, minor, perfect) at sight.- Play 5–7 Grade 3 pieces with confident phrasing. Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves) B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering: Wait — that's three "low" fingers across the scale. The hand shape changes per string. > Trick: the rule for any key with flats: drop the finger that plays the flatted note half a finger-width toward the nut. For B♭ major, that's 2 on G string, 1 on D, 1 on A. Memorise the pattern of which finger is low, not the abstract scale. Top note: B♭5 (4th finger on E in 1st position). Why this key matters B♭ is the first key violinists genuinely struggle with. The hand has to relearn its default spacing. Master B♭ by Grade 3, and every flat key after becomes intuitive. Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave) E♭ has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. One octave only at Grade 3. Different editions fingerprint this differently. The point: 3 flats means 3 "low" finger placements. Get the pattern of lowness right and your fingers will land. > Where I got stuck: I tried to memorise E♭ as "B♭, E♭, A♭" abstractly. Fix: I stopped naming the flats and just thought "low-low-low" in the right places. Faster, more reliable. Chapter 3 — The three minor forms Grade 2 introduced natural minor. Grade 3 adds harmonic and (sometimes) melodic. Natural minor (review) Same notes as the relative major. A natural minor: Harmonic minor — raises the 7th The leading-tone effect that classical music expects: That raised 7th creates an augmented 2nd between F and G♯ (3 semitones — a full step + half-step). This gives harmonic minor its Middle-Eastern or Spanish flavour. > Trick: harmonic minor sounds exotic compared to natural minor. Sing both ascending — natural feels "modal/folk", harmonic feels "classical/dramatic". After ten A/B comparisons, your ear locks the difference. Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down Two different note sets in one scale. Composers chose this in the Baroque to handle melodic lines without the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd "bump". Grade 3 typically requires melodic minor only on paper; full playing comes Grade 4. D, G, A minor (Grade 3 syllabus) | Major | Relative minor | Common form examined ||-------|----------------|----------------------|| F major | D natural & harmonic minor | Both || B♭ major | G natural & harmonic minor | Both || C major | A natural & harmonic minor | Both | Practise: play the major scale, then immediately its relative minor (natural form). Then play harmonic minor of the same root. Six versions per session. Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato A new bowing. Notation: slur (curved line) + dot under each note within the slur. The bow stays on the string; the wrist makes a small stop-start motion for each note while moving in one direction. > Where I got stuck: I lifted the bow between notes — that's staccato volante (flying staccato), Grade 6 stuff. Fix: practise on open strings, bow stays on the string, only the wrist pulses. Picture a bow that "hesitates" 4 times in one stroke. Hooked bowing Two unequal notes (often dotted-quaver + semiquaver) on one bow with a tiny stop between them: Used to keep the dotted figure on the same bow direction throughout a phrase. Saves bow-direction confusion. Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview True shifting (moving the hand to 2nd/3rd/etc. positions) is Grade 4 territory, but Grade 3 introduces the concept and may use one shift in a piece or as a "step out" of 1st position. Why shift? 1. Reach higher notes the 1st position can't.2. Stay on one string for a phrase (changing tone colour).3. Make awkward fingerings easier. What 3rd position feels like In 3rd position, the 1st finger plays what the 3rd finger plays in 1st position. So on the A string: - 1st position: 3rd finger = D5.- 3rd position: 1st finger = D5. The hand moves up the neck by about 1 inch. First shift exercise On the A string:1. Open A, then 3rd finger D (1st position).2. Slide the 1st finger up the string until it sounds D.3. Now you're in 3rd position. Play 2 (E) and 3 (F♯). Don't lift; slide smoothly. The thumb travels with the hand. > Trick: the thumb moves at the same time as the fingers. If the thumb stays back, you get a stretched, painful hand. Practise the shift with eyes closed, only feeling the smooth slide. Listen for the slide pitch — at first it'll glissando, later it'll be silent. Grade 3 may include one piece with a marked shift; Grade 4 requires the technique. Violin Notes — 02 · Grade 2 Two-Octave Scales & Slur Control
Created2026-05-03|Music
Grade 2 ABRSM violin doubles your reach: two-octave scales, the first minor key, dotted rhythms, and longer slurs that demand real bow planning. The hand shape from Grade 1 stays, but it now travels — string crossings within a scale become the central skill. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 (this post) → 03 Grade 3 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play two-octave scales of G and D major, one-octave C and F major.- Play A natural minor (one octave) and recognise the relative-minor concept.- Use dotted-quaver + semiquaver rhythms accurately.- Slur 4–8 notes per bow with even tone.- Read fluently up to top D / E above the staff (E string, 4th finger).- Play 4–6 Grade 2 pieces with hands together, musically shaped. Chapter 1 — Two-octave scales arrive A two-octave scale spans two strings (Grade 1 used two strings for one octave); now you traverse three strings. G major (two octaves) Grade 2 typically asks for the scale to reach G5 (3rd finger on E in 1st position). Some editions extend to A5. D major (two octaves) Top note is A5. C major (one octave) — first piece with no sharps > Trick: C major is the shock key for violin beginners. Every other scale you've played has the half-step between 2 and 3. C major has it between 1 and 2 on the D string. Mark the music with an arrow over the F natural for the first 2 weeks. F major (one octave) Half-step lands between finger 1 and 2 on the A string (B♭ → C). The B♭ requires placing finger 1 low — closer to the nut than normal. > Where I got stuck: "low 1" felt unnatural for weeks. The finger wants to land where it always does. Fix: physically mark a tape line for "low 1" position on the A string for one practice session. Once your finger learns the distance, peel the tape off. Chapter 2 — The first minor scale: A natural minor A natural minor uses the same notes as C major but starts on A: Fingering (one octave starting from open A): Or starting on the G string for low-octave practice: 1=A (low) ... but Grade 2 starts on open A. What "natural minor" means Three forms of minor exist: | Form | Pattern (intervals) | Where used ||------|---------------------|------------|| Natural | W-H-W-W-H-W-W | Grade 2 introduction || Harmonic | Raises 7th by half-step | Grade 3+ || Melodic | Raises 6th + 7th going up, naturals coming down | Grade 4+ | Grade 2 only requires natural minor. Memorise it now; the others build on it. > Trick: A natural minor = the white notes from A to A. C major = the white notes from C to C. They share all notes — they're relative. Every major key has a relative minor a minor 3rd (3 semitones) below. Memorise this pairing now: it saves hours later. Chapter 3 — Dotted rhythms A dot after a note adds half its value: The Grade 2 staple is the dotted-quaver + semiquaver pair: Count subdivisions: > Trick: I subdivided in semiquavers aloud for every dotted pair for 2 weeks: "ONE-e-and-AH, ONE-e-and-AH". Sounds ridiculous, locks the ratio in. Once it's automatic, drop the speaking. Bow distribution for dotted rhythms The long note gets more bow, the short note gets less. Roughly 3:1 ratio matching the duration: If you give them equal bow, the short note sounds louder (more bow speed in less time). Wrong feel. Chapter 4 — Longer slurs and bow planning Grade 1 slurs were 2–4 notes. Grade 2 introduces 6, 8, even a full scale (8 notes) on one bow. The bow-budget rule Total bow length = 25 inches (varies by player + bow).For 8 notes in one bow: 25 ÷ 8 ≈ 3 inches per note. You cannot play loud + slow + long-slurred. Pick two. Grade 2 long slurs are usually mezzo-piano and steady — the bow conserves itself. Slur with string crossing Example: slur "open A → 1st finger B → open E" in one bow. Two string changes inside one stroke. The right wrist + elbow tilt to roll between strings without breaking the sound. > Where I got stuck: every slur with a string crossing scratched on the crossing point. Fix: slow practice with the metronome at 40 bpm, paying attention to the angle of the bow hair at the crossing. The bow should tilt gradually, not snap. Drill: 5 minutes a day for 2 weeks → solved. Practice progression 1. 2-note slur, same string.2. 2-note slur, crossing strings.3. 4-note slur, same string.4. 4-note slur, crossing strings.5. 8-note slur = full scale. Don't jump steps. Each stage takes about a week. Chapter 5 — Intervals (Grade 2 theory) Grade 2 theory paper introduces interval size within the major and natural minor keys: | Size | Example (C major) | Notes spanned ||------|--------------------|----------------|| 2nd | C–D | 2 || 3rd | C–E | 3 || 4th | C–F | 4 || 5th | C–G | 5 || 6th | C–A | 6 || 7th | C–B | 7 || 8ve | C–C (high) | 8 | > Trick: count letter names inclusive. C to E = C, D, E = 3 letters = a 3rd. Never count semitones for size at Grade 2 — that's Grade 3 (interval quality). Practise: pick a key, name every interval from the tonic. Then play each on the violin. Linking name + sound + hand position triples retention. Chapter 7 — Intonation upgrade: cross-string checking Grade 1 used drones on open strings. Grade 2 adds two-string checks at the same pitch: | Pitch | Way 1 | Way 2 ||-------|-------|-------|| A4 | Open A | 3rd finger on D || E5 | Open E | 3rd finger on A || D5 | 3rd finger on A | (also = open string interval check) | If the two ways give the same pitch, you're in tune. If the octave rings, your top note is true. If it beats, adjust. Tape removal If you used tape on the fingerboard in Grade 1, remove it by Grade 2 exam. The eyes-on-fingerboard habit costs you sight-reading speed. Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills ABRSM Grade 2 aural adds: - Clap back rhythm in 6/8 (new at Grade 2).- Sing back a 4-bar phrase.- Identify if a melody is in major or minor.- Identify the time signature (2/4, 3/4, 4/4, or 6/8). Major vs minor identification Major = "bright, happy". Minor = "sad, darker". The most reliable test: the third. Major 3rd (C–E) = 4 semitones. Minor 3rd (C–E♭) = 3 semitones. > Trick: sing "Happy Birthday" (major) immediately, then "Hey Jude" verse (also major) — internalise the colour. Then sing "Greensleeves" (Dorian, sounds minor) or "House of the Rising Sun" (minor) — feel the gloom. After a week of A/B comparisons, you'll identify them in 2 seconds. Violin Notes — 01 · Grade 1 First Steps & Repertoire
Created2026-05-02|Music
Grade 1 ABRSM violin is where fingers meet strings. The syllabus is small but the techniques inside are foundational: 1st position fingering, three major keys (G, D, A), one-octave scales, basic bowing (détaché and slur), and a handful of short pieces that you'll remember decades later. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Place fingers 1, 2, 3, 4 in 1st position on all four strings.- Play one-octave scales of G, D, A majors (separate bows).- Play one-octave arpeggios for the same keys.- Read fluently in treble clef across the first 1.5 octaves above Middle C.- Use détaché (separate) bowing cleanly.- Begin to feel "in tune" — recognise when a finger placement is sharp or flat.- Play 3–5 Grade 1 / Initial Grade pieces with hands together. Chapter 1 — 1st Position fingering What "1st position" means The position number tells you where the first finger sits on the fingerboard. In 1st position, the index finger sits just below the nut — about a whole tone above the open string. From there, fingers 2, 3, 4 extend up the fingerboard, each roughly a whole tone apart (with one half-step adjustment per scale). The diagram shows the A/D major pattern: whole-step between every finger. In other keys, the half-step location shifts (Chapter 3). Finger numbering | Finger | Number ||--------|--------|| Index | 1 || Middle | 2 || Ring | 3 || Little (pinky) | 4 || (Thumb supports underneath, no number) | — | This is opposite to piano — there, the thumb is 1. On the violin, the thumb has no number because it never stops a string; its job is to brace the neck. > Trick: coming from piano, I kept calling my thumb "finger 1" and reaching with my index expecting it to be "2". The fix: every Grade 1 piece marks "1" above the index. Trust the printed number, not your old habit. Placing fingers cleanly 1. Fingertip down, not the pad — the smaller contact area produces a cleaner stop.2. Curved finger — knuckles bent.3. Just enough pressure to stop the string vibrating above the finger. Too much pressure = sharp pitch + finger fatigue.4. Drop and lift — don't slide. Vertical motion only. > Where I got stuck: I pressed every finger too hard for two months. My left hand cramped after 10 minutes. The fix: deliberately under-press until the note buzzes; then add the minimum extra pressure to stop the buzz. That's the right amount. Most students press 2-3× more than needed. Chapter 2 — Tuning revisited (the violinist's ear) You learnt to tune in Post 00. Grade 1 adds double-stop tuning — bow two adjacent open strings together and listen for the perfect 5th. How a perfect 5th sounds When two strings tuned a perfect 5th apart sound together, the combined tone is smooth and ringing. When slightly off, you hear beats — a wobbling, rough pulse like an out-of-sync washing machine. To tune by 5ths:1. Tune A to A=440 with a tuner.2. Bow A and D together gently. Adjust D's fine tuner until beats vanish.3. Bow D and G together. Adjust G.4. Bow A and E together. Adjust E. > Trick: sit in a quiet room. Beats are easier to hear without background noise. After 6 months of tuning by 5ths, you'll do it automatically — and you'll have built a perfect-5th template in your ear that helps every interval recognition exercise. Chapter 3 — The three Grade 1 scales G major (one octave, G string + D string) Open G → 1st finger A → 2nd finger B → 3rd finger C → open D → 1st finger E → 2nd finger F♯ → 3rd finger G. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 on each string. (B→C and F♯→G are half-steps.) D major (one octave, D string + A string) Open D → 1 E → 2 F♯ → 3 G → open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 again. A major (one octave, A string + E string) Open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D → open E → 1 F♯ → 2 G♯ → 3 A. Same half-step pattern. > Trick: G, D, A majors all use the same finger pattern in 1st position: 0-1-2-3 with the half-step between 2 and 3 on each string. Memorise one hand shape; transfer to all three keys. This is the "violin equivalent" of the piano's W-W-H-W-W-W-H — but expressed as finger placement. Arpeggios (Grade 1) The tonic triad spelled out: root, third, fifth, octave. Practise scales and arpeggios in pairs: G major scale → G major arpeggio → G again. Builds the key-feel. Chapter 4 — Bowing: détaché and slur Détaché "Detached" — each note gets its own bow stroke (down or up). The bow doesn't lift between notes; it changes direction on the string. In Grade 1 you play almost everything détaché. Goal: smooth, even tone with no scratching at the bow direction change. The wrist makes a tiny "give" at each change, like a pendulum bottom. Slur A curved line over two or more notes means play them in one bow stroke (one direction, no change). Same hair, multiple finger changes. > Where I got stuck: I scratched every bow change for the first 2 months. Fix: practise the bow change motion itself without a string — just air bow with the empty bow, smoothly reversing direction. The wrist learns to soften the change. Then add the string back. Bow distribution On a slur of 4 notes, you have to "ration" the bow length so you don't run out. Roughly: divide the bow into 4 equal parts; one part per note. Practice: slur 2 notes per bow, then 4, then 8. By Grade 3 you'll be slurring entire scales in one bow. Chapter 5 — Reading music for violin Violin reads in treble clef only (unlike the piano, which uses both). Range covers from open G (below the staff, on a ledger line) up to many ledger lines above. By Grade 1 you read up to about A or B above the staff (3rd finger on E string). Violin Notes — 00 · Beginner Basics & Your First Sound
Created2026-05-01|Music
The first post in a self-study violin series. Goal: by the end you can hold the violin without straining, name every open string, draw a straight bow that makes one sustained tone, and play three short open-string pieces. The violin is harder than the piano at the start — there's no "pressed key = right pitch" guarantee — but the payoff is the most expressive single-line instrument we have. > Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Hold the violin with relaxed posture, no shoulder hunching.- Hold the bow with the correct grip and produce a straight stroke.- Name the four open strings (G, D, A, E) low to high and find them on the staff.- Tune with a clip-on tuner or app.- Play three open-string exercises that produce a clear, ringing tone.- Understand the difference between violin, viola, cello, and double bass — pick the right one. Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now) The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is unfretted — you place your finger somewhere and the precision of placement decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise. In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match. > Where I started: I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Size matters first Violins come in fractional sizes for children: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 4/4 (full). Adults use 4/4 unless they have unusually short arms. Avoid $50-$100 Amazon "VSO" (Violin-Shaped Object) instruments — they're often un-tuneable, with cheap pegs that slip and bridges that are wrong. Cheap-and-dull will discourage you within a month. Chapter 3 — The instrument anatomy Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these): - Scroll, pegs, peg box — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs- Nut — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard- Fingerboard — the long black wooden strip your fingers press- Bridge — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration- F-holes — the two carved openings; let sound out- Soundpost — small wooden dowel inside the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.- Bass bar — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.- Tailpiece — anchors the strings at the bottom- Fine tuners — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)- Chinrest — where your chin rests- Shoulder rest — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years. Standing 1. Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.2. Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).3. Head facing forward, then turn it slightly left to look down the fingerboard.4. Place the violin on your left collarbone, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.5. The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — don't grip with your jaw. The shoulder rest does the work. > Where I got stuck: I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest higher until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is supported, not gripped. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit. Sitting Same upper-body posture. Sit on the front half of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch. Left-hand position - Thumb rests on the side of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.- Fingers curve over the strings.- Wrist straight — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.- A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger. Common mistakes:- Wrist collapsed under the neck (the "frying pan grip") — kills agility.- Thumb wrapped over the top of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.- Fingers flat on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down. Chapter 5 — The bow Anatomy of the bow - Stick — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)- Hair — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings- Frog — black bottom piece where the screw lives- Screw / button — adjusts hair tension- Tip / point — top end- Grip / wrap — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life) 1. Pick the bow up at the frog with your right hand.2. Thumb bent, tip placed in the notch between the frog and the wrap.3. Middle finger opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.4. Ring finger rests on the side of the frog.5. Pinky curved on top of the stick, near the screw.6. Index finger wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight. All fingers curved, never locked straight. The grip is light — too tight and the bow can't move. > Trick: practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — then pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows. Tightening the bow Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a standard pencil fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings. Always loosen after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber. Rosin Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times before every session. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone. Chapter 6 — Tuning The four strings, low to high: G — D — A — E. Each interval is a perfect 5th (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5. With a clip-on tuner or app 1. Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.2. Pluck or bow each string one at a time.3. Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise. Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments) Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing into the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Push-and-turn together. - Turn away from you (top of peg moves forward) = string winds up = pitch rises.- Turn toward you = pitch falls. Small turns. Pegs are sensitive. Tune the G first, then D, A, E. Fine tuning (small adjustments) The little screws on the tailpiece. Turn clockwise to raise pitch, counter-clockwise to lower. Use these for daily small corrections (< quarter-tone). > Where I got stuck: I terrified myself snapping a string in week one. Fix: tune slowly and small. If a string is way off, raise it gradually, not in one big peg crank. Snapped strings are normal — buy a backup E string ($5) and stop being afraid. Tuning A first (orchestral convention) In orchestral settings, tune A first (because the oboe gives an A). Then tune D, G, E by listening for perfect fifths with the open A. This is your first ear-training exercise — when the two strings are exactly a 5th apart, the combined sound is clean. When slightly off, you hear "beats" — a wobbling pulse. Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing Before fingers go on strings, master pulling a clean tone on each open string. The straight bow The bow must move perpendicular to the strings (parallel to the bridge). If it angles toward the fingerboard or the bridge, the contact point changes and the tone breaks up. Practice exercise:1. Place the bow flat on the A string, about halfway between bridge and fingerboard.2. Slowly pull a downstroke (bow moving from frog toward tip). Count "1-2-3-4".3. Pause. Reset. Upstroke (tip toward frog). Same count.4. Watch the bow in a mirror — is it staying parallel to the bridge? Contact point Three knobs control your sound:- Weight — how much the bow presses down (mostly from the index finger via the stick)- Speed — how fast the bow moves- Contact point — distance from the bridge (closer to bridge = louder + sharper; closer to fingerboard = softer + warmer) Balance these three. A faster bow needs more weight to maintain pressure. A bow close to the bridge needs slower speed to avoid scratching. The "sweet spot" for a clean, warm tone is about 1.5–2 cm from the bridge for a normal mezzo-forte dynamic. > Analogy: think of the bow like painting. Speed is how fast you move the brush. Weight is how hard you press. Contact point is which part of the paper you're stroking. Change any one and the paint stroke changes. Beginners change all three accidentally and produce a mess. Master one knob at a time. Three first exercises Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string Whole bow length, 8 beats per stroke. Down-up-down-up on each string in turn: G, D, A, E. Listen for a clean, sustained tone with no scratches or wobbles. Five minutes daily. Exercise 2 — String crossings Bow A for one beat, then D for one beat. The forearm rotates from elbow; wrist stays neutral. Do A-D-A-D-A-D, then D-A-D-A. Then add G-D and A-E. Tests bow-arm coordination without fingers complicating things. Exercise 3 — "Hot cross buns" (open strings only) The melody fits perfectly on three open strings if you treat it as A-D-A-D-G-D-A. Just bow each string in the rhythm of the tune. You'll feel embarrassed playing nursery rhymes — and then proud, because you sound musical. Chapter 8 — How to practise The single most important rule: slow and small reps beat fast big ones. Five minutes of focused open-string bowing teaches more than thirty minutes of trying to play melodies you can't yet.
