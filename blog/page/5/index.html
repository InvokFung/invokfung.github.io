Chapter 4 — Mipmaps + anisotropy A mipmap is a chain of pre-downscaled copies of the texture (1024 → 512 → 256 → 128 → ... → 1). Chapter 7 — Environment maps + IBL A reflects its surroundings via an environment map (a panoramic image of "what the object sees"). Without one, metals look black and dielectrics look flat. Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap Terminology - Pitch (音高) — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.- Accidental — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.- Sharp (♯) — raise the pitch by one semitone.- Flat (♭) — lower the pitch by one semitone.- Natural (♮) — cancel a previous sharp or flat. Chapter 1 — Why piano is a good first instrument The keyboard is the clearest visual map of Western music. Every other instrument hides pitch behind a fingering chart; the piano lays the entire pitch space out in front of you, left-to-right, low-to-high. If you understand "how a scale looks" on the piano, you understand it on a guitar, a violin, or in a software synth — you just don't see it as plainly. A second practical reason: the piano is monophonically simple but polyphonically rich. Beginners can produce a clean tone from day one (press → sound), while advanced players spend a lifetime on the same instrument. Few instruments span that range. > Where I started: I wasted my first two weeks googling "best digital piano under $500" instead of practising. The truth is any 88-key weighted keyboard from a major brand (Yamaha, Roland, Casio, Kawai) is fine for the first year. The piano you have and play every day beats the piano you research and never own. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Acoustic (real) piano - Upright (直立式) — most homes. Footprint 150 cm wide, depth 60 cm. Tone is good but action is lighter than a grand.- Grand (平台式) — long horizontal action, more responsive. Babies (150 cm) to concert (275 cm). Expensive, needs tuning twice a year. Digital piano - Weighted action (重錘鍵盤) is non-negotiable for serious practice. "Semi-weighted" and "synth action" feel different and will delay your finger development.- 88 full keys preferred over 76 or 61 — exam pieces will eventually need the full range.- Built-in speakers are a "nice to have"; quality varies. Chapter 3 — The keyboard map Black-key landmarks The black keys come in groups of 2 and 3. The 2-group anchors C-D-E; the 3-group anchors F-G-A-B. - The white key immediately left of a 2-group is always C.- The white key immediately left of a 3-group is always F. These two anchors are the only ones you ever need to memorise. The other letters (D, E, G, A, B) are derived by counting in alphabetical order from C or F. > Trick: the 2-group is C-D-E; 3-group is F-G-A-B. Stack the consonants in your head: Car Drives East, Fish Goes Around Back. Cheesy works. Middle C The C closest to the centre of the keyboard. On an 88-key piano this is the fourth C from the bottom — C4 in scientific pitch notation. On a 61-key it's the third C. Either way, find the centre, find the 2-group nearest it, drop your right thumb on the white key to the left of that pair. Octaves Same letter, doubled frequency: $f{n+12} = 2 \cdot fn$. The keyboard pattern repeats every 12 keys (7 white + 5 black). Once you memorise the 7-white-key span (C D E F G A B), the entire 88-key keyboard is mapped. AI Prompting (Foundations & Practice)
Created2026-05-15|AI
Prompting is the practice of writing inputs that steer a Large Language Model (LLM) toward useful outputs. It is part interface design, part rhetoric, and part empirical science. This note covers the foundations and the techniques that have been validated by published research, with worked examples you can copy. Learning Outcome - Explain what a prompt is and why model behaviour is sensitive to its phrasing.- Decompose a prompt into its functional parts (instruction, context, examples, format spec).- Apply core techniques: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role conditioning, decomposition, and self-consistency.- Recognise common anti-patterns and explain why they fail.- Design an evaluation harness with a golden set + rubric + A/B framework.- Read a research paper on prompting and judge whether its claims will transfer to your task. Chapter 1 - Foundations Terminology - Prompt — the full token sequence the model conditions on before generating the next token.- Completion — the model's generated response.- Token — sub-word unit (4 characters of English on average). All limits (context window, throughput, billing) are measured in tokens.- Context window — maximum prompt+completion length the model can attend to.- In-context learning (ICL) — model adapts its behaviour from examples in the prompt without weight updates. Reported in Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, Brown et al. (2020).- Temperature — sampling parameter; $0$ is greedy, higher values increase diversity.- Top-p / Top-k — nucleus / top-k sampling, alternatives to temperature.- System / developer message — privileged prompt segment used to set persistent behaviour. Mechanism formalised by The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Why prompts matter An LLM is a next-token predictor over a learned distribution $P(tn \mid t1, \dots, t{n-1})$. The prompt is the conditional sequence $t1, \dots, t{n-1}$. Two prompts that read as semantically equivalent to a human can produce very different distributions because the model is sensitive to token-level cues it has learned to associate with answer quality (formality, code-block fences, "Let's think step by step", etc.). This sensitivity has been measured empirically. Lu et al. (2022) showed that simply reordering few-shot examples can swing accuracy on classification benchmarks by tens of percentage points. Sclar et al. (2024) showed that benign formatting changes (newline vs. colon as separator) shift task accuracy by a comparable margin. The practical consequence: prompts are programs. They deserve version control, testing, and review. How the model "reads" a prompt A typical chat-tuned model receives a structured sequence — for instance OpenAI's ChatML or Anthropic's / turn markers — where each turn is tagged with a role. The model has been fine-tuned (instruction-tuned + RLHF or DPO) to: 1. Treat the system / developer message as a high-priority constraint.2. Treat user messages as questions or requests.3. Treat its own prior responses as commitments to maintain consistency with. The training procedure was first detailed in InstructGPT, Ouyang et al. (2022) and the Anthropic RLHF paper, Bai et al. (2022). The hierarchy of trust between roles was later sharpened in The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Chapter 2 - Anatomy of a Prompt A well-formed prompt contains, in some combination, six functional parts. Naming them helps you debug what is missing. | Part | Purpose | Example || --- | --- | --- || Role / persona | Set a frame the model uses to choose vocabulary, depth, formality | "You are a senior database administrator." || Instruction | The actual verb of the request | "Explain why this query is slow." || Context | Domain facts, retrieved documents, prior turns | The SQL query + output || Examples (shots) | Demonstrations of input → output | A prior worked optimisation || Format spec | Constraint on output shape | "Return JSON matching schema X" || Stop / guardrails | Negative constraints, refusal triggers | "If the query is fine, say only." | A minimal template The order matters less than the presence of each part. The OpenAI prompt-engineering guide (OpenAI 2024) and Anthropic's prompt library both converge on the same six-part skeleton. Chapter 3 - Core Techniques Zero-shot Ask the question directly with no examples. Works when the task is well-represented in the training distribution (translation, summarisation, common QA). Few-shot (in-context learning) Provide $k$ input/output pairs, then the new input. Brown et al. (2020) showed few-shot prompting closes a large fraction of the gap to fine-tuning on many tasks without any gradient updates. Tips: - Order examples easy → hard (Lu et al., 2022).- Make examples cover the failure modes you observe in zero-shot, not the easy cases.- Keep formatting identical across shots; a stray newline can degrade accuracy (Sclar et al., 2024). Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting Ask the model to show its work before committing to an answer. Reported in Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, Wei et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning on large models. Two variants worth knowing: - Zero-shot CoT: append "Let's think step by step." Kojima et al. (2022). Cheap and surprisingly effective.- Self-consistency: sample $n$ chains at temperature $>0$, take the majority vote on the final answer. Wang et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic accuracy further at $n \times$ cost. Role / persona conditioning Setting an expert persona biases vocabulary, depth, and inferred audience. The effect is real but smaller than CoT or few-shot; treat it as fine-tuning of style, not capability. Decomposition Break a hard problem into ordered sub-problems and prompt each separately, chaining outputs. Equivalent to Khot et al. (2023) "Decomposed Prompting" or least-to-most prompting by Zhou et al. (2023). The chain is easier to debug than a single monolithic prompt; you can swap or rerun any step. ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) Interleave reasoning steps with tool calls. Yao et al. (2023). The model alternates between (a reasoning step), (a tool call), and (tool result), until ready to emit an . ReAct is the foundation pattern under most "agent" frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex, modern function-calling SDKs). XML tags have the advantage of being self-documenting and survive whitespace normalisation. Anthropic explicitly recommends XML in their prompt-engineering documentation. Markdown structure Models trained on heavy Markdown corpora respond well to headings (), lists, and tables. Use the same conventions in your prompt that you want to see in the output. Numbered constraints Numbered constraints are easier for the model to enumerate against than a paragraph of "and also, make sure to..." Format specifications Three escalating strengths: | Strength | Mechanism | Reliability || --- | --- | --- || Soft | Natural-language description ("return JSON") | 60-80% || Schema | Provide explicit schema (TypeScript / JSON Schema / Pydantic) | 90% || Hard | Use the provider's structured-output / JSON-mode API | 100% (provider-enforced) | If you need a guaranteed shape, use the provider's structured output. Soft format is convenient but will fail on tail cases. Chapter 5 - Reasoning Boosters Self-consistency Already mentioned. Formally: sample $n$ chains $c1, \dots, cn$ at $T > 0$, extract the final answer $ai$ from each, return $\arg\maxa \sumi \mathbb{1}[ai = a]$. The probability that the majority answer is correct converges to the per-sample accuracy raised to a Condorcet-style power as $n$ grows, when individual samples are conditionally independent given the question. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) Yao et al. (2023). Generalises CoT from a chain to a search tree. The model proposes multiple next-thoughts at each step, a value model scores each, and a search algorithm (BFS/DFS) explores. Helps on combinatorial problems (Game of 24, crosswords) where a single chain commits too early. Reflexion Shinn et al. (2023). After a wrong attempt, the model writes a critique of its own trace; that critique is appended to context for the next attempt. The agent improves across attempts within a single session. Step-Back prompting Zheng et al. (2024). Before answering a specific question, ask the model to first derive the general principle that governs the question. The principle then conditions the specific answer. Improves abstract / multi-step reasoning. Chapter 6 - Common Anti-patterns Vague verbs , , , are unmeasurable. The model has to guess a direction. Replace with a measurable verb: , , . Conflicting constraints The model resolves contradictions arbitrarily. Pick one side of each axis and state it. Leading questions The framing forces the model to defend a premise instead of evaluating it. Prefer: Long irrelevant context Liu et al. (2023) Lost in the Middle showed models degrade on retrieval tasks when relevant information sits in the middle of a long context. Prune aggressively. If you must include long retrieved documents, put the most relevant snippets at the start or the end. Negation-only instructions is weaker than Models follow positive constraints more reliably than negative ones. "Polite filler" adds tokens without affecting behaviour. Strip it. Chapter 7 - Evaluation A prompt is a piece of software, so evaluate it like software. Golden set A small, curated set of representative inputs with hand-graded expected outputs. 30–100 items is enough to detect a 5pp accuracy shift. Rubric Decompose "is the output good?" into yes/no axes. For a summarisation task you might score: - Factual (no hallucinated entities)- Complete (all named main points present)- Concise (within length limit)- Format (parses as JSON)- Refusal (correctly refused when no claim present) Each axis scored 0/1 by a human grader or a separate "judge" model. LLM-as-judge Zheng et al. (2023). A stronger model grades a weaker model's output against a rubric. Cheap, scales, but biased toward outputs that look like the judge's style. Use a different family of model as the judge where possible, and spot-check 10% with a human. A/B framework Track $\Delta$ per axis, not just overall. A change that improves factuality but hurts conciseness is still a regression on the conciseness axis. Statistical sanity With $n=100$ binary outcomes, the 95% confidence interval on accuracy is roughly $\pm 10$pp around the observed rate. Differences smaller than that are noise. Increase $n$ or repeat the run to tighten. Chapter 8 - Tool Use and Structured Output Modern model providers expose two APIs that should replace most prompt-engineering hacks: Function calling / tool use You declare a JSON schema for one or more tools; the model picks one and emits arguments matching the schema. The runtime executes the tool and returns the result, which you append to the conversation and prompt again. The model is fine-tuned to know when to call a tool versus answer directly. This is more reliable than ReAct in raw text because the model emits parsed JSON via the API. Structured output (JSON / Pydantic mode) You supply the full output schema; the provider's decoder constrains generation so the response is guaranteed valid against the schema. Eliminates "the model added a friendly preamble that broke my parser" failures. When NOT to use tools If the task is pure transformation of text the model already has the knowledge for (summarise, translate, classify), tools add latency and cost. Use tools when you need fresh data, deterministic computation, or side effects.
