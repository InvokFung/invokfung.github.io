Chapter 1 — The paradigms, and what each is for Terminology - Parametric, history-based modelling — the model is a recorded sequence of operations (a feature tree) driven by named dimensions. The reasons are structural, not stylistic: 1. No dimensions. A mesh stores vertex coordinates. It does not store "this face is 20 mm from that face because a bearing is 20 mm wide". Change one thing and you re-derive everything by hand.2. No constraints. Nothing enforces that two faces stay parallel or that a hole stays centred. Move a vertex and the design intent is silently gone.3. No solid guarantee. A mesh can be non-manifold, self-intersecting or open, and Blender will happily let you build and export one. Chapter 2 — BREP solids and triangle meshes This is the single most important technical idea in the post, because every accuracy problem in Post 05's Chapter 4 traces back to it. Two ways to describe a shape BREP (boundary representation) describes a solid by its boundary, as a topological structure of faces, edges and vertices, where each face carries an exact analytic surface — a plane, a cylinder, a cone, a sphere, a torus, or a NURBS patch — and each edge an exact curve. A cylindrical hole is stored as "cylindrical surface, axis here, radius 3.0". That radius is exact at every scale. Zoom in a million times and it is still a perfect circle. Triangle mesh describes a surface as a list of vertices and a list of triangles. There are no analytic surfaces. A cylindrical hole is stored as some number of flat facets approximating a circle. The approximation is fixed at the moment of export. | | BREP | Mesh ||---|---|---|| Curved surfaces | exact analytic / NURBS | flat facets approximating the surface || Precision | resolution-independent | fixed at export || Topology | faces, edges, vertices with adjacency | triangles that may or may not connect properly || Validity | kernel enforces a watertight solid | nothing enforces anything || Booleans | exact | approximate, fragile, and can fail || Fillet a 3 mm edge | one operation, exact | rebuild the surrounding topology by hand || File size for a smooth shape | small | large — smoothness costs triangles || Units | carried in the format (STEP) | not carried by STL at all || What the slicer wants | not this | this | The conversion, and what it destroys The slicer needs a mesh. It slices by intersecting planes with triangles, which is a fast, robust, well-understood operation. So somewhere between CAD and printer, tessellation happens: the BREP's analytic surfaces are approximated by triangles. What is lost: 1. Exactness on curves. A cylinder becomes a prism. This is the polygonal chord error of Post 05: because tessellation places vertices on the true surface, an internal cylinder becomes an inscribed polygon and prints undersize, while an external cylinder becomes inscribed too and prints undersize on its outer dimension. The error is controllable but never zero.2. Feature identity. "This is a hole for an M3 screw" becomes anonymous triangles. Nothing downstream knows it was a hole.3. The feature tree. All design intent, all parameters, all constraints. Gone.4. Editability. You can push a mesh around; you cannot change the diameter of a hole that no longer exists as a concept.5. Exact adjacency, if the tessellator is sloppy — producing the mesh-integrity problems of Chapter 4. The practical rule that follows: keep the BREP as the master. Export a mesh as a build artefact, the way a compiler emits a binary. Never edit the mesh and treat it as the source. If you find yourself repairing an STL, the real fix is upstream. > Watch out: this is why "I downloaded an STL and want to change the hole size" is a hard problem and "I downloaded a STEP and want to change the hole size" is a two-minute job. When choosing what to publish or archive, publish both — the STEP so people can modify it, the mesh so people can print it immediately. Chapter 3 — A parametric workflow that survives editing Sketch discipline A sketch is the 2D profile that features are built from, and it is where most parametric models go wrong. - Fully constrain every sketch. A fully-constrained sketch has zero degrees of freedom; every entity is locked by constraints and dimensions. Most CAD packages colour it differently when it is. An under-constrained sketch will silently change shape when you edit something else — the single largest source of "my model exploded when I changed one number".- Prefer geometric constraints over dimensions. Coincident, collinear, tangent, symmetric, equal. These express intent; a dimension expresses a value. A hole constrained symmetric about the origin stays centred when the part gets wider. A hole dimensioned at 17.5 mm from the left edge does not.- Sketch on planes and on datums, not on model faces, where you can. Sketching on a face creates a dependency on that face's identity; if a later edit removes or splits the face, the sketch loses its reference. This is topological naming, and it is why parametric trees break.- Keep sketches small. One sketch per feature is easier to debug than one heroic sketch containing everything.- Sketch the design intent, not the picture. If two features must always be the same size, constrain them equal rather than dimensioning both to the same number. Mesh export settings — where Post 05's chord error is decided Every tessellator is controlled by two tolerances, whatever the UI calls them: - Chord / linear / deviation tolerance — the maximum distance between the flat facet and the true surface. Smaller ⇒ more triangles ⇒ closer to the real shape. This is the setting that controls hole undersizing.- Angular tolerance — the maximum angle between adjacent facet normals. Controls how many facets a curve gets regardless of its size, which matters on large-radius surfaces where the chord tolerance alone would produce visible flats. The error grows as n falls — obvious, and the reason the default facet count is usually too low.2. Because the polygon is inscribed, an internal cylinder — a hole — is always printed smaller than nominal. Assemblies, mating features and alignment For a printed assembly, alignment features are far more effective than tight tolerances. - Pin-and-socket. A pin on one part, a socket on the other, with your measured . Three pins overconstrain; two pins and one slot is the correct kinematic answer — one pin locates, the slot allows for the pitch error between them.- Tongue-and-groove / lip-and-recess. Aligns two shells, adds stiffness, and hides the parting line.- Dovetails and keyed profiles. Prevent assembly in the wrong orientation. Model them explicitly; do not expect a screw head to sit flush against a printed surface.- Boss-and-recess with generous lead-ins. Every mating feature should have a chamfer at its entry so it self-centres rather than jamming on the first layer's elephant foot.- One adjustable dimension per chain. Scan-to-CAD and reverse engineering Reverse engineering an existing object — to make a bracket that fits a specific part, or to replace something discontinued — has two routes. Measure and remodel. Calipers, a straight edge, a radius gauge, a printed profile gauge, and a fresh parametric model. Slow, and it produces a clean, editable, parameterised solid. A scanner advertising "0.05 mm accuracy" is usually quoting a point accuracy under ideal conditions on an ideal target — often a single-shot figure that says nothing about the accumulated error across a full multi-scan alignment of a real object. Learning Outcome - State the difference between accuracy, precision (repeatability and reproducibility) and resolution, and explain why an advertised "0.05 mm resolution" says essentially nothing about accuracy.- Measure a printed part defensibly: right instrument, controlled force, controlled temperature, repeated readings, recorded uncertainty.- Build an error budget for a printed dimension and name which contributions are systematic and which are random.- Recognise and correct the standard systematic errors: elephant foot, hole undersizing, external-large/internal-small, and X/Y versus Z asymmetry.- Run a test-artefact procedure that produces a machine-and-material-specific offset table you fill in yourself.- Design fits using clearance/transition/interference as a concept, with a printed sweep gauge determining the actual value.- Choose between compensating in CAD and compensating in the slicer, and know which is correct when.- Design so that tolerance stack-up is absorbed by an adjustable dimension rather than demanded of every dimension. Chapter 1 — Saying what you mean Most confusion about printer accuracy is vocabulary confusion. These terms are not synonyms and the rest of the post keeps them strictly distinct. Terminology - Nominal size — the number in the CAD model. A design intent, not a prediction.- Actual size — what a specific physical feature measures, on a specific instrument, at a specific time and temperature.- Deviation (error) — actual minus nominal, signed. Sign matters: a hole 0.15 mm undersize and a hole 0.15 mm oversize are opposite problems with opposite fixes.- Accuracy (formally, trueness) — how close the mean of many measurements is to the true value. Accuracy is about the centre of the distribution.- Precision — how tightly repeated results cluster, independent of whether they cluster in the right place. Precision is about the spread.- Repeatability — spread when the same operator measures the same feature with the same instrument under the same conditions, or when the same machine prints the same part repeatedly without changes.- Reproducibility — spread when something legitimately changes: a different operator, a different instrument, a different day, a new spool of the same filament, the same model on a different printer.- Resolution — the smallest increment an instrument can display, or the smallest increment a machine can command. It is a property of the readout or the motion system, and it bounds nothing else.- Uncertainty — the interval within which the true value plausibly lies, given the instrument, the method and the operator. Always larger than the resolution.- Tolerance — the permitted deviation, decided by the designer from the function of the part. A tolerance is a requirement, not a measurement.- Systematic error (bias) — a repeatable, signed offset. Can be measured and compensated.- Random error — scatter with no fixed sign. Can be characterised statistically and reduced by averaging, but not compensated away. The four-quadrant picture, stated plainly | | Precise | Not precise ||---|---|---|| Accurate | every part right, every time — the goal | on average right, individually unpredictable || Not accurate | every part wrong by the same amount — compensable | every part wrong by a different amount — the hard case | The bottom-left quadrant is the most important thing in this post. A machine that is precise but not accurate is a good machine: the error is systematic, you measure it once, and you compensate for it. A machine that is imprecise cannot be fixed by compensation, because there is no single number to compensate by. So the first diagnostic question is never "is it accurate?" — it is "is it repeatable?" Why "0.05 mm resolution" is a meaningless accuracy claim Marketing copy quotes numbers like "0.0125 mm XY resolution". That figure is a motion increment: the linear distance corresponding to one microstep of a stepper motor, derived from the motor's step angle, the microstepping setting, and the belt pitch or lead screw pitch. It is arithmetic on the kinematics. It says nothing about: - whether the belt is tensioned identically in both directions (backlash),- whether the frame is square,- whether the extruder delivers the commanded volume of plastic (flow),- whether the extrusion width matches what the slicer assumed,- whether the filament is the diameter the slicer was told,- how much the part shrinks as it cools,- whether the motor actually reaches every commanded microstep under load, which at high microstepping ratios it frequently does not. A stepper motor's ability to command a 0.0125 mm move does not mean the toolhead goes there, and even if it did, the plastic that ends up on the plate is not the toolhead path. Treat advertised resolution as a specification of the controller, not of the part. > Watch out: the same confusion appears on the CAD side. A modeller displaying 20.0000 mm is showing you the precision of a floating-point representation, not a claim about anything physical. Digits in a CAD field are free; micrometres on a plate are not. Chapter 2 — The instrument is part of the experiment Before measuring error, establish that the measurement itself is not the largest source of error. On printed polymer parts, it very often is. Calipers versus micrometers | | Digital calipers | Micrometer ||---|---|---|| Typical display resolution | 0.01 mm | 0.001 mm || Realistic uncertainty in normal use | far worse than the display — commonly a few hundredths of a mm | roughly an order of magnitude better || Measuring force | uncontrolled, entirely operator-dependent | controlled by a ratchet or friction thimble || Reach | external, internal, depth, step | external only (for a standard outside micrometer) || Cost | low | moderate || Right for | almost everything in this post | verifying a critical dimension, or calibrating your calipers' bias | The decisive difference is measuring force. Calipers have no force control at all: how hard you squeeze determines the reading. On steel this barely matters. On a printed polymer part — a material with a modulus orders of magnitude below steel, and a surface made of rounded extrusion beads rather than a plane — it matters enormously. Squeezing calipers hard on a PLA cube can easily change the reading by more than the deviation you are trying to measure, and on TPU it is hopeless. A micrometer's ratchet applies a repeatable force. That is why a micrometer is more precise, not the extra digit. > Trick: to find out how much your grip is worth, measure the same feature ten times deliberately gently and ten times deliberately firmly. The difference between the two means is your personal measuring-force error. Everyone should know this number about themselves. It is usually larger than expected. The other things that move the reading - Zero. Close the jaws, check zero, re-zero, before every measurement session and again if you drop the tool. A caliper that drifted 0.03 mm off zero produces a beautifully repeatable, entirely wrong dataset.- Temperature. Polymers have a coefficient of thermal expansion roughly an order of magnitude larger than steel. A part measured warm off the plate and the same part measured cold are different sizes, and your steel calipers are not expanding with it. Let parts equilibrate to room temperature — a couple of hours for anything substantial — and record the room temperature with the data.- Where on the feature. Printed surfaces are not planes. A measurement across the middle of a face, across a corner, at the first layer, and at the top of a 40 mm pillar are four different measurements of the same nominal dimension. Decide a location convention and record it.- Seam and blob. The layer seam is a local bulge. Measuring across a seam gives a systematically larger reading. Either measure away from the seam and say so, or measure across it deliberately and say that.- Bead geometry. The outer surface is a stack of rounded beads, not a flat face. Calipers contact bead crowns; a flat contact averages differently than a knife-edge contact.- Internal features are harder. Caliper internal jaws are stubby and imprecise, and on a hole they measure a chord, not necessarily the diameter. Pin gauges (or, adequately, drill bit shanks of known size, or ground dowel pins) measure a hole far better: the largest pin that enters is a real, functional, repeatable answer. The measurement protocol The whole of the rest of this post depends on measurements taken like this: 1. Let the part reach room temperature. Record the temperature.2. Zero the instrument. Verify against a known reference if you have one (a gauge block, a ground dowel pin, a new drill bit shank).3. Deburr nothing and clean nothing that changes the dimension; do remove obvious stringing and the brim.4. Take at least five readings of each feature, re-seating the instrument each time. Rotate the part between readings where the feature allows.5. Record every reading, not just the one you like.6. Report the mean and the spread (range or standard deviation). The mean estimates the bias; the spread estimates your precision.7. If the spread is comparable to the deviation you are chasing, stop — improve the measurement before drawing any conclusion about the printer. > Common pitfall: measuring once, getting 19.86 on a 20 mm nominal, and concluding "my printer prints 0.14 mm small". One reading contains no information about its own reliability. It could be bias, it could be grip, it could be a seam, it could be the caliper's zero. Five readings tell you which. The general model worth carrying in your head: Fit that model to your own data and you know which knob to turn. Measure one cube and you cannot separate the terms at all. Chapter 4 — The systematic errors, and their actual causes These four account for the large majority of real-world fit failures. Elephant foot Symptom: the bottom one to three layers bulge outward. A part that measures correctly at mid-height is oversize at the base, and it will not sit flat in a recess or slide into a slot. Cause: two things at once. The first layer is deliberately squashed into the plate for adhesion, which displaces material sideways. And the first layers cool against a heated bed that keeps them soft, under the weight and pressure of everything printed above. Fixes, in the order to try them: 1. Get the first-layer Z offset right (Post 01). Over-squish is the root cause; everything else is compensation.2. Chamfer the bottom edge in CAD — a 0.4–0.6 mm chamfer on the bottom perimeter. This is the most robust fix because it survives moving the model to a different printer, a different slicer or a different profile. It costs one CAD feature.3. Slicer elephant-foot compensation — shrinks the first layer's perimeter by a set amount. Effective, but it is a profile setting that does not travel with the model.4. Reduce bed temperature slightly if the material allows. Holes come out undersize Symptom: a 5 mm hole measures under 5 mm, and a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse. This is the single most reported dimensional complaint in FDM, and it has two independent causes that add. Cause A — polygonal chord error. The slicer works on a triangle mesh. A cylindrical hole in CAD becomes an N-sided polygon on export, and the toolpath follows that polygon. Because the mesh vertices sit on the true circle and the polygon edges cut inside it, the printed hole is inscribed in the nominal circle and is therefore smaller. The error grows as the facet count falls and as the hole gets smaller relative to the facet size. This error is created in CAD export, not in the printer — see Post 06 on chord/deviation tolerance. Cause B — extrusion overlap on a concave path. When the nozzle traverses a concave (inward-curving) path, the deposited bead's outer material is compressed toward the inside of the curve. The bead does not simply lie along the commanded centreline; it bulges into the hole. Small holes have tighter curvature, so the effect is larger — which is why a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse than a 12 mm one. This is a real physical effect of extruding a finite-width bead along a curve, and it cannot be fixed in CAD export. Fixes: 1. Export with a tight chord tolerance so the facets are fine (Post 06). This removes Cause A entirely and costs nothing but file size.2. Print, measure, and compensate. Because Cause B depends on hole size, the compensation is a curve, not a constant — hence the sweep in Chapter 5.3. For holes that must fit a fastener or a shaft, drill or ream to size after printing. A printed hole reamed with a standard drill or reamer is dramatically more accurate than any compensation scheme, and this is a legitimate professional answer, not a cop-out.4. Slicer hole compensation where available (Cura's Hole Horizontal Expansion, OrcaSlicer's X-Y hole compensation). Note that PrusaSlicer's XY size compensation is not hole-specific: it grows or shrinks all perimeters, so a positive value that enlarges the outside simultaneously shrinks holes. > Watch out: never design a hole at exactly the nominal shaft size and expect it to fit. A 3 mm hole for a 3 mm shaft will not accept the shaft on any FDM printer without help. Clearance is designed in, not hoped for — Chapter 6. External dimensions large, internal features small Symptom: the outside of the box is 20.15 on a 20 nominal, and the pocket inside it is 19.85 on a 20 nominal. Both errors are the same sign of material excess. Cause: any excess deposited material grows the part outward from the toolpath in both cases. On an external wall that means larger; on an internal wall it means the cavity is smaller. Over-extrusion, an extrusion width larger than the slicer assumed, or a too-hot first layer all produce this signature. This is the diagnostic signature of over-extrusion, and it is why both an external and an internal feature must be in the test artefact. Measuring only external features and compensating them makes internal features worse. X/Y and Z behave differently Z is not the same problem as X and Y and should never be lumped with them. | | X / Y | Z ||---|---|---|| Feature size determined by | extrusion width and toolpath geometry | layer height and the number of layers || Quantisation | effectively continuous | discrete — heights land on multiples of layer height || Typical accuracy | worse — the error budget above | often better, because Z steps are mechanically direct (lead screws) and there is no bead-width term || Error mechanism | flow, width, curvature, compensation | first-layer offset (a fixed error on every Z dimension), plus quantisation || Compensation | XY size compensation / hole compensation | design to layer-height multiples; correct the first-layer offset | The important asymmetry: your first-layer Z offset applies a constant error to every Z dimension in the part, in the same direction, regardless of height. Squish the first layer 0.05 mm extra and every Z dimension is 0.05 mm short. That is a systematic, easily measured, easily corrected error — and it is a completely different animal from the size-proportional errors in X and Y. > Trick: measure a tall feature's height and a short feature's height on the same print. If both are short by the same amount, it is first-layer offset. If the tall one is short by proportionally more, it is steps/mm or thermal contraction. The pattern of the error tells you the cause. Chapter 5 — The calibration-cube fallacy, and what to do instead Why the 20 mm cube proves almost nothing The 20 mm calibration cube is the most-printed object in the hobby and one of the least informative. Consider what it can and cannot tell you. What it does test: whether a 20 mm external dimension in X and in Y comes out near 20 mm, once. What it cannot tell you: - Anything about internal features. It has no holes. It therefore cannot detect the over-extrusion signature above, which is the most common real cause of fit failure.- Anything about size dependence. One nominal size cannot separate the offset term from the scale term of the error model. A cube that measures 20.10 could be a +0.10 mm offset or a +0.5 % scale error; those need completely different fixes and the cube cannot distinguish them.- Anything about repeatability. One cube is n = 1. It contains no information about spread.- Anything about fits. The actual engineering question is almost never "is a 20 mm face 20 mm?" It is "does this 3 mm pin go into this 3 mm hole with the right amount of friction?" The cube is silent on that.- Anything about your other materials or profiles. The result is specific to the filament, temperature, speed and layer height used. A printer can produce a perfect 20 mm cube and still fail to produce a hole a shaft will enter. A 20 mm cube validates a 20 mm cube. The artefact that does the job The replacement is a test artefact that sweeps a range of nominal sizes, in both internal and external features, in all three axes. Steps/mm is determined by belt pitch, pulley tooth count and microstepping — it is arithmetic, and on a correctly built machine it is already right. Changing steps/mm to compensate for a flow error introduces a scale error to cancel an offset error, which cancels correctly at exactly one size and is wrong everywhere else. Chapter 6 — Designing fits The concept, borrowed correctly Mechanical engineering has a mature vocabulary for how two mating features relate. ISO 286 ("Geometrical product specifications (GPS) — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes") defines it: a hole and a shaft each get a tolerance zone, described by a letter (position of the zone relative to nominal) and a grade number (width of the zone, IT01 through IT18). The classic designations — H7/g6, H7/p6 — come from that system, and fits fall into three classes: - Clearance fit — the hole is always larger than the shaft. There is always a gap. Parts assemble by hand and can move or rotate.- Transition fit — the tolerance zones overlap. Depending where each part lands within its zone, the assembly may be slightly loose or slightly tight. Used for accurate location with hand assembly.- Interference (press) fit — the shaft is always larger than the hole. Assembly requires force, heating or cooling, and the joint transmits torque by friction. Now the honest part: ISO 286 fit classes are not directly applicable to FDM printing. Borrow the three concepts; do not copy the values. The reasons are specific: 1. The IT grades assume machining processes with characterised, stable capability. FDM's capability is not that, and it is different for every machine, material and profile.2. The system assumes tolerance zones roughly symmetric about a predictable process centre. FDM's bias is strongly feature-dependent — external and internal features err in opposite directions, and small holes err differently from large ones.3. FDM surfaces are made of rounded beads with layer-scale texture. "Size" on such a surface is less well-defined than on a machined one, which undermines the measurement the system is built on.4. The material is compliant and viscoelastic. A polymer press fit relaxes over time (creep — Post 07); a steel one does not. So: use "I want a clearance fit here and an interference fit there" as design language. Then determine the actual clearance experimentally. The fit-test gauge The right tool is a printed gauge that sweeps clearance in known steps against a known reference. Use a real reference object rather than another printed part, so you are measuring one unknown instead of two: a ground dowel pin, a drill bit shank, an M3 screw, a 608 bearing, a specific connector. Record the result as a judgement, not a number, because the judgement is what you actually want to reproduce: | Clearance | Result | Classification ||---|---|---|| 0.00 | | won't enter / needs force / press || 0.05 | | || 0.10 | | || 0.15 | | || 0.20 | | || 0.25 | | || 0.30 | | || 0.40 | | || 0.50 | | free / sloppy | From one gauge you get your machine's four working numbers: press fit, tight locating fit, sliding fit, free-running fit. Write them into your CAD parameter file as named variables (Post 06) and stop guessing. Three things that change the answer and must be held constant or re-tested: - Material. PETG and PLA do not give the same clearances. Neither do two colours (Post 04).- Engagement depth. A 2 mm-deep hole and a 20 mm-deep hole with identical diameters behave completely differently. Test at a realistic depth.- Orientation. A hole printed with its axis vertical (a circle in the layer plane) and one printed horizontally (an arch, with bridging at the top) are different features with different errors. Test the orientation you will use. > Trick: design a deliberately adjustable interface where you can — a slot instead of a hole, a screw-clamped collar instead of a press fit, a shim pocket. One adjustable feature absorbs the whole tolerance stack of the assembly and turns a metrology problem into a five-second assembly step. Compensating in CAD versus in the slicer Both are legitimate. They belong in different places, and mixing them without noticing is how people end up double-compensating. | Compensate in CAD | Compensate in the slicer ||---|---|| The correction is a design decision: a functional clearance, a bottom chamfer, a wall thickness set to a multiple of extrusion width | The correction is a machine/process characteristic: your printer's systematic offset for this material and profile || Travels with the model to any printer, slicer or person | Lives in the profile; correctly re-applied to every model you print with that profile || Correct for: fit clearances, chamfers, thread reliefs, features sized to nozzle geometry | Correct for: global XY size compensation, hole compensation, elephant-foot compensation || Wrong for: your machine's private bias — you would bake a local defect into a shared file | Wrong for: functional clearances — the slicer setting applies to every feature, including the ones that were already fine | The rule: design intent goes in CAD, machine bias goes in the slicer. A clearance of 0.2 mm because a shaft must rotate is design intent — it belongs in the model and should be a named parameter. A −0.06 mm XY compensation because your extruder runs slightly hot is machine bias — it belongs in the profile, and it should not follow the model to someone else's printer. > Watch out: if you compensate in the slicer and then adjust the model because parts still do not fit, you now have two corrections that must be maintained together, and neither is documented. Pick one place per cause, and write down what you did. Chapter 7 — Stack-up, standards, and the argument Tolerance stack-up Errors add along an assembly path. Three parts stacked, each within ±0.15 mm, can be ±0.45 mm out at the end in the worst case. Design a mechanism as a chain of five tightly-toleranced printed features and you have designed something that will not assemble. Two ways to add up the contributions: - Worst case (arithmetic sum). Add all the tolerances. Guarantees assembly, and is conservative — it assumes every part is simultaneously at its extreme, which is unlikely.- Statistical (root-sum-square). If the individual errors are independent and roughly centred, the combined spread grows like the square root of the sum of squares — much smaller than the arithmetic sum. Valid only when the errors really are independent and centred, and FDM errors often are neither: a whole batch printed from one spool on one day shares the same bias, which is precisely the correlated case RSS is invalid for. For hobby and small-batch printing, the honest position is: use worst-case reasoning, and design so that the worst case is absorbed rather than resisted. Techniques that absorb stack-up, roughly in order of usefulness: 1. Make one dimension in the chain adjustable. A slot, an oversized clearance hole with a washer, a screw-adjusted stop, a shim. One adjustable feature is worth ten tight tolerances.2. Shorten the chain. Print two mating features as one part rather than three parts that must align. This is FDM's genuine superiority over machining — arbitrary geometry is free, so consolidate.3. Locate on a datum, not on an accumulation. Reference every feature to one face rather than each to the previous one, so errors do not accumulate down the chain.4. Use compliance deliberately. A printed snap-fit, a flexure, a slightly springy tab tolerates variation that a rigid interface will not.5. Post-process the one critical feature. Ream the bearing hole. Tap the thread. Face the mating surface flat with sandpaper on a granite tile. One machined feature on an otherwise printed part is cheap and effective. The closing argument Everything above converges on one position, and it is the same position the planned furniture-CAD series will argue about woodworking: CAD precision does not survive contact with the physical world. The number in the model is exact. The part is not, and the material is not, and the machine is not, and the measurement is not. Every physical process has a distribution of outcomes, and the designer's job is not to eliminate that distribution — that is impossible — but to design something that works across it. Three consequences follow, and they are the practical content of the whole post: 1. A design that only works if a tolerance is met is a fragile design. If the assembly requires a 0.1 mm clearance and the process delivers ±0.15 mm, it will fail, and no amount of care changes that. Redesign the interface — make it adjustable, make it compliant, or consolidate the parts — rather than demanding a capability the process does not have.2. Measure your own process, then design inside what it can do. The offset and scale you fit in Chapter 5, and the clearances you found in Chapter 6, are your process capability. They are the design constraint. Someone else's numbers are not data about your machine.3. Where precision is genuinely required, get it from a process that has it. Ream the hole. Tap the thread. Press in a heat-set insert. Use a bought bearing, a bought shaft, a bought fastener. FDM's strength is arbitrary geometry produced quickly and cheaply, not precision — and combining it with a precise process at the two or three points that need precision is a better engineering answer than fighting the printer for a capability it does not have. The most valuable skill in this entire course is not making an accurate print. It is knowing which dimensions need to be accurate — usually far fewer than beginners assume — and designing so the rest do not have to be. Posts 01–03 treated filament as an undifferentiated grey thread that melts. It is not. The material is the single largest lever over what a printed part can survive, and most of the surprises beginners hit — a bracket that sags in a car, a case that cracks on the bench, a print that curls off the plate at layer 40, a spool that spits and pops — are material physics, not printer faults. This post covers the polymer science that actually predicts printing behaviour, a family-by-family comparison of the common filaments and resins, and the operational side that nobody puts on the label: moisture, abrasion, annealing, pigment, and emissions. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials (this post) → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - Explain glass transition temperature (Tg) and melting behaviour, and state why Tg — not the melting point — sets a printed part's real service temperature.- Distinguish amorphous from semi-crystalline polymers and predict warping, shrinkage and enclosure requirements from that distinction alone.- Read a filament comparison table as typical published ranges and know that the spool's own datasheet overrides every table, including this one.- Diagnose wet filament by sound, surface and cross-section, and dry and store it correctly.- Choose nozzle hardware for abrasive filaments and pick a soluble support material that is compatible with the model material.- Decide whether annealing helps or ruins a given part, and account for the shrinkage it causes.- Select a material from a stated requirement rather than from habit, and print it with appropriate ventilation. Chapter 1 — The polymer physics that predicts printing behaviour Almost everything a filament does on the machine follows from two thermal transitions and one question about molecular order. Terminology - Glass transition temperature (Tg) — the temperature range over which an amorphous polymer (or the amorphous fraction of a semi-crystalline one) changes from a rigid, glassy solid into a soft, rubbery one. It is not a phase change with latent heat; it is a change in molecular mobility. Tg is a range, and the number a datasheet reports depends on the measurement method (DSC, DMA) and the heating rate.- Melting temperature (Tm) — the temperature at which crystalline regions melt. Only semi-crystalline polymers have a true Tm. Purely amorphous polymers soften progressively and have no sharp melting point.- Amorphous polymer — chains arranged with no long-range order, frozen in a tangle. Examples used in printing: ABS, ASA, PC, PETG (largely amorphous by design), most photopolymer resins after cure.- Semi-crystalline polymer — contains ordered crystalline domains embedded in amorphous regions. Examples: PLA (slow-crystallising, usually printed nearly amorphous), the nylons (PA6, PA12), PP, PEEK.- Heat deflection temperature (HDT) — the temperature at which a standard test bar deflects a specified amount under a specified load. A load-dependent engineering figure, not a material constant; always quoted with its stress level (commonly 0.45 MPa or 1.8 MPa).- Crystallinity — the mass fraction of the polymer that is in ordered crystalline domains. It changes with cooling rate and with annealing, and it drives stiffness, chemical resistance, opacity and shrinkage. Why Tg, not Tm, sets the service temperature A printed part carries load through solid polymer. Above Tg the amorphous fraction becomes rubbery: modulus drops by orders of magnitude, and a part that was rigid at room temperature will sag, creep and lose dimensional stability long before anything melts. This is why a PLA phone mount fails in a car parked in the sun even though PLA does not melt until far higher: the interior of a closed car in direct sun readily reaches the low end of PLA's Tg range. Three consequences worth internalising: 1. Compare service temperature against Tg, with margin. Treat Tg as the ceiling, and derate further if the part is loaded — polymers creep faster as they approach Tg (Post 07).2. Tm is a printing parameter, not a service parameter. It tells you roughly where the extruder must operate. It tells you nothing useful about whether a bracket survives on an engine bay.3. HDT is closer to what you want but still not it. HDT is measured at a specific stress on a specific bar geometry, in a solid moulded specimen — not a 3-perimeter, 15 %-infill print with anisotropic layer bonding. > Watch out: the Tg values circulating in the hobby community for a given material vary by 10–20 °C between sources, because different grades, different additives and different measurement methods genuinely give different numbers. Use them as a family ranking (PLA < PETG < ABS/ASA < PC), and use the specific spool's technical datasheet for anything that matters. Chapter 3 — Warping, shrinkage and the enclosure question Warping is not a mystery and it is not a bed-adhesion problem in origin — bed adhesion is only where the symptom appears. The physics Every polymer contracts as it cools. In a printed part, that contraction happens unevenly in time: the layer being deposited is at nozzle temperature, the layer below is somewhere between, and the first layer has been sitting on a heated plate for an hour. Each new layer solidifies and then tries to contract while bonded to a layer beneath that has already finished contracting and will not move. The result is a stress field locked into the part, tensile in the newer material, and it grows with every layer. That locked-in stress has to go somewhere. It is relieved by: - Curling — the free edges of the part lift, which is geometrically the cheapest way to relieve it. Corners lift first because they have two free directions.- Delamination — a layer interface splits, usually on a tall thin part or at a stress raiser.- Bed release — the whole part peels off the plate. Two independent quantities drive the magnitude: the total contraction from nozzle to room temperature (roughly the coefficient of thermal expansion times the temperature drop, plus any crystallisation shrinkage) and the temperature gradient through the part while printing. Why ABS needs an enclosure and PLA does not Run the comparison: - PLA is printed around 200 °C and, being effectively amorphous as printed, contracts modestly. Its Tg is around 55–65 °C, so the part becomes rigid at a temperature only a little above the bed, meaning the stress-accumulating window is short and the accumulated stress is small. An enclosure adds nothing and can hurt: it raises the temperature around the heatsink and hotend, promoting heat creep and jams.- ABS is printed around 250 °C, has a Tg around 100–110 °C, and contracts substantially. Its Tg is far above room temperature, so the part spends a long stretch of its cooling path below Tg — rigid, storing stress rather than relaxing it. An enclosure holds the chamber high enough that the part stays near or above Tg — mobile, able to relax stress — for longer, and it removes the gradients caused by room draughts. That is the mechanism: the enclosure is a stress-relief oven, not a heater for the plastic. Consequences that follow directly: | Symptom | Mechanism | Fix in order of leverage ||---|---|---|| Corners lift on a large flat ABS part | high contraction plus draught-driven gradients | enclosure; raise chamber temperature; brim; reduce part footprint or split the part || Part releases mid-print, PLA | adhesion, not warp | clean the plate (IPA, then soap and water for oils); first-layer squish (Post 01) || Tall PC part delaminates near the top | chamber cools with height; interface bonding falls | enclosure with an actively warm chamber; raise nozzle temperature; reduce cooling || Nylon curls despite an enclosure | crystallisation shrinkage plus moisture | dry the filament (Chapter 4) before blaming warp || Fibre-filled version of the same polymer warps less | fibres restrain matrix contraction | expected — this is a genuine benefit of CF/GF grades | > Common pitfall: adding a brim or raft to fight ABS warp. It buys a little and hides the cause. The stress is still in the part; you have merely bonded it harder to the plate, which converts corner lift into internal stress or a delamination crack higher up. Fix the thermal environment. > Trick: you can see the locked-in stress. Print a thin ABS bar without an enclosure, let it cool fully, then cut it lengthwise with a sharp blade. The two halves spring apart. A bar printed in a warm enclosure moves much less. This is a five-minute experiment that makes the whole chapter concrete. Chapter 4 — Moisture, the invisible variable Which polymers care Hygroscopic means the polymer absorbs water from air into its bulk, not just onto its surface. In rough order of severity for printing filaments: | Severity | Materials | Practical consequence ||---|---|---|| Extreme | PVA, PA (nylon), PC | unprintable within hours to days of open-air exposure in a humid room || High | TPU, PETG, ASA | visible surface and strength degradation over days to weeks || Moderate | ABS, PLA | degrades over weeks to months; PLA additionally hydrolyses when hot and wet, permanently shortening chains || Low | dried, sealed, unopened spools of anything | the state you want to maintain | The hydrolysis point matters and is often missed: for PLA and the polyesters, water is not just a bubble source. At extrusion temperature, absorbed water chemically cleaves the polymer chains. Drying removes the water but does not undo the chain scission. Wet-then-dried PLA prints acceptably and is measurably weaker than it should be. What wet filament looks and sounds like | Sense | Signature ||---|---|| Sound | intermittent popping, crackling, hissing at the nozzle — steam escaping || Extrusion | visible steam or micro-bubbles; the strand from a hand-purge is foamy or wavy rather than smooth || Surface | rough, matte, pitted; PETG turns hazy and blistered rather than glossy || Stringing | dramatically worse, and unresponsive to retraction tuning || Dimensions | inconsistent extrusion width; unpredictable flow calibration (this contaminates the Post 05 measurements) || Strength | reduced, often severely, especially interlayer || Cross-section | cut a purged strand: voids and bubbles inside the bead | > Common pitfall: tuning retraction for hours to chase stringing that is caused by moisture. Before any retraction tuning session, do the hand-purge test: extrude 100 mm by hand and look and listen. If it pops, dry the spool first and start the tuning session over. Drying Drying is a diffusion process: temperature sets the rate, time lets the water get out of the core of the spool. Getting the temperature wrong in the other direction fuses the spool. That is why nylon tolerates 80–90 °C and PLA does not — nylon's amorphous fraction is not the load-bearing part of a spool wound from a semi-crystalline polymer, while PLA at 70 °C is above Tg and structurally soft. Equipment, ranked by how well it works: 1. Purpose-built filament dryer — controlled temperature, adequate airflow, can print directly from the box. The right answer if you print hygroscopic materials at all.2. Food dehydrator — cheap, good airflow, often needs the shelves cut out to fit a spool. Verify the actual temperature with a separate thermometer; dial markings on cheap units are frequently wrong by 10 °C or more.3. Convection oven — workable if it can hold a low temperature accurately. Many domestic ovens cannot regulate below 70–80 °C and cycle far above the setpoint. Verify with a thermometer before trusting it with a spool.4. The printer's heated bed with a box over it — a last resort, poorly controlled. Silica gel in a sealed box maintains dryness; it does not dry a wet spool in any reasonable time. Dry first, then store. Storage that actually works - Sealed container (vacuum bag or gasketed box) with indicating desiccant, plus a cheap hygrometer inside so you can see whether the system works. A spool that has sat in a warehouse for two years is not automatically dry. Chapter 5 — Abrasives, multi-material adhesion, and soluble supports Abrasive filaments and nozzle wear Carbon fibre, glass fibre, metal-filled, wood-filled, glow-in-the-dark (glass or strontium aluminate particles) and many "sparkle" or "silk-matte" filaments contain hard particles. A standard brass nozzle is soft; those particles cut it. The failure is insidious: the orifice grows and goes out-of-round gradually, so extrusion width drifts, first layers degrade, and dimensional accuracy quietly gets worse over weeks — exactly the sort of slow drift that invalidates the calibration you did in Post 01. | Nozzle material | Abrasion resistance | Thermal conductivity | Notes ||---|---|---|---|| Brass | poor | excellent | fine for PLA/PETG/ABS; wears fast on any filled filament || Hardened steel | good | lower — may need +5–10 °C | the common budget answer || Steel with wear coating | good | lower | varies enormously by product || Tungsten carbide / ruby-tipped | excellent | varies by construction | expensive; the long-run answer for a fibre-heavy workflow | > Trick: keep one nozzle dedicated to abrasives and one to everything else, and label them. Soluble supports and their own moisture problems - PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) — dissolves in plain water. Compatible with PLA and (with care) PETG. It is the most hygroscopic common filament by a wide margin: it will absorb enough moisture to become unprintable within hours in a humid room, and wet PVA foams, oozes, jams and clogs. PVA requires a dry box feeding the printer, not merely a dried spool. It also degrades in the hotend if held hot and idle.- HIPS (high-impact polystyrene) — dissolves in d-limonene, not water. For everything else, revisit orientation (Post 03) first. Support material is a solution to a geometry problem you may not need to have. Chapter 6 — Properties you set after the print: annealing, post-cure, pigment Annealing Annealing is holding a printed part at an elevated temperature — above Tg but below the melting/softening point — for a period, then cooling it slowly. For a semi-crystalline polymer printed too fast to crystallise (PLA is the archetype), it does three things at once: 1. Increases crystallinity. Chains gain mobility above Tg and organise into crystalline domains. Stiffness, heat resistance and chemical resistance rise. Annealed PLA can retain useful stiffness meaningfully above its as-printed service ceiling — how much depends on the grade, the additives (nucleating agents in "HT" PLA grades are there specifically to make this work) and the schedule.2. Relieves internal stress. The locked-in stress from Chapter 3 relaxes. Good for stability, and it is why the part changes shape.3. Improves interlayer bonding somewhat. Chains interdiffuse across layer interfaces given mobility and time, reducing the Z-direction weakness of Post 07 — reducing, not eliminating. The cost is dimensional change, and it is not small. Crystallisation densifies the polymer, so the part shrinks — and it shrinks anisotropically, because the print's own layer structure and residual stress field are anisotropic. Parts also sag under their own weight if unsupported above Tg. Practical annealing procedure, if you decide the part needs it: 1. Decide first whether it does. Annealing is worth it for heat-exposed and stiffness-critical PLA parts. It is not worth it for anything that has to fit something else, unless you are prepared to re-measure and iterate.2. Support the part fully — bury it in dry sand, salt or plaster, or lay it on a flat plate with nothing overhanging.3. Hold above Tg but below softening: a common starting window for PLA is roughly 80–110 °C. Longer at a lower temperature is safer than shorter at a higher one.4. Cool slowly in the oven. Quenching re-introduces the stress you just removed.5. Measure the same features before and after and record the change as a percentage. That percentage is your machine-plus-material-plus-schedule constant, and you scale the CAD model by it next time. It is not a number anyone can give you — Post 05 gives the measurement discipline. > Watch out: annealing an amorphous polymer (ABS, PETG, PC) does not increase crystallinity, because there is no crystalline phase to grow. It still relieves stress, which can be useful, and it still causes dimensional change. Do not expect the stiffness gain that PLA shows. Resin families and post-cure Vat photopolymerisation (Post 08 in the extension plan will go deep) has a materials landscape of its own. The critical structural fact: for photopolymer resin, a large share of the part's final mechanical properties is set by post-processing, not by printing. A green part straight out of the vat is under-polymerised, soft, and full of uncured monomer. Wash and post-cure are part of the process, not optional finishing. | Family | Character | Typical use ||---|---|---|| Standard / "general purpose" | high detail, hard, brittle | display models, miniatures, figures || Tough / "ABS-like" | more impact energy absorbed, lower detail fidelity, softer | prototype functional parts || Water-washable | cleans in water instead of IPA | convenience — but generally lower mechanical properties, and the wash water is contaminated waste, not drain-safe || Engineering (high-temp, rigid, castable, flexible, dental) | narrow, specified properties | the specified job, per the vendor's validated workflow | Post-cure variables that change the result: cure time, cure temperature (warm post-cure typically outperforms room-temperature cure), wavelength, whether the part is cured in water or in air (oxygen inhibits surface cure — curing submerged is a common trick), and part thickness. Vendors publish validated schedules; deviating from them makes their published mechanical data inapplicable to your part. > Common pitfall: treating resin mechanical datasheets as if they described your part. Those numbers come from specimens cured on the vendor's validated schedule. An under-cured part is dramatically weaker; an over-cured part is more brittle. Post-cure is the variable, and it is yours to control. Colour, pigment and additives The single most under-appreciated fact about filament: colour is not cosmetic. The pigments, fillers and processing aids change the polymer's behaviour. - Thermal behaviour. Pigment loading changes melt viscosity and thermal conductivity. It is normal for two colours from the same vendor and the same product line to want different nozzle temperatures — sometimes by 10 °C or more.- Crystallisation. Some pigments act as nucleating agents. This changes crystallisation rate, which changes shrinkage and warping. Black and white parts of the same nominal material genuinely do not always behave the same.- Abrasion. Metallics, "silk", glitter and glow additives are abrasive. See Chapter 5.- Strength. High filler loading dilutes the polymer matrix. Heavily filled decorative filaments are usually weaker than the plain version of the same material.- Surface finish. Matte finishes come from filler particles that scatter light; they also hide layer lines, which is genuinely useful and is a materials effect, not a slicer effect.- UV behaviour. Carbon black is an effective UV absorber; black parts often outlast light-coloured ones of the same polymer outdoors. This is not a reason to skip ASA for an outdoor part, but it is a real effect. > Trick: when you switch colour within the same product line, re-run a temperature tower and a flow check before assuming your profile transfers. It costs twenty minutes and explains a surprising number of "the printer suddenly got worse" reports. Chapter 7 — Choosing a material, and printing it safely The decision tree Start from the requirement, not the material. Read down until a branch matches. Two meta-rules that outrank the tree: 1. Prototype in PLA regardless of the final material. Iterate the geometry cheaply and fast, then reprint the final version in the material the job needs. Every step up in material difficulty costs enclosure, drying, nozzle hardware, tuning time and failed prints. Only pay that when the requirement demands it. Chapter 4 — : lazy, cached, and quiet when nothing changed Laziness and caching A computed is both a subscriber (it reads deps) and a dep (effects read it).
