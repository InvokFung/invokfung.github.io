Abstract Algebra — 05 · Field Extensions and Galois Theory
Created2026-07-28|Mathematics Abstract Algebra
Post 04 ended by building new fields: $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$ and $\mathbb{F}4 = \mathbb{F}2[x]/(x^2+x+1)$. This post studies such constructions systematically. A bigger field $E$ containing $F$ is a vector space over $F$, and its dimension, the degree, turns out to be a very strong invariant: a single divisibility argument proves that no ruler-and-compass construction doubles a cube or trisects a 60° angle. Galois theory then attaches to a polynomial a finite group, the permutations of its roots that respect all algebraic relations, and the Fundamental Theorem matches the subfields of the splitting field with the subgroups of that group. Everything from posts 01–04 comes together here: groups, normal subgroups and quotients, group actions, Sylow-type counting, irreducible polynomials and quotient rings. The chapter ends with the reason there is no quintic formula. > Series map: 00 Integers, Relations & Modular Arithmetic → 01 Groups & Subgroups → 02 Cosets, Lagrange & Quotient Groups → 03 Homomorphisms, Group Actions & Sylow → 04 Rings, Ideals & Fields → 05 Field Extensions & Galois Theory (this post). Learning Outcome - Compute the degree of a field extension, and prove and use the tower law.- Find minimal polynomials, and prove that $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m\alpha)$ with degree $\deg m\alpha$.- Show that $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ has degree 4 and is generated by $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$.- Prove that doubling the cube and trisecting a 60° angle are impossible with ruler and compass.- Build splitting fields, and prove that there is one finite field of each prime-power order, up to isomorphism.- Define separable, normal and Galois extensions and the Galois group.- State the Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory and draw the subfield/subgroup lattices for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ and for the splitting field of $x^3 - 2$.- Define solvable groups and outline why the roots of $x^5 - 4x + 2$ cannot be written with radicals. Chapter 1 — Field extensions and degree Terminology - Extension — a pair of fields $F \subseteq E$, written $E/F$ ("$E$ over $F$"). $F$ is the base field.- Degree — $[E : F] = \dimF E$, the dimension of $E$ as a vector space over $F$. The extension is finite if the degree is finite.- Generated extension — for $\alpha \in E$, $F(\alpha)$ is the smallest subfield of $E$ containing $F$ and $\alpha$. Likewise $F(\alpha1, \dots, \alphan)$. An extension $F(\alpha)/F$ is simple and $\alpha$ is a primitive element.- Intermediate field — a field $K$ with $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$. Examples: $[\mathbb{C} : \mathbb{R}] = 2$ with basis $1, i$. $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$ with basis $1, \sqrt2$, since $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) = \{a + b\sqrt2 : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ and $\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$. $[\mathbb{R} : \mathbb{Q}]$ is infinite (a countable field cannot span the uncountable $\mathbb{R}$ with finitely many vectors). A field with $q$ elements that contains $\mathbb{F}p$ has $q = p^n$ where $n = [E : \mathbb{F}p]$, because an $n$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}p$ has $p^n$ elements. The tower law Theorem (tower law). If $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$ are fields, then $$[E : F] = [E : K]\,[K : F],$$ in the sense that the left side is finite exactly when both factors are, and then equality holds. Proof (finite case). Let $\alpha1, \dots, \alpham$ be a basis of $K$ over $F$ and $\beta1, \dots, \betan$ a basis of $E$ over $K$. The claim is that the $mn$ products $\alphai\betaj$ form a basis of $E$ over $F$. - Spanning. Any $e \in E$ is $\sumj kj \betaj$ with $kj \in K$, and each $kj = \sumi f{ij}\alphai$ with $f{ij} \in F$. So $e = \sum{i,j} f{ij}\,\alphai\betaj$.- Independence. If $\sum{i,j} f{ij}\alphai\betaj = 0$, regroup as $\sumj \big(\sumi f{ij}\alphai\big)\betaj = 0$. The inner sums are in $K$ and the $\betaj$ are independent over $K$, so each inner sum is $0$. The $\alphai$ are independent over $F$, so every $f{ij} = 0$. ∎ Corollaries. (1) If $K$ is intermediate in a finite extension $E/F$, then $[K : F]$ divides $[E : F]$. (2) If $[E : F]$ is prime, there is no intermediate field other than $F$ and $E$. (3) If $\alpha \in E$, then $[F(\alpha) : F]$ divides $[E : F]$. > Trick: most degree computations are "bound above by a tower, bound below by divisibility". If $[K : F] = 2$ and $[L : F] = 3$ are both inside $E$, then $6 \mid [E : F]$. Chapter 2 — Algebraic elements and minimal polynomials Terminology - Algebraic — $\alpha \in E$ is algebraic over $F$ if $f(\alpha) = 0$ for some nonzero $f \in F[x]$. Otherwise $\alpha$ is transcendental over $F$.- Algebraic extension — every element of $E$ is algebraic over $F$.- Minimal polynomial — the monic polynomial $m\alpha \in F[x]$ of least degree with $m\alpha(\alpha) = 0$. $\sqrt2$, $i$, $\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $e^{2\pi i/5}$ are algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hermite proved in 1873 that $e$ is transcendental and Lindemann in 1882 that $\pi$ is transcendental; both proofs are analytic and are not needed below. The minimal polynomial Theorem. Let $\alpha$ be algebraic over $F$. Then: 1. $m\alpha$ exists, is unique, and is irreducible over $F$;2. for $f \in F[x]$: $f(\alpha) = 0$ if and only if $m\alpha \mid f$;3. $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(m\alpha)$, so $[F(\alpha) : F] = \deg m\alpha$, with basis $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}$ where $n = \deg m\alpha$. Proof. Evaluation $\mathrm{ev}\alpha : F[x] \to E$, $f \mapsto f(\alpha)$, is a ring homomorphism. Its kernel $I$ is nonzero because $\alpha$ is algebraic, and $F[x]$ is a PID (post 04), so $I = (m)$ for a unique monic $m$; this $m$ has least degree among nonzero elements of $I$, so $m = m\alpha$, and statement 2 is "$f \in I \iff m\alpha \mid f$". The image of $\mathrm{ev}\alpha$ is a subring of the field $E$, hence a domain, so by the First Isomorphism Theorem $F[x]/(m\alpha)$ is a domain: $(m\alpha)$ is prime, so $m\alpha$ is irreducible. Then $(m\alpha)$ is maximal (post 04, Chapter 6), so the image $F[\alpha]$ is a field containing $F$ and $\alpha$; it is therefore $F(\alpha)$. The basis claim is the division algorithm: every class has a unique representative of degree $< n$. ∎ So to find $[F(\alpha) : F]$: find a polynomial with root $\alpha$ and prove it irreducible with the tests of post 04. > Common pitfall: the minimal polynomial depends on the base field. $m{\sqrt2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x^2 - 2$; over $\mathbb{R}$ it is $x - \sqrt2$. Arithmetic in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$. Let $a = \sqrt[3]{2}$, with $ma = x^3 - 2$ (Eisenstein at 2), so $[\mathbb{Q}(a) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3$ with basis $1, a, a^2$. To invert $1 + a$, use $(1 + a)(1 - a + a^2) = 1 + a^3 = 3$: $$\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt[3]{2}} = \frac{1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}}{3}.$$ Finite implies algebraic. If $[E : F] = n$ and $\alpha \in E$, the $n + 1$ elements $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^n$ are linearly dependent over $F$, and a dependence relation is a nonzero polynomial with root $\alpha$. Consequently, if $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are algebraic then $F(\alpha, \beta)$ is finite (tower law), so $\alpha \pm \beta$, $\alpha\beta$ and $\alpha/\beta$ are algebraic too: the algebraic numbers form a field. Worked example: Q(√2, √3) Degree. $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2$. Next, $\sqrt3 \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$: if $(a + b\sqrt2)^2 = 3$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $a^2 + 2b^2 = 3$ and $2ab = 0$. $b = 0$ gives $a^2 = 3$ and $a = 0$ gives $b^2 = 3/2$, neither possible in $\mathbb{Q}$. So $x^2 - 3$ stays irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, and by the tower law $$[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3) : \mathbb{Q}] = [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3) : \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)]\,[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) : \mathbb{Q}] = 2 \cdot 2 = 4,$$ with basis $1, \sqrt2, \sqrt3, \sqrt6$ (products of the two bases, as in the proof of the tower law). A primitive element. Let $\theta = \sqrt2 + \sqrt3$. Then $\theta^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt6$, so $(\theta^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, that is $$\theta^4 - 10\theta^2 + 1 = 0.$$ Also $\theta^3 = 11\sqrt2 + 9\sqrt3$, which gives $\sqrt2 = (\theta^3 - 9\theta)/2$ and $\sqrt3 = (11\theta - \theta^3)/2$. So $\mathbb{Q}(\theta)$ contains $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$, hence $\mathbb{Q}(\theta) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ has degree 4. A degree-4 monic polynomial with root $\theta$ must then be the minimal polynomial: $m\theta = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$, irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (its four roots are $\pm\sqrt2 \pm \sqrt3$). This is an instance of the primitive element theorem: every finite separable extension (Chapter 5; in particular every finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$) is simple. Chapter 3 — Ruler and compass Start with the points $0$ and $1$ in the plane $\mathbb{C}$. A ruler draws the line through two constructed points; a compass draws a circle with a constructed centre through a constructed point. New points are intersections of lines and circles. A real number is constructible if it is a coordinate of a constructible point. Key observation. If all points so far have coordinates in a field $K$, then: - the intersection of two lines solves a linear system, so it has coordinates in $K$;- an intersection involving a circle solves a quadratic equation over $K$, so it has coordinates in $K$ or in some $K(\sqrt d)$ with $d \in K$, an extension of degree 2. Theorem. If $\alpha$ is constructible, then $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of 2. Proof. By the observation, $\alpha$ lies in the top field of a tower $\mathbb{Q} = K0 \subset K1 \subset \cdots \subset Kr$ with each $[K{i+1} : Ki] = 2$. By the tower law $[Kr : \mathbb{Q}] = 2^r$, and $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}]$ divides it. ∎ Doubling the cube. A cube of volume 2 needs side $\sqrt[3]{2}$, whose minimal polynomial $x^3 - 2$ has degree 3. Not a power of 2, so impossible. Trisecting 60°. A 60° angle is constructible (equilateral triangle). Trisecting it means constructing $c = \cos 20^\circ$. From $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$ with $\theta = 20^\circ$ and $\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12$: $$4c^3 - 3c = \tfrac12 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 8c^3 - 6c - 1 = 0.$$ By the rational root test, a rational root would be one of $\pm1, \pm\tfrac12, \pm\tfrac14, \pm\tfrac18$; substituting gives $1, -3, -3, 1, -\tfrac{19}{8}, \tfrac38, -\tfrac{111}{64}, -\tfrac{17}{64}$, never $0$. A cubic without rational roots is irreducible, so $[\mathbb{Q}(c) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3$ and the trisection is impossible. (Some angles, such as 90°, can be trisected; the theorem is that no method works for every angle.) Squaring the circle. A square of area $\pi$ needs side $\sqrt\pi$. If $\sqrt\pi$ were constructible it would be algebraic, so $\pi$ would be algebraic, contradicting Lindemann. > Watch out: "degree a power of 2" is necessary, not sufficient. The exact criterion is that the splitting field of the minimal polynomial has degree a power of 2. The Gauss–Wantzel theorem answers the polygon question: a regular $n$-gon is constructible if and only if $n$ is a power of 2 times a product of distinct Fermat primes $3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$. Chapter 4 — Splitting fields and finite fields Splitting fields A splitting field of a nonconstant $f \in F[x]$ is an extension $E/F$ in which $f$ factors into linear factors, $f = c(x - \alpha1)\cdots(x - \alphan)$, and which is generated by the roots: $E = F(\alpha1, \dots, \alphan)$. Theorem. Every nonconstant $f \in F[x]$ has a splitting field $E$, with $[E : F] \le n!$ where $n = \deg f$. Any two splitting fields of $f$ over $F$ are isomorphic by an isomorphism that fixes $F$. (Existence: take an irreducible factor $p$ of $f$; in $F[x]/(p)$ it has a root (post 04), so $f$ gains a linear factor over an extension of degree $\le n$; repeat on the quotient, whose degree is $n - 1$. Uniqueness is proved by the same induction, extending an isomorphism one root at a time.) Examples: - $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, degree 2.- $x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$: roots $\pm\sqrt2 \pm \sqrt3$, splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$, degree 4.- $x^3 - 2$: roots $\alpha$, $\alpha\omega$, $\alpha\omega^2$ with $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, a root of $x^2 + x + 1$. The splitting field is $E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \omega)$ (it contains $\omega = \alpha\omega/\alpha$). $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3$, and $\omega \notin \mathbb{Q}(\alpha) \subset \mathbb{R}$, so $[E : \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)] = 2$. Hence $[E : \mathbb{Q}] = 6 = 3!$, the maximum. Finite fields Let $E$ be a finite field. Its characteristic is a prime $p$ (post 04), so it contains $\mathbb{F}p$ and has $p^n$ elements, $n = [E : \mathbb{F}p]$. Lemma (freshman's dream). In a commutative ring of characteristic $p$, $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$. Proof. Expand with the binomial theorem. For $0 < k < p$, $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p!}{k!(p-k)!}$ is divisible by $p$: $p$ divides the numerator but not the denominator. So all middle terms vanish. ∎ Hence the Frobenius map $\mathrm{Fr}(a) = a^p$ is a ring homomorphism. On a field it is injective (post 04: homomorphisms out of fields are injective), so on a finite field it is an automorphism, and it fixes exactly the elements of $\mathbb{F}p$ (the roots of $x^p - x$; there are at most $p$ of them, and all of $\mathbb{F}p$ qualifies by Fermat's little theorem from post 00). Theorem (existence and uniqueness of finite fields). For every prime $p$ and $n \ge 1$ there is a field with $q = p^n$ elements, and any two such fields are isomorphic. It is written $\mathbb{F}q$. Proof. Existence. Let $E$ be a splitting field of $g = x^q - x$ over $\mathbb{F}p$, and $S$ the set of roots of $g$ in $E$. The derivative $g' = qx^{q-1} - 1 = -1$ has no common root with $g$, so the roots are distinct and $|S| = q$. $S$ is a subfield: if $a^q = a$ and $b^q = b$, then $(a + b)^q = a^q + b^q = a + b$ (apply the lemma $n$ times), $(ab)^q = ab$, and $(a^{-1})^q = a^{-1}$. A subfield containing all roots is the whole splitting field, so $E = S$ has $q$ elements. Uniqueness. In any field $K$ with $q$ elements, the multiplicative group has order $q - 1$, so $a^{q-1} = 1$ for $a \ne 0$ by Lagrange, and $a^q = a$ for all $a$. So $K$ consists of the $q$ roots of $x^q - x$, and is a splitting field of $x^q - x$ over $\mathbb{F}p$. Splitting fields are unique up to isomorphism. ∎ Two further facts, stated without proof: the multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}q^\times$ is cyclic (post 04's $\mathbb{F}8$ table showed a generator), and $\mathbb{F}{p^d}$ sits inside $\mathbb{F}{p^n}$ exactly when $d \mid n$. Worked example. Over $\mathbb{F}2$: $$\begin{aligned}x^4 - x &= x\,(x + 1)\,(x^2 + x + 1), \\x^8 - x &= x\,(x + 1)\,(x^3 + x + 1)\,(x^3 + x^2 + 1).\end{aligned}$$ The first line lists the minimal polynomials of the 4 elements of $\mathbb{F}4$; the second those of the 8 elements of $\mathbb{F}8$. The two cubics are the two irreducible cubics over $\mathbb{F}2$, each with three roots in $\mathbb{F}8$. No quadratic appears in the second line, matching $2 \nmid 3$: $\mathbb{F}4 \not\subseteq \mathbb{F}8$. Chapter 5 — Galois extensions and the Galois group Terminology - $F$-automorphism of $E$ — a field automorphism $\sigma : E \to E$ with $\sigma(a) = a$ for every $a \in F$. They form a group $\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$.- Fixed field — for a subgroup $H \le \operatorname{Aut}(E)$, $E^H = \{ e \in E : \sigma(e) = e \text{ for all } \sigma \in H \}$.- Separable polynomial — one with no repeated root in its splitting field. An algebraic extension is separable if every minimal polynomial is separable.- Normal extension — an algebraic $E/F$ such that every irreducible polynomial in $F[x]$ with one root in $E$ splits completely in $E$.- Galois extension — a finite extension that is normal and separable. Its Galois group is $\operatorname{Gal}(E/F) = \operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$.- Galois group of a polynomial — $\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)$ for $E$ a splitting field of the (separable) polynomial. Separability is automatic in the cases you meet most. Over a field of characteristic 0, and over any finite field, every irreducible polynomial is separable. (An irreducible $f$ has a repeated root only if $\gcd(f, f') \ne 1$, which forces $f' = 0$; in characteristic 0 that is impossible for nonconstant $f$.) The standard inseparable example is $x^p - t$ over the field $\mathbb{F}p(t)$ of rational functions: it is irreducible (Eisenstein at the prime $t$), but over its splitting field it equals $(x - t^{1/p})^p$. Automorphisms permute roots Lemma. If $f \in F[x]$, $\alpha \in E$ is a root of $f$, and $\sigma \in \operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$, then $\sigma(\alpha)$ is a root of $f$. Proof. If $f = \sum ck x^k$ with $ck \in F$, then $f(\sigma(\alpha)) = \sum ck \sigma(\alpha)^k = \sigma\big(\sum ck\alpha^k\big) = \sigma(0) = 0$, since $\sigma$ fixes each $ck$. ∎ So if $E = F(\alpha1, \dots, \alphan)$ is a splitting field of $f$, an $F$-automorphism is determined by how it permutes $\alpha1, \dots, \alphan$, and $\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$ is (isomorphic to) a subgroup of $Sn$, an action on the roots in the sense of post 03. Theorem (characterisations of Galois extensions). For a finite extension $E/F$ the following are equivalent: 1. $E/F$ is Galois (normal and separable);2. $E$ is the splitting field over $F$ of a separable polynomial;3. $\lvert\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)\rvert = [E : F]$;4. the fixed field of $\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$ is exactly $F$. In general $\lvert\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)\rvert \le [E : F]$ and equality is what "Galois" means in practice. Examples. - $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}) = \{\mathrm{id}, \text{complex conjugation}\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.- $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not Galois. An automorphism must send $\sqrt[3]{2}$ to a root of $x^3 - 2$ inside $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$, and the only real root is $\sqrt[3]{2}$ itself. So $\operatorname{Aut}$ is trivial, of order $1 < 3$. It fails normality: $x^3 - 2$ has one root in the field but does not split there.- $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{F}{p^n}/\mathbb{F}p)$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by Frobenius. ($\mathrm{Fr}^k$ fixes exactly the roots of $x^{p^k} - x$, so $\mathrm{Fr}$ has order exactly $n$; and the group has order $[\mathbb{F}{p^n} : \mathbb{F}p] = n$ because $\mathbb{F}{p^n}$ is the splitting field of the separable $x^{p^n} - x$.) Chapter 6 — The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory). Let $E/F$ be a finite Galois extension with group $G = \operatorname{Gal}(E/F)$. Then the maps $$H \longmapsto E^H \qquad\text{and}\qquad K \longmapsto \operatorname{Gal}(E/K)$$ are mutually inverse bijections between subgroups $H \le G$ and intermediate fields $F \subseteq K \subseteq E$, and: 1. they reverse inclusion: $H1 \le H2$ if and only if $E^{H1} \supseteq E^{H2}$;2. degrees match indices: $[E : E^H] = |H|$ and $[E^H : F] = [G : H]$;3. $K/F$ is Galois if and only if $\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, and then $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F) \cong G / \operatorname{Gal}(E/K)$. So the lattice of intermediate fields is the lattice of subgroups turned upside down: the big field $E$ matches the trivial group, the base $F$ matches all of $G$. Part 3 is where normal subgroups and quotient groups (post 02) acquire their original meaning. Worked example 1: Q(√2, √3) $E = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ is the splitting field of the separable $(x^2 - 2)(x^2 - 3)$, so it is Galois of degree 4 and $G$ has 4 elements. Each automorphism sends $\sqrt2 \mapsto \pm\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3 \mapsto \pm\sqrt3$, and all four sign choices occur. Let $$\sigma : \sqrt2 \mapsto -\sqrt2,\ \sqrt3 \mapsto \sqrt3, \qquad \tau : \sqrt2 \mapsto \sqrt2,\ \sqrt3 \mapsto -\sqrt3.$$ Each has order 2 and they commute, so $G = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma, \tau, \sigma\tau\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$. The subgroups and their fixed fields: | Subgroup | Order | Fixed field | Degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ ||---|---|---|---|| $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ | 1 | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ | 4 || $\langle\sigma\rangle$ | 2 | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$ | 2 || $\langle\tau\rangle$ | 2 | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ | 2 || $\langle\sigma\tau\rangle$ | 2 | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt6)$ | 2 || $G$ | 4 | $\mathbb{Q}$ | 1 | ($\sigma\tau(\sqrt6) = (-\sqrt2)(-\sqrt3) = \sqrt6$.) By the theorem these are all the intermediate fields: exactly three quadratic subfields, which is not obvious without Galois theory. $G$ is abelian, so every subgroup is normal and every intermediate field is Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$. Worked example 2: the splitting field of x³ − 2 $E = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \omega)$ with $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{2}$, of degree 6 (Chapter 4). It is Galois, so $|G| = 6$, and $G$ acts on the three roots $\alpha, \alpha\omega, \alpha\omega^2$, so $G$ is a subgroup of $S3$ of order 6: $G \cong S3$. Concretely an automorphism is fixed by $\alpha \mapsto \alpha\omega^a$ ($a \in \{0, 1, 2\}$) and $\omega \mapsto \omega$ or $\omega^2$. Let $$\sigma : \alpha \mapsto \alpha\omega,\ \omega \mapsto \omega \quad(\text{order } 3), \qquad \tau : \alpha \mapsto \alpha,\ \omega \mapsto \omega^2 \quad(\text{complex conjugation, order } 2).$$ Composing right to left (apply $\tau$ first in $\sigma\tau$): $\sigma\tau$ sends $\alpha \mapsto \alpha\omega$ and $\omega \mapsto \omega^2$, so $\sigma\tau(\alpha\omega^2) = \alpha\omega \cdot \omega^4 = \alpha\omega^2$. Similarly $\sigma^2\tau$ fixes $\alpha\omega$. The six subgroups of $S3$ and their fixed fields: | Subgroup | Order | Normal? | Fixed field | Degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ ||---|---|---|---|---|| $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ | 1 | yes | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha, \omega)$ | 6 || $\langle\tau\rangle$ | 2 | no | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ | 3 || $\langle\sigma\tau\rangle$ | 2 | no | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha\omega^2)$ | 3 || $\langle\sigma^2\tau\rangle$ | 2 | no | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha\omega)$ | 3 || $\langle\sigma\rangle = A3$ | 3 | yes | $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ | 2 || $S3$ | 6 | yes | $\mathbb{Q}$ | 1 | Each order-2 subgroup fixes one real or complex cube root, so its fixed field contains that root; the degree count $[G : H] = 3 = [\mathbb{Q}(\alpha\omega^k) : \mathbb{Q}]$ makes it equal to $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha\omega^k)$. $\sigma$ fixes $\omega$, and $[G : \langle\sigma\rangle] = 2 = [\mathbb{Q}(\omega) : \mathbb{Q}]$. Part 3 of the theorem, checked on this example: $A3 = \langle\sigma\rangle$ is normal, and $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ is Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$ (it is the splitting field of $x^2 + x + 1$), with $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\omega)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S3/A3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$. The three subgroups of order 2 are not normal (they are conjugate, like the three Sylow 2-subgroups of $S3$ in post 03), and correspondingly $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ and its two conjugate fields are not Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$. > Common pitfall: the correspondence reverses order. A bigger subgroup fixes more conditions and so has a smaller fixed field. Draw the two lattices with $E$ at the top and $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ at the top. Chapter 7 — Solvable groups and the quintic Solvable groups A group $G$ is solvable if there is a chain of subgroups $$\{e\} = G0 \trianglelefteq G1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq Gk = G$$ with each $Gi$ normal in $G{i+1}$ and each quotient $G{i+1}/Gi$ abelian. - Abelian groups are solvable ($k = 1$).- $S3$: $\{e\} \trianglelefteq A3 \trianglelefteq S3$ with quotients $\mathbb{Z}/3$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2$.- $S4$: $\{e\} \trianglelefteq V4 \trianglelefteq A4 \trianglelefteq S4$ with quotients $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, $\mathbb{Z}/3$, $\mathbb{Z}/2$. So every subgroup of $S4$ is solvable too (subgroups and quotients of solvable groups are solvable).- $S5$ is not solvable. Its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$, $A5$, $S5$, and $A5$ is simple and non-abelian (post 02). A chain would have to pass through $A5$, and the step below $A5$ would need a normal subgroup of $A5$ with abelian quotient; the only candidates are $A5$ itself (quotient trivial, no progress) and $\{e\}$ (quotient $A5$, not abelian). Equivalently, the commutator subgroup of $A5$ is $A5$, so the derived series $S5 \supset A5 \supset A5 \supset \cdots$ never reaches $\{e\}$. Radicals A polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals if its roots can be written starting from rational numbers using $+, -, \times, \div$ and $k$-th roots. The quadratic formula shows this for degree 2; Cardano's and Ferrari's formulas do it for degrees 3 and 4. Theorem (Galois, characteristic 0). $f$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable. (Idea of "only if": adjoining a $k$-th root to a field that already contains the $k$-th roots of unity gives a Galois extension with cyclic group; adjoining roots of unity gives an abelian group. A tower of such steps gives, through part 3 of the Fundamental Theorem, a chain of normal subgroups with abelian quotients.) Since $S4$ is solvable and the Galois group of a degree-$n$ polynomial embeds in $Sn$, every polynomial of degree $\le 4$ is solvable by radicals. The Abel–Ruffini theorem says the general quintic is not: the generic polynomial $x^5 + a4x^4 + \cdots + a0$ with independent indeterminate coefficients has Galois group $S5$ over $\mathbb{Q}(a0, \dots, a4)$, which is not solvable, so there is no formula in radicals valid for all quintics. Galois theory gives more: specific quintics with rational coefficients have unsolvable roots. A concrete unsolvable quintic Lemma. Let $p$ be prime and $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ irreducible of degree $p$ with exactly two non-real roots. Then $\operatorname{Gal}(f) \cong Sp$. Proof. View $G = \operatorname{Gal}(f)$ as a subgroup of $Sp$ acting on the $p$ roots. For a root $\alpha$, $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) : \mathbb{Q}] = p$ divides $[E : \mathbb{Q}] = |G|$, so by Cauchy's theorem (post 03) $G$ has an element of order $p$; in $Sp$ the elements of order $p$ are the $p$-cycles. Complex conjugation restricts to an element of $G$ that swaps the two non-real roots and fixes the $p - 2$ real ones: a transposition. For prime $p$, a $p$-cycle and a transposition generate all of $Sp$. ∎ Theorem. $f(x) = x^5 - 4x + 2$ is not solvable by radicals over $\mathbb{Q}$. Proof. Irreducible: Eisenstein at $p = 2$ ($2 \mid -4$, $2 \mid 2$, $4 \nmid 2$, leading coefficient 1). Exactly three real roots: $f'(x) = 5x^4 - 4$ vanishes only at $x = \pm c$ with $c = (4/5)^{1/4} \approx 0.9457$. So $f$ increases, then decreases on $[-c, c]$, then increases. The local maximum $f(-c) \approx 5.03 > 0$ and the local minimum $f(c) \approx -1.03 < 0$, while $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $f \to +\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$. So $f$ crosses zero once on each of the three monotone pieces: three real roots, approximately $-1.5185$, $0.5085$ and $1.2436$, and the other two roots are a complex-conjugate pair. By the lemma with $p = 5$, $\operatorname{Gal}(f) \cong S5$, which is not solvable. By Galois's theorem, $f$ is not solvable by radicals. ∎ > Watch out: "not solvable by radicals" does not mean the roots are hard to compute. Numerically they are easy (the values above). It means there is no finite expression in $+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt[k]{\ }$ for them. Practice Exercises 1. Abstract Algebra — 04 · Rings, Ideals, Integral Domains and Fields
Created2026-07-22|Mathematics Abstract Algebra
Groups have one operation. Most of the algebra you already use has two: integers, polynomials and matrices can be added and multiplied, and the distributive law ties the two together. A structure with both is a ring. This post builds ring theory the same way posts 01–03 built group theory: definitions and examples, then substructures (ideals play the role of normal subgroups), quotients and a First Isomorphism Theorem. Two questions then drive the rest. Which rings behave like $\mathbb{Z}$, with unique factorisation? And how do you manufacture new fields, such as $\mathbb{C}$ or a field with 4 elements? The answer to the second, "take polynomials modulo an irreducible polynomial", is the starting point of Galois theory in post 05. > Series map: 00 Integers, Relations & Modular Arithmetic → 01 Groups & Subgroups → 02 Cosets, Lagrange & Quotient Groups → 03 Homomorphisms, Group Actions & Sylow → 04 Rings, Ideals & Fields (this post) → 05 Field Extensions & Galois Theory. Learning Outcome - State the ring axioms (with identity), and recognise units, zero divisors, integral domains and fields.- Prove that a finite integral domain is a field and that the characteristic of a domain is 0 or a prime.- Define ring homomorphisms, ideals and quotient rings, and prove the First Isomorphism Theorem for rings.- Prove that $R/P$ is a domain exactly when $P$ is prime, and $R/M$ is a field exactly when $M$ is maximal.- Use the division algorithm in $F[x]$ and prove that $F[x]$ is a principal ideal domain.- Test polynomials for irreducibility: roots, the rational root test, Eisenstein's criterion, reduction mod $p$.- Place a ring in the chain Euclidean domain ⊂ PID ⊂ UFD ⊂ integral domain, and show that $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.- Build $\mathbb{C}$ as $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$ and the finite fields $\mathbb{F}4$ and $\mathbb{F}8$, and compute in them. Chapter 1 — Rings Terminology - Ring — a set $R$ with two operations $+$ and $\cdot$ such that:1. $(R, +)$ is an abelian group, with identity $0$ and inverses $-a$;2. multiplication is associative: $(ab)c = a(bc)$;3. there is a multiplicative identity $1$ with $1a = a1 = a$;4. the distributive laws hold: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ and $(a + b)c = ac + bc$.- Convention. In this series every ring has a $1$ (some books call these "rings with unity" and allow rings without one). Multiplication need not be commutative.- Commutative ring — a ring with $ab = ba$ for all $a, b$.- Zero ring — $\{0\}$ with $1 = 0$. It is the only ring in which $1 = 0$ (if $1 = 0$ then $a = 1a = 0a = 0$).- Unit — an element $u$ with a two-sided inverse: $uv = vu = 1$. The units form a group $R^\times$ under multiplication.- Zero divisor — a nonzero $a$ for which some nonzero $b$ has $ab = 0$ or $ba = 0$.- Integral domain — a commutative ring with $1 \ne 0$ and no zero divisors.- Field — a commutative ring with $1 \ne 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit.- Division ring — like a field but not necessarily commutative (the quaternions are the standard example). Examples | Ring | Commutative? | Units | Zero divisors? ||---|---|---|---|| $\mathbb{Z}$ | yes | $\pm 1$ | none (domain) || $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ | yes | all nonzero | none (fields) || $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ | yes | classes with $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ | yes, unless $n$ is prime || $M2(\mathbb{R})$, $2\times 2$ real matrices | no | matrices with $\det \ne 0$ | yes || $\mathbb{R}[x]$, real polynomials | yes | nonzero constants | none || $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ | yes | $\pm 1, \pm i$ | none || $C[0,1]$, continuous functions, pointwise operations | yes | functions with no zero | yes | A few of the entries, worked out: - $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. The units are $1, 5, 7, 11$ (coprime to 12; each is its own inverse, e.g. $5 \cdot 5 = 25 \equiv 1$). Every other nonzero class is a zero divisor: $2 \cdot 6 = 12 \equiv 0$, $3 \cdot 4 \equiv 0$, $8 \cdot 3 \equiv 0$, and so on, giving $2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10$. In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ every nonzero element is either a unit or a zero divisor: if $d = \gcd(a, n) > 1$, then $a \cdot (n/d) \equiv 0$ with $n/d \not\equiv 0$.- Matrices. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, although neither factor is zero.- Gaussian integers. Use the norm $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = (a+bi)(a-bi)$, which is multiplicative: $N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$. If $uv = 1$ then $N(u)N(v) = 1$ in the positive integers, so $N(u) = 1$, and $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ leaves $\pm 1, \pm i$.- Functions. Let $f$ be zero on $[0, \tfrac12]$ and $g$ zero on $[\tfrac12, 1]$, both continuous and not identically zero (for example $f(t) = \max(0, t - \tfrac12)$, $g(t) = \max(0, \tfrac12 - t)$). Then $fg = 0$. First consequences of the axioms For all $a, b$ in a ring: 1. $0 \cdot a = a \cdot 0 = 0$. Proof: $0a = (0 + 0)a = 0a + 0a$; subtract $0a$ from both sides.2. $(-a)b = a(-b) = -(ab)$. Proof: $ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))b = 0b = 0$.3. $(-a)(-b) = ab$, and in particular $(-1)^2 = 1$. Cancellation. In an integral domain, if $a \ne 0$ and $ab = ac$ then $b = c$, because $a(b - c) = 0$ forces $b - c = 0$. This is exactly what fails in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$: $2 \cdot 1 = 2 \cdot 7$ but $1 \ne 7$. Every field is an integral domain: if $a \ne 0$ and $ab = 0$, then $b = a^{-1}ab = 0$. The converse fails ($\mathbb{Z}$), except for finite rings: Theorem. A finite integral domain is a field. Proof. Let $R$ be a finite domain and $a \ne 0$. The map $\mua : R \to R$, $x \mapsto ax$, is injective by cancellation. An injective map from a finite set to itself is surjective, so $ax = 1$ for some $x$. Since $R$ is commutative, $x$ is a two-sided inverse. ∎ Corollary. $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime. (If $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$, then $a$ and $b$ are zero divisors. If $n = p$ is prime, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors because $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$, post 00; so it is a finite domain.) This field is written $\mathbb{F}p$. Characteristic The characteristic of a ring $R$ is the least $n \ge 1$ with $n \cdot 1 = 1 + 1 + \cdots + 1 = 0$ ($n$ terms), or $0$ if there is no such $n$. So $\operatorname{char}\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{char}\mathbb{Q} = 0$ and $\operatorname{char}\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = n$. Theorem. The characteristic of an integral domain is $0$ or a prime. Proof. Suppose $\operatorname{char} R = n > 0$ and $n = ab$ with $1 \le a, b < n$. Then $0 = n \cdot 1 = (a \cdot 1)(b \cdot 1)$. In a domain one factor is $0$, contradicting the minimality of $n$. And $n \ne 1$ because $1 \ne 0$. ∎ > Watch out: characteristic is about $n \cdot 1$, not about the size of the ring. The infinite field $\mathbb{F}p(t)$ of rational functions over $\mathbb{F}p$ has characteristic $p$. Chapter 2 — Homomorphisms, ideals and quotient rings Terminology - Subring — a subset $S \subseteq R$ containing $1$ that is closed under $+$, $-$ and $\cdot$. Example: $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Z}[i] \subset \mathbb{C}$.- Ring homomorphism — a map $\varphi : R \to S$ with $\varphi(a + b) = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b)$, $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ and $\varphi(1) = 1$.- Kernel — $\ker\varphi = \{a : \varphi(a) = 0\}$.- Ideal — an additive subgroup $I \subseteq R$ that absorbs multiplication: $r a \in I$ and $a r \in I$ for all $r \in R$, $a \in I$. (For commutative rings one condition suffices.)- Principal ideal — in a commutative ring, $(a) = aR = \{ra : r \in R\}$, the smallest ideal containing $a$. More generally $(a1, \dots, ak) = \{r1a1 + \cdots + rkak\}$. Examples of homomorphisms: reduction $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; evaluation $\mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{C}$, $f \mapsto f(i)$; complex conjugation $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$; the inclusion of a subring. Ideals are the kernels Theorem. The kernel of a ring homomorphism is an ideal. Proof. It is an additive subgroup, being the kernel of a homomorphism of additive groups (post 03). If $\varphi(a) = 0$ then $\varphi(ra) = \varphi(r) \cdot 0 = 0$ and $\varphi(ar) = 0$. ∎ Ideals are to rings what normal subgroups are to groups: exactly the things you can take a quotient by. Two quick facts: - If an ideal $I$ contains a unit $u$, then $I = R$: for any $r$, $r = (ru^{-1})u \in I$. So a proper ideal contains no units, and in particular does not contain $1$.- A commutative ring $R$ with $1 \ne 0$ is a field if and only if its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $R$. (If $R$ is a field, any nonzero ideal contains a unit. Conversely, for $a \ne 0$, $(a) = R \ni 1$, so $ra = 1$ for some $r$.) Consequently every ring homomorphism from a field to a nonzero ring is injective: its kernel is a proper ideal (it misses 1), hence $\{0\}$. Theorem. Every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$ for a unique $n \ge 0$. Proof. Let $I \ne \{0\}$ and let $n$ be the least positive element of $I$. For $a \in I$ divide: $a = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Then $r = a - qn \in I$, so $r = 0$ by minimality, and $a \in (n)$. ∎ In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(a, b) = (\gcd(a, b))$: for example $(4, 6) = (2)$. This is Bézout's identity from post 00 in ideal language. Quotient rings Let $I$ be an ideal of $R$. The additive cosets $a + I$ form an abelian group $R/I$ (post 02). Define multiplication by $(a + I)(b + I) = ab + I$. Well defined. If $a' = a + i$ and $b' = b + j$ with $i, j \in I$, then $a'b' = ab + (aj + ib + ij)$, and the bracket lies in $I$ because $I$ absorbs multiplication. This is precisely where "ideal" (rather than just "subring") is needed. The ring axioms for $R/I$ follow from those of $R$, with $1 + I$ as identity, and $\pi : R \to R/I$, $a \mapsto a + I$, is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $I$. Theorem (First Isomorphism Theorem for rings). If $\varphi : R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism, then $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$ via $a + \ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(a)$. Proof. By the group version (post 03) the map $\bar\varphi$ is a well-defined isomorphism of additive groups. It is multiplicative: $\bar\varphi((a + K)(b + K)) = \bar\varphi(ab + K) = \varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$; and $\bar\varphi(1 + K) = \varphi(1) = 1$. ∎ Worked applications: - $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Evaluation at 0, $f \mapsto f(0)$, is surjective, and its kernel is the polynomials with zero constant term, which is $(x)$.- $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$. Evaluation at $i$ is surjective ($a + bx \mapsto a + bi$). Its kernel contains $x^2 + 1$; Chapter 6 shows that $(x^2 + 1)$ is a maximal ideal, so the kernel (a proper ideal containing it) is exactly $(x^2 + 1)$.- $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2, x) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The map $f \mapsto f(0) \bmod 2$ is surjective with kernel $\{f : f(0) \text{ even}\} = (2, x)$.- Chinese Remainder Theorem. If $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, the map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$, $a \mapsto (a \bmod m, a \bmod n)$, is a surjective ring homomorphism (surjective by post 00's CRT) with kernel $(m) \cap (n) = (mn)$. So $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as rings, and taking units gives $\phi(mn) = \phi(m)\phi(n)$ for Euler's function. > Trick: the fastest way to understand $R/I$ is again to find a surjective homomorphism out of $R$ whose kernel is $I$. Evaluation maps are the usual candidates for polynomial rings. Chapter 3 — Prime and maximal ideals From here on, "ring" means commutative ring with $1$. Terminology - Prime ideal — a proper ideal $P$ such that $ab \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$.- Maximal ideal — a proper ideal $M$ such that no ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$: if $M \subseteq J \subseteq R$ then $J = M$ or $J = R$. In $\mathbb{Z}$: $(p)$ for a prime $p$ is prime (this is Euclid's lemma) and maximal; $(0)$ is prime (because $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain) but not maximal, since $(0) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$; $(6)$ is neither, since $2 \cdot 3 \in (6)$ but $2, 3 \notin (6)$. The two quotient theorems Theorem. $P$ is a prime ideal of $R$ if and only if $R/P$ is an integral domain. Proof. $R/P$ is not the zero ring exactly when $1 \notin P$, i.e. when $P$ is proper. In $R/P$, the product $(a + P)(b + P) = ab + P$ is zero exactly when $ab \in P$, and $a + P$ is zero exactly when $a \in P$. So "no zero divisors in $R/P$" says word for word "$ab \in P \Rightarrow a \in P$ or $b \in P$". ∎ Theorem. $M$ is a maximal ideal of $R$ if and only if $R/M$ is a field. Proof. ($\Rightarrow$) Let $a + M \ne 0$, i.e. $a \notin M$. The ideal $M + (a) = \{m + ra\}$ strictly contains $M$, so by maximality it is $R$ and contains $1$: $1 = m + ra$ for some $m \in M$, $r \in R$. Then $(r + M)(a + M) = ra + M = 1 - m + M = 1 + M$, so $a + M$ is a unit. Also $1 + M \ne 0$ because $M$ is proper. ($\Leftarrow$) If $R/M$ is a field, $M$ is proper. Let $M \subsetneq J$ and pick $a \in J \setminus M$. Then $a + M$ has an inverse $r + M$, so $ra - 1 \in M \subseteq J$. Since $ra \in J$, also $1 \in J$ and $J = R$. ∎ Corollary. Every maximal ideal is prime (a field is a domain). Examples in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. - $(x)$ is prime but not maximal: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain but not a field. Indeed $(x) \subsetneq (2, x) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}[x]$.- $(2, x)$ is maximal: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2, x) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, a field.- $(x^2 + 1)$ is prime but not maximal: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{Z}[i]$, a domain that is not a field. A standard existence result (it needs Zorn's lemma): every proper ideal of a ring with $1$ is contained in a maximal ideal. Chapter 4 — Polynomial rings over a field Let $F$ be a field. $F[x]$ is the ring of polynomials $a0 + a1x + \cdots + anx^n$ with $ai \in F$. If $an \ne 0$, the degree is $n$ and $an$ is the leading coefficient; the polynomial is monic if $an = 1$. The zero polynomial has no degree (some books use $-\infty$). Degree, units, roots If $R$ is a domain, the leading coefficient of $fg$ is the product of the leading coefficients, which is nonzero. Hence, for nonzero $f, g$: $$\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g,$$ $R[x]$ is again a domain, and the units of $F[x]$ are the nonzero constants (a unit $f$ has $\deg f + \deg f^{-1} = 0$). Theorem (division algorithm). For $f, g \in F[x]$ with $g \ne 0$ there are unique $q, r \in F[x]$ with $f = qg + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$. Proof sketch. Existence by induction on $\deg f$: if $\deg f \ge \deg g = m$, subtract $(an/bm)\,x^{n-m}g$ to kill the leading term of $f$ and apply induction to the result. Dividing by $bm$ is where a field is used. Uniqueness: if $qg + r = q'g + r'$ then $(q - q')g = r' - r$; the right side has degree $< m$, so $q = q'$ by the degree formula, and then $r = r'$. ∎ Worked example. Divide $f = x^4 + 3x^3 - 2x + 5$ by $g = x^2 + x + 1$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$: $$x^4 + 3x^3 - 2x + 5 = (x^2 + x + 1)(x^2 + 2x - 3) + (8 - x).$$ (Check: $(x^2 + x + 1)(x^2 + 2x - 3) = x^4 + 3x^3 - x - 3$, and adding $8 - x$ gives $f$.) Factor theorem. $a$ is a root of $f$ if and only if $(x - a) \mid f$ (divide by $x - a$; the remainder is the constant $f(a)$). So a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ over a domain has at most $n$ roots. > Common pitfall: over a ring with zero divisors this fails. In $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, $x^2 - 1$ has four roots: $1, 3, 5, 7$ (each squares to $1$ mod 8). F[x] is a principal ideal domain A principal ideal domain (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal. Theorem. If $F$ is a field, $F[x]$ is a PID. Proof. Let $I \ne \{0\}$ be an ideal and let $g \in I$ be nonzero of least degree. For $f \in I$ write $f = qg + r$. Then $r = f - qg \in I$ and, if $r \ne 0$, $\deg r < \deg g$, contradicting minimality. So $r = 0$ and $I = (g)$. ∎ The proof is the one for $\mathbb{Z}$ with "size" replaced by "degree". As in $\mathbb{Z}$, $(f, g) = (\gcd(f, g))$, and the Euclidean algorithm computes the gcd: $$\begin{aligned}x^6 - 1 &= x^2\,(x^4 - 1) + (x^2 - 1), \\x^4 - 1 &= (x^2 + 1)(x^2 - 1) + 0,\end{aligned}$$ so $\gcd(x^6 - 1, x^4 - 1) = x^2 - 1$. > Watch out: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID, although $\mathbb{Z}$ is. Suppose $(2, x) = (h)$. Then $h \mid 2$, so $h$ is a constant $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$. $h = \pm 1$ is impossible because $(2, x)$ is proper (its quotient is $\mathbb{Z}/2$). $h = \pm 2$ is impossible because $x \notin (2)$. Irreducibility tests A nonzero non-unit $f \in F[x]$ is irreducible if it is not a product of two polynomials of smaller degree. Irreducible polynomials are the "primes" of $F[x]$, and whether $f$ is irreducible depends on the field: $x^2 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but factors as $(x - \sqrt2)(x + \sqrt2)$ over $\mathbb{R}$; $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but not over $\mathbb{C}$. 1. Degree 2 or 3. A polynomial of degree 2 or 3 over a field is irreducible if and only if it has no root in the field (any factorisation includes a linear factor).2. Rational root test. If $f = anx^n + \cdots + a0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $p/q$ in lowest terms is a root, then $p \mid a0$ and $q \mid an$.3. Gauss's lemma. A polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ that factors in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ into factors of lower degree already factors in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ into factors of those same degrees. So it suffices to look for integer factorisations.4. Eisenstein's criterion. Let $f = anx^n + \cdots + a0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $p$ a prime with $p \nmid an$, $p \mid ai$ for all $i < n$, and $p^2 \nmid a0$. Then $f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.5. Reduction mod $p$. Let $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ and let $p$ be a prime not dividing the leading coefficient. If the reduction $\bar f \in \mathbb{F}p[x]$ is irreducible (of the same degree), then $f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. Worked examples: - $x^3 - 2$: Eisenstein with $p = 2$. Also, the rational root test leaves only $\pm 1, \pm 2$, none of which is a root.- $x^3 + 2x + 1$: candidates $\pm 1$ give $4$ and $-2$, so no rational root; degree 3, so irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.- $x^5 - 4x + 2$: Eisenstein with $p = 2$ ($2 \mid -4$, $2 \mid 2$, $4 \nmid 2$). This polynomial returns in post 05.- Cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi5(x) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1 = (x^5 - 1)/(x - 1)$. Eisenstein does not apply directly, but $f(x)$ is irreducible exactly when $f(x+1)$ is, and$\Phi5(x + 1) = x^4 + 5x^3 + 10x^2 + 10x + 5$, which is Eisenstein at $5$.- $x^4 + 5x^3 + 3x^2 + 7x + 11$: reduce mod 2 to $x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$, which is irreducible in $\mathbb{F}2[x]$. So the original is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. > Common pitfall: "no rational roots" proves irreducibility only in degree 2 and 3. $x^4 + 4$ has no rational root, yet $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$. > Common pitfall: the reduction test only works one way. $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, but mod 2 it is $(x + 1)^2$. A reducible reduction proves nothing; so does a failed Eisenstein check. Chapter 5 — Unique factorisation Terminology In an integral domain $R$: - $a$ divides $b$, written $a \mid b$, if $b = ac$ for some $c \in R$.- $a$ and $b$ are associates if $a = ub$ for a unit $u$ (for example $2$ and $-2$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, or $1 + i$ and $i(1+i) = -1 + i$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$).- A nonzero non-unit $p$ is irreducible if $p = ab$ forces $a$ or $b$ to be a unit.- A nonzero non-unit $p$ is prime if $p \mid ab$ forces $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$ (equivalently, $(p)$ is a prime ideal).- Unique factorisation domain (UFD) — a domain in which every nonzero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and associates.- Euclidean domain — a domain with a function $N : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}{\ge 0}$ such that for all $a$ and $b \ne 0$ there are $q, r$ with $a = qb + r$ and $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$. Prime implies irreducible in any domain: if $p$ is prime and $p = ab$, then $p \mid ab$, say $p \mid a$, so $a = pc$ and $p = pcb$; cancelling gives $cb = 1$, so $b$ is a unit. The converse holds in a UFD (and so in a PID) but fails in general, which is exactly how unique factorisation breaks. The hierarchy $$\text{fields} \subset \text{Euclidean domains} \subset \text{PIDs} \subset \text{UFDs} \subset \text{integral domains}.$$ - Euclidean ⇒ PID: the proof that $F[x]$ is a PID works verbatim with $N$ in place of degree.- PID ⇒ UFD: a standard theorem (existence uses the fact that increasing chains of ideals in a PID stop; uniqueness uses "irreducible ⇒ prime" in a PID).- Every inclusion is strict, as the diagram's examples show. Gaussian integers are Euclidean Take $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$. To divide $\alpha$ by $\beta$, compute $\alpha/\beta$ in $\mathbb{C}$ and round each coordinate to the nearest integer to get $q$; the rounding error has both coordinates at most $\tfrac12$, so $N(\alpha/\beta - q) \le \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 < 1$ and $r = \alpha - q\beta$ has $N(r) < N(\beta)$. Worked example. Divide $7 + 2i$ by $2 - i$: $$\frac{7 + 2i}{2 - i} = \frac{(7 + 2i)(2 + i)}{5} = \frac{12 + 11i}{5} = 2.4 + 2.2i.$$ Round to $q = 2 + 2i$. Then $r = (7 + 2i) - (2 + 2i)(2 - i) = (7 + 2i) - (6 + 2i) = 1$, and $N(1) = 1 < N(2 - i) = 5$. So $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a PID and a UFD. A domain without unique factorisation Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ with norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$, again multiplicative. In $R$, $$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}).$$ - Norms. $N(2) = 4$, $N(3) = 9$, $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = 1 + 5 = 6$.- Units. $N(u) = 1$ forces $a^2 + 5b^2 = 1$, so $u = \pm 1$.- All four factors are irreducible. A proper factor of an element of norm 4, 9 or 6 would have norm 2 or 3. But $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ or $3$ has no integer solutions ($b \ne 0$ gives at least 5; $b = 0$ needs $a^2 \in \{2, 3\}$).- The factorisations really differ. $2$ is not an associate of $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ (the only units are $\pm 1$, and the norms 4 and 6 differ). So $R$ is not a UFD. Equivalently, $2$ is irreducible but not prime: $2 \mid (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, but $2$ divides neither factor, since $(1 \pm \sqrt{-5})/2 \notin R$. The other separating examples, stated without proof: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $F[x, y]$ are UFDs (Gauss: $R$ a UFD implies $R[x]$ a UFD) but not PIDs, as shown for $(2, x)$ above; and $\mathbb{Z}\big[\tfrac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\big]$ is a PID that is not Euclidean (Dummit & Foote, §8.1–8.2). Chapter 6 — Building fields from polynomials Theorem. Let $F$ be a field and $p \in F[x]$ nonconstant. Then $F[x]/(p)$ is a field if and only if $p$ is irreducible. Proof. In a PID, $(p)$ is maximal exactly when $p$ is irreducible. If $p$ is irreducible and $(p) \subseteq (h)$, then $h \mid p$, so $h$ is a unit (and $(h)$ is everything) or an associate of $p$ (and $(h) = (p)$). If $p = ab$ with $a, b$ of lower degree, then $(p) \subsetneq (a) \subsetneq F[x]$. Now apply "maximal ⇔ quotient is a field" from Chapter 3. ∎ What the elements look like. By the division algorithm every class has exactly one representative of degree $< n = \deg p$. So $F[x]/(p)$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ with basis $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}$, where $\alpha = x + (p)$ is a root of $p$. If $F = \mathbb{F}q$ is finite, the field has $q^n$ elements. AWS Course — 02 · Compute & Storage — EC2, EBS, S3
Created2026-07-19|Cloud AWS
With the network in place (Post 01), the next step is to put workloads onto it. AWS's workhorse trio for "run code + keep data" is EC2 (virtual machines), EBS (block storage attached to them), and S3 (object storage anyone in the account can use over HTTPS). Understanding the cost, durability, and access characteristics of each is the foundation for every later service — RDS sits on EBS, Lambda packages and container images live in S3, and the load balancer in Post 03 fronts EC2. > Series map: 00 Foundations → 01 Network First → 02 Compute & Storage (this post) → 03 Scale & Survive → 04 Managed Data → 05 Serverless & Events → 06 Workflow Orchestration → 07 Observability & Security → 08 IaC.
