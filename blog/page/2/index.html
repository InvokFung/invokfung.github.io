Violin Notes — 03 · Grade 3 New Keys, Shifting Prep & Slurred Staccato
Created2026-05-21|Music
Grade 3 ABRSM violin is the gateway between "beginner who can play tunes" and "real student". Two new flat keys appear (B♭ and E♭), the three minor scales must be distinguished, a hint of position shifting is introduced, and bowings begin to diversify (slurred staccato, hooked bowing). The repertoire shifts toward Baroque dances and early Classical pieces. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 (this post) → 04 Grade 4 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play two-octave scales of G, D, A, B♭ majors and one-octave E♭ major.- Play D, G, A natural and harmonic minors (one or two octaves as syllabus).- Distinguish the three minor forms by ear and on paper.- Use slurred staccato and hooked bowing.- Prepare for 3rd position shifting (theoretical introduction).- Identify interval quality (major, minor, perfect) at sight.- Play 5–7 Grade 3 pieces with confident phrasing. Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves) B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering: Wait — that's three "low" fingers across the scale. The hand shape changes per string. > Trick: the rule for any key with flats: drop the finger that plays the flatted note half a finger-width toward the nut. For B♭ major, that's 2 on G string, 1 on D, 1 on A. Memorise the pattern of which finger is low, not the abstract scale. Top note: B♭5 (4th finger on E in 1st position). Why this key matters B♭ is the first key violinists genuinely struggle with. The hand has to relearn its default spacing. Master B♭ by Grade 3, and every flat key after becomes intuitive. Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave) E♭ has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. One octave only at Grade 3. Different editions fingerprint this differently. The point: 3 flats means 3 "low" finger placements. Get the pattern of lowness right and your fingers will land. > Where I got stuck: I tried to memorise E♭ as "B♭, E♭, A♭" abstractly. Fix: I stopped naming the flats and just thought "low-low-low" in the right places. Faster, more reliable. Chapter 3 — The three minor forms Grade 2 introduced natural minor. Grade 3 adds harmonic and (sometimes) melodic. Natural minor (review) Same notes as the relative major. A natural minor: Harmonic minor — raises the 7th The leading-tone effect that classical music expects: That raised 7th creates an augmented 2nd between F and G♯ (3 semitones — a full step + half-step). This gives harmonic minor its Middle-Eastern or Spanish flavour. > Trick: harmonic minor sounds exotic compared to natural minor. Sing both ascending — natural feels "modal/folk", harmonic feels "classical/dramatic". After ten A/B comparisons, your ear locks the difference. Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down Two different note sets in one scale. Composers chose this in the Baroque to handle melodic lines without the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd "bump". Grade 3 typically requires melodic minor only on paper; full playing comes Grade 4. Then play harmonic minor of the same root. Six versions per session. Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato A new bowing. Notation: slur (curved line) + dot under each note within the slur. The bow stays on the string; the wrist makes a small stop-start motion for each note while moving in one direction. > Where I got stuck: I lifted the bow between notes — that's staccato volante (flying staccato), Grade 6 stuff. Fix: practise on open strings, bow stays on the string, only the wrist pulses. Picture a bow that "hesitates" 4 times in one stroke. Hooked bowing Two unequal notes (often dotted-quaver + semiquaver) on one bow with a tiny stop between them: Used to keep the dotted figure on the same bow direction throughout a phrase. Saves bow-direction confusion. Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview True shifting (moving the hand to 2nd/3rd/etc. positions) is Grade 4 territory, but Grade 3 introduces the concept and may use one shift in a piece or as a "step out" of 1st position. Why shift? 1. Reach higher notes the 1st position can't.2. Stay on one string for a phrase (changing tone colour).3. Make awkward fingerings easier. What 3rd position feels like In 3rd position, the 1st finger plays what the 3rd finger plays in 1st position. So on the A string: - 1st position: 3rd finger = D5.- 3rd position: 1st finger = D5. The hand moves up the neck by about 1 inch. First shift exercise On the A string:1. Open A, then 3rd finger D (1st position).2. Slide the 1st finger up the string until it sounds D.3. Now you're in 3rd position. Play 2 (E) and 3 (F♯). Don't lift; slide smoothly. The thumb travels with the hand. > Trick: the thumb moves at the same time as the fingers. If the thumb stays back, you get a stretched, painful hand. Practise the shift with eyes closed, only feeling the smooth slide. Listen for the slide pitch — at first it'll glissando, later it'll be silent. Grade 3 may include one piece with a marked shift; Grade 4 requires the technique. Violin Notes — 02 · Grade 2 Two-Octave Scales & Slur Control
Created2026-05-21|Music
Grade 2 ABRSM violin doubles your reach: two-octave scales, the first minor key, dotted rhythms, and longer slurs that demand real bow planning. The hand shape from Grade 1 stays, but it now travels — string crossings within a scale become the central skill. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 (this post) → 03 Grade 3 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play two-octave scales of G and D major, one-octave C and F major.- Play A natural minor (one octave) and recognise the relative-minor concept.- Use dotted-quaver + semiquaver rhythms accurately.- Slur 4–8 notes per bow with even tone.- Read fluently up to top D / E above the staff (E string, 4th finger).- Play 4–6 Grade 2 pieces with hands together, musically shaped. Chapter 1 — Two-octave scales arrive A two-octave scale spans two strings (Grade 1 used two strings for one octave); now you traverse three strings. G major (two octaves) Grade 2 typically asks for the scale to reach G5 (3rd finger on E in 1st position). Some editions extend to A5. D major (two octaves) Top note is A5. C major (one octave) — first piece with no sharps > Trick: C major is the shock key for violin beginners. Every other scale you've played has the half-step between 2 and 3. C major has it between 1 and 2 on the D string. Mark the music with an arrow over the F natural for the first 2 weeks. F major (one octave) Half-step lands between finger 1 and 2 on the A string (B♭ → C). The B♭ requires placing finger 1 low — closer to the nut than normal. > Where I got stuck: "low 1" felt unnatural for weeks. The finger wants to land where it always does. Fix: physically mark a tape line for "low 1" position on the A string for one practice session. Once your finger learns the distance, peel the tape off. Chapter 2 — The first minor scale: A natural minor A natural minor uses the same notes as C major but starts on A: Fingering (one octave starting from open A): Or starting on the G string for low-octave practice: 1=A (low) ... but Grade 2 starts on open A. What "natural minor" means Three forms of minor exist: | Form | Pattern (intervals) | Where used ||------|---------------------|------------|| Natural | W-H-W-W-H-W-W | Grade 2 introduction || Harmonic | Raises 7th by half-step | Grade 3+ || Melodic | Raises 6th + 7th going up, naturals coming down | Grade 4+ | Grade 2 only requires natural minor. Memorise it now; the others build on it. > Trick: A natural minor = the white notes from A to A. C major = the white notes from C to C. They share all notes — they're relative. Every major key has a relative minor a minor 3rd (3 semitones) below. Memorise this pairing now: it saves hours later. Chapter 3 — Dotted rhythms A dot after a note adds half its value: The Grade 2 staple is the dotted-quaver + semiquaver pair: Count subdivisions: > Trick: I subdivided in semiquavers aloud for every dotted pair for 2 weeks: "ONE-e-and-AH, ONE-e-and-AH". Sounds ridiculous, locks the ratio in. Once it's automatic, drop the speaking. Bow distribution for dotted rhythms The long note gets more bow, the short note gets less. Roughly 3:1 ratio matching the duration: If you give them equal bow, the short note sounds louder (more bow speed in less time). Wrong feel. Chapter 4 — Longer slurs and bow planning Grade 1 slurs were 2–4 notes. Grade 2 introduces 6, 8, even a full scale (8 notes) on one bow. The bow-budget rule Total bow length = 25 inches (varies by player + bow).For 8 notes in one bow: 25 ÷ 8 ≈ 3 inches per note. You cannot play loud + slow + long-slurred. Pick two. Grade 2 long slurs are usually mezzo-piano and steady — the bow conserves itself. Slur with string crossing Example: slur "open A → 1st finger B → open E" in one bow. Two string changes inside one stroke. The right wrist + elbow tilt to roll between strings without breaking the sound. > Where I got stuck: every slur with a string crossing scratched on the crossing point. Fix: slow practice with the metronome at 40 bpm, paying attention to the angle of the bow hair at the crossing. The bow should tilt gradually, not snap. Drill: 5 minutes a day for 2 weeks → solved. Practice progression 1. 2-note slur, same string.2. 2-note slur, crossing strings.3. 4-note slur, same string.4. 4-note slur, crossing strings.5. 8-note slur = full scale. Don't jump steps. Each stage takes about a week. Chapter 1 — 1st Position fingering What "1st position" means The position number tells you where the first finger sits on the fingerboard. In 1st position, the index finger sits just below the nut — about a whole tone above the open string. From there, fingers 2, 3, 4 extend up the fingerboard, each roughly a whole tone apart (with one half-step adjustment per scale). The diagram shows the A/D major pattern: whole-step between every finger. In other keys, the half-step location shifts (Chapter 3). On the violin, the thumb has no number because it never stops a string; its job is to brace the neck. > Trick: coming from piano, I kept calling my thumb "finger 1" and reaching with my index expecting it to be "2". The fix: every Grade 1 piece marks "1" above the index. Trust the printed number, not your old habit. Placing fingers cleanly 1. Fingertip down, not the pad — the smaller contact area produces a cleaner stop.2. Curved finger — knuckles bent.3. Just enough pressure to stop the string vibrating above the finger. Too much pressure = sharp pitch + finger fatigue.4. Drop and lift — don't slide. Vertical motion only. > Where I got stuck: I pressed every finger too hard for two months. My left hand cramped after 10 minutes. The fix: deliberately under-press until the note buzzes; then add the minimum extra pressure to stop the buzz. That's the right amount. Most students press 2-3× more than needed. Chapter 2 — Tuning revisited (the violinist's ear) You learnt to tune in Post 00. Grade 1 adds double-stop tuning — bow two adjacent open strings together and listen for the perfect 5th. How a perfect 5th sounds When two strings tuned a perfect 5th apart sound together, the combined tone is smooth and ringing. When slightly off, you hear beats — a wobbling, rough pulse like an out-of-sync washing machine. To tune by 5ths:1. Tune A to A=440 with a tuner.2. Bow A and D together gently. Adjust D's fine tuner until beats vanish.3. Bow D and G together. Adjust G.4. Bow A and E together. Adjust E. > Trick: sit in a quiet room. Beats are easier to hear without background noise. After 6 months of tuning by 5ths, you'll do it automatically — and you'll have built a perfect-5th template in your ear that helps every interval recognition exercise. Chapter 3 — The three Grade 1 scales G major (one octave, G string + D string) Open G → 1st finger A → 2nd finger B → 3rd finger C → open D → 1st finger E → 2nd finger F♯ → 3rd finger G. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 on each string. (B→C and F♯→G are half-steps.) D major (one octave, D string + A string) Open D → 1 E → 2 F♯ → 3 G → open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 again. A major (one octave, A string + E string) Open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D → open E → 1 F♯ → 2 G♯ → 3 A. Same half-step pattern. > Trick: G, D, A majors all use the same finger pattern in 1st position: 0-1-2-3 with the half-step between 2 and 3 on each string. Memorise one hand shape; transfer to all three keys. This is the "violin equivalent" of the piano's W-W-H-W-W-W-H — but expressed as finger placement. Arpeggios (Grade 1) The tonic triad spelled out: root, third, fifth, octave. Practise scales and arpeggios in pairs: G major scale → G major arpeggio → G again. Builds the key-feel. Chapter 4 — Bowing: détaché and slur Détaché "Detached" — each note gets its own bow stroke (down or up). The bow doesn't lift between notes; it changes direction on the string. In Grade 1 you play almost everything détaché. Goal: smooth, even tone with no scratching at the bow direction change. The wrist makes a tiny "give" at each change, like a pendulum bottom. Slur A curved line over two or more notes means play them in one bow stroke (one direction, no change). Same hair, multiple finger changes. > Where I got stuck: I scratched every bow change for the first 2 months. Fix: practise the bow change motion itself without a string — just air bow with the empty bow, smoothly reversing direction. The wrist learns to soften the change. Then add the string back. Bow distribution On a slur of 4 notes, you have to "ration" the bow length so you don't run out. Roughly: divide the bow into 4 equal parts; one part per note. Practice: slur 2 notes per bow, then 4, then 8. By Grade 3 you'll be slurring entire scales in one bow. Chapter 5 — Reading music for violin Violin reads in treble clef only (unlike the piano, which uses both). Range covers from open G (below the staff, on a ledger line) up to many ledger lines above. By Grade 1 you read up to about A or B above the staff (3rd finger on E string).
