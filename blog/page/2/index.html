Billiards & Snooker — 11 · Safety
Created2026-09-10|Sports Billiards
When there is no good pot, the best shot leaves your opponent nothing. This lesson covers what a good safety leaves, the break-off and why thickness is everything, a thin safety back behind a baulk colour, how exact the pace must be, why distance and cushions defend, the shot to nothing, soft safeties, and how to choose. > Series map. ★ Basics: 01 Cue action and elevation → 02 Aiming and potting → 03 Tip and power → 04 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles · ★★★ Advanced: 09 Escaping snookers → 10 Multi-ball shots → 11 Safety (this post) → 12 Position play · ★★★★ Hard: 13 Break-building → 14 Special shots · Reference: 15 Rules, tactics and practice · ★★★★★ Pro: 16 Cue-ball control. Learning Outcome - Know what a good safety leaves: the white far away, on a cushion, or behind a colour.- Play a break-off, and miss on the thin side.- Play a thin safety back to baulk, and judge its pace.- Use distance and cushions as defence.- Pick between safety options by what your opponent has next. Chapter 1 — The idea 1 · What a good safety leaves After a good safety your opponent has no easy pot, and the white is: 1. far from the reds (a long shot has little room), or2. on a cushion (they must raise the cue and hit the top of the white), or3. behind a colour (a snooker: they cannot hit a red directly). One of these is good. Two or three together is very good. You do not need a perfect snooker every time. 2 · Distance is defence From post 02: the same pot from 30 cm has 2.20° of room, from 1.5 m only 0.36°. Every metre you put between the white and the reds makes your opponent's next shot much harder. Chapter 2 — The break-off 3 · The break-off 1. White in the D, between the brown and the yellow.2. Aim to just clip the end red of the back row, on the outside.3. Power 4, a touch above centre.4. The white goes top cushion, side cushion, and back to the baulk end. <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b12clipbreak.webp" alt="Clip from behind the baulk end: the white leaves the D, clips the end red of the pack, runs round off the top and side cushions and comes back past the yellow to the baulk end"></div> Only 3 reds move more than 4 cm, and the white stops in baulk near the cushion ✓ (model). > Trick: pick a mark on the top cushion where the white should land, and watch that mark, not the red, as you play. 4 · Thickness is everything | Hit | White touches | Reds moved | White ends ||-----|---------------|------------|------------|| just right | the end red | 3 | baulk end, near the cushion ✓ || a bit thick (2) | the end red | 9 | baulk cushion, but reds everywhere ✗ || too thick (1) | two reds | 12 | loose in baulk ✗ | "Just right" and "a bit thick" are only 0.8° apart at the white. Miss on the thin side: a fraction too thin may miss the red (a foul, 4 points), but too thick opens the reds for your opponent. Chapter 3 — Safety in the frame 5 · Thin off a red, back behind a colour The same idea as the break-off, later in the frame. The reds are up the table; one red sits on the side cushion. 1. Clip that red thin: it moves only 1 cm and stays on the cushion.2. The white comes off the cushion, runs to the baulk cushion and back.3. It stops about 35 cm behind the brown. From there no red can be hit at both edges (post 09): the colours are in the way. A full snooker. 6 · Pace makes the snooker The same aim at different paces (model): | Pace | The white ends | Reds it can hit ||------|----------------|-----------------|| a touch soft | nearer the baulk cushion, off the line behind the brown | 1 ✗ || right | behind the brown | none ✓ || a touch firm | closer to the brown, but off to the side | 2 ✗ | The snooker works over about 6% of the pace, somewhere between power 2 and power 3. Even when it misses by a little, the white is still in baulk, far from the reds: a good safety, just not a snooker. > Trick: pick the stopping spot first ("a ball behind the brown"), then play the pace for that spot. The aim is the easy part. 7 · Leave the white on a cushion A white that dies on a cushion is hard to play from. Straight in with a low tip and soft pace, it comes back only 8 cm (post 07). Your opponent then has to raise the cue about 12°, and hit the top of the white: no screw, no stun (post 07). 8 · A soft touch is a shot too Snooker has no cushion rule: after the white hits a ball on, nothing has to reach a cushion (unlike pool). So a soft safety is legal: - roll the white up to a red so it stops just behind it, or behind a colour next to it;- nudge a red a few centimetres off a pocket;- just touch a red in the pack and stay there. A soft touch moves the fewest balls, so you know exactly what you leave. > Watch out: stopping touching a red is a special case (post 14). Your opponent may play away from it without hitting anything, which can be easy for them. 9 · The shot to nothing A shot to nothing is a pot where the white goes somewhere safe whether it goes in or not (coaching advice): - you play the pot at a pace that also sends the white back to baulk;- if it goes in, you have a chance to build a break;- if it misses, your opponent still faces a long shot. Before a long pot, ask: "if this misses, where are the red and the white?" In the model, a long red into a top corner missed by 0.6° ran all the way back to baulk, so check where the red ends, not just the white. 10 · Choosing a safety | Option | When | The idea ||--------|------|----------|| thin off a red, white to baulk | the reds are up the table | like the break-off: thin contact, the white runs back off the cushions || behind a colour | a colour is near the line back | a snooker (section 5) || white on a cushion | a straight line into a cushion is open | low tip, soft pace || put the red safe | a loose red is near a pocket | send it to a cushion away from the pockets, the white the other way || soft touch | the reds are tight | move as little as possible | For each option ask: "what does my opponent have next?" Choose the one that leaves the least, and the one you can play. A language model knows nothing about your own notes. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) fixes that without any training: when a question arrives, find the few passages of your documents that answer it, put them in the prompt, and ask the model to answer from those passages, with citations. This post builds a complete RAG tool in about 180 lines of Python, one piece per chapter: load Markdown files, chunk them by heading, embed them with a free local model, store the vectors in a NumPy file, retrieve the top matches, and generate a cited answer with the Anthropic Messages API. Then it measures retrieval with a small hand-written test set (hit rate and MRR), improves it with hybrid search and reranking, and covers the ways RAG fails. The running example is "ask questions about your own notes", for instance a blog's posts folder. It builds on the embeddings from post 17. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 18 Coding with AI Agents → 19 RAG (this post) → 20 Reading Papers → … → 24 Desk Health. Part D · AI tools. Chapter 1 — RAG in one picture Terminology | Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Chunk | A small piece of a document, the unit you embed and retrieve. || Index | The stored chunks plus their vectors. || Retrieval | Finding the chunks most related to a question. 3D Printing Course — 07 · Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts
Created2026-08-28|Engineering 3D Printing
The base course closes where it should: with the question of whether the part actually works. Posts 00–03 covered the process, Post 04 the materials, Post 05 whether the part is the right size, and Post 06 how to model it. This post covers whether it survives being used. That requires a working vocabulary of mechanics, an honest treatment of the anisotropy that makes a printed part fundamentally different from a moulded one, a correctly-ordered list of which print settings actually matter for strength, a catalogue mapping observed failures to their causes, and — the section most 3D printing material omits — a clear-eyed account of when the right answer is not to print the part at all. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts (this post). Learning Outcome - Use the mechanics vocabulary correctly: stress, strain, stiffness, strength, toughness, yield, ultimate, ductile, brittle, fatigue, creep.- Explain why creep — not strength — is what quietly destroys loaded PLA parts, and design around it.- Describe printed-part anisotropy correctly, and run a test that measures your Z-strength ratio rather than quoting someone else's number.- Rank the print-profile levers on strength in the correct order, and explain why infill density sits far lower than beginners assume.- Argue the perimeters-versus-infill case from where bending stress actually acts.- Design out stress concentrations, and use fillets as the cheapest strength upgrade available.- Diagnose a broken part from the fracture surface and map symptom to root cause to fix.- Build a repeatable comparative test rig and test the actual part rather than a coupon.- Decide when machining, moulding, sheet metal, or simply buying the part is the better answer. Chapter 1 — The mechanics you actually need Terminology - Stress (σ) — force per unit area, in pascals (N/m²) or MPa. What the material experiences, independent of the part's size.- Strain (ε) — deformation per unit length, dimensionless. How much the material stretched relative to its original length.- Stiffness (elastic modulus, E) — the slope of stress against strain in the elastic region. How much it deflects under load. A stiff material barely moves.- Strength — the stress at which the material fails, whether by yielding or by fracturing. How much load it takes before something goes wrong. Stiffness and strength are independent properties: a material can be stiff and weak (glass), or compliant and strong (many elastomers).- Yield strength — the stress at which deformation stops being elastic and becomes permanent. Past yield the part is bent, even after the load comes off.- Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) — the maximum stress the material carries before fracture.- Toughness — the energy absorbed before fracture; the area under the stress-strain curve. How much abuse it takes before breaking. A tough material bends, absorbs and survives.- Ductile failure — the material yields, stretches, necks and then tears. It gives warning. PETG, ABS, nylon, TPU.- Brittle failure — the material fractures suddenly at or near its elastic limit, with little permanent deformation and no warning. PLA, standard photopolymer resin, and most fibre-filled composites.- Fatigue — failure from repeated cycles of load well below the static strength. A part that survives a 100 N pull once may fail after 10,000 cycles at 30 N.- Creep — slow, continuous deformation under a constant load over time, without any increase in load. It accelerates dramatically as temperature approaches Tg.- Stress concentration — a local multiplication of stress at a geometric discontinuity (a sharp internal corner, a notch, a hole edge, a sudden change of section).- Neutral axis — in a beam under bending, the surface where the stress is zero; material on one side is in tension, on the other in compression. The distinction beginners get wrong most often Stiff ≠ strong ≠ tough. These are three different questions and different materials win each: | Question | Property | The common filament that wins ||---|---|---|| How much does it bend under load? | stiffness | PLA is the stiffest common filament — genuinely || How much load until it breaks? | strength | depends heavily on direction; PLA is competitive in-plane || Does it survive being dropped or shocked? | toughness | PLA is poor; PETG, ABS, PC and nylon are far better | This is why PLA feels great on the bench (stiff, holds shape, precise) and disappoints in service (brittle, notch-sensitive, creeps). It is not a bad material. It is a material with a specific and well-known profile. Creep — the quiet killer Creep deserves its own treatment because it is the failure mode most often misdiagnosed as "the print was weak". Polymers are viscoelastic: under sustained load they behave partly like solids and partly like very stiff liquids. Hold a constant load on a printed bracket and it will slowly, continuously deform. Not fail — deform. Over hours, days, months, depending on the material, the stress level and the temperature. The temperature dependence is the sting. Creep rate rises sharply as the material approaches Tg (Post 04). A PLA part at 20 °C creeps slowly enough to be irrelevant for many uses. The same part at 50 °C — a closed car in summer, a sunlit window sill, a shelf above a radiator, next to a stepper motor — is within striking distance of PLA's Tg of roughly 55–65 °C, and creeps fast enough to be visible in a day. The canonical failure: a PLA phone mount, wall hook, or shelf bracket that was perfectly fine for weeks and one day is simply drooping. Nothing broke. It flowed. Design responses, in order of effectiveness: 1. Don't use PLA for a sustained load in a warm place. PETG is a large improvement, ABS/ASA more, PC more again, and a fibre-filled grade of any of them better still.2. Reduce the stress. Creep rate is strongly stress-dependent; halving the stress helps disproportionately. Thicker sections, more perimeters, a shorter moment arm.3. Put the load into compression rather than bending. Compressive creep in a well-supported column is far less troublesome than bending creep in a cantilever.4. Carry the sustained load in metal. A printed body with a steel screw, rod or bracket taking the actual load is a robust pattern.5. Anneal (Post 04) if the part is PLA and must be warm — increased crystallinity meaningfully raises creep resistance. Then re-check the fit, because the dimensions changed. > Watch out: static strength tests tell you nothing about creep. Hanging a weight on a bracket for thirty seconds and declaring it strong is not a test of a load it will carry for a year. If the part carries a sustained load, the only meaningful test is a sustained one. Chapter 2 — Anisotropy, honestly What is actually going on A moulded part is essentially isotropic: the polymer is a continuous mass and its properties are the same in every direction. A printed part is not. It is a stack of extruded beads bonded to their neighbours, and the bond between beads is not the same as the bulk material. - In-plane (X/Y), along and across the extrusion direction, load is carried mostly by continuous polymer within beads and by side-to-side welds between adjacent beads laid down while both were hot.- Through-thickness (Z), across layers, load is carried entirely by the interlayer weld: the region where a new bead was deposited onto a layer that had already partly cooled, and the polymer chains had a limited time and a limited temperature at which to diffuse across the interface and entangle. That diffusion process is the entire mechanism. The strength of the Z direction is the strength of that weld, and the weld is imperfect: it forms in a fraction of a second, at a temperature falling rapidly toward Tg, between two surfaces that also have geometric voids at the bead junctions. The honest statement of the magnitude: interlayer (Z) strength is substantially lower than in-plane strength for essentially every FDM material and profile. The size of the gap varies enormously — it depends on the polymer, the nozzle temperature, the layer height, the part cooling, the chamber temperature, the layer time, the bead geometry, and whether the part was annealed. Published studies find a wide range of ratios, and any single percentage quoted as a general fact about "3D printing" is not one. You will see figures like "Z is 50 % of XY" repeated widely. Treat them the way Post 05 taught you to treat "0.05 mm accuracy": as a number for someone's specific machine, material and profile, not as a property of the technology. Measure it on yours. Exercise 2 gives the procedure. Chapter 3 — What actually controls strength, in order Beginners tune this list in roughly reverse order. Here it is correctly ordered by leverage. 1. Orientation — by a wide margin the most important Rotating the part on the plate can change its breaking load by a factor. Nothing else in the profile comes close. The rule: never let the primary tensile load path cross a layer boundary. Identify how the part is loaded, identify where the tension is highest, and orient so the layers run along the load, not across it. Concrete cases: | Part | Wrong orientation | Right orientation ||---|---|---|| Hook carrying a downward load | printed upright — the bending tension at the root is across layers | printed flat on its side (or standing in the load plane) so layers run along the hook || Bracket with an L-shape | either leg vertical — the corner is a layer interface in tension | flat, with both legs in the layer plane || Threaded boss taking a pull-out load | axis vertical — the thread strips layer by layer | axis horizontal, or use a heat-set insert (Post 06) || Long thin rod under tension | vertical — every layer is a potential fracture plane | horizontal || A gear | either works for tooth bending; hub axis vertical is standard | axis vertical, so tooth loads are in-plane | Post 03 covers orientation for surface quality and supports. This is the same decision viewed through strength, and when the two conflict, strength usually wins on a functional part. 2. Perimeter (wall) count Second-largest lever, and much larger than infill. Going from 2 to 4 perimeters typically makes a far bigger difference than going from 20 % to 50 % infill, at a fraction of the material and time cost. Why: where bending stress actually acts Most printed parts fail in bending, not pure tension. In a beam under bending: - The material on the outside of the bend is in tension; on the inside, in compression; and somewhere between them, at the neutral axis, the stress is zero.- Stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis. The extreme fibres carry the maximum; material at the neutral axis carries nothing.- Bending stiffness is governed by the second moment of area, which weights material by the square of its distance from the neutral axis. Bending strength is governed by the section modulus, which likewise rewards distance from the axis. That is the entire argument. Perimeters are the material at the extreme fibres — the material that is doing the work. Infill is the material near the neutral axis, where the stress is small. Adding infill adds mass and print time in the place where it contributes least. Infill is not useless; it does three real jobs: 1. Supports the top surfaces so they can be printed at all (Post 02).2. Stabilises the perimeters against buckling — a thin shell under compression fails by buckling long before it fails by crushing, and infill braces it.3. Carries compression and shear in a part loaded through its bulk rather than in bending. But for the common case — a bracket, a hook, a mount, a lever, anything cantilevered — perimeters win decisively. 3. Material Third. Choosing PETG over PLA for a part that gets knocked, or nylon over PETG for a part that wears, changes the outcome more than any slicer setting except the two above. Post 04's decision tree is the tool. Note that the material choice interacts with orientation: fibre-filled composites are dramatically stiffer in-plane and are usually more brittle across layers, so the anisotropy gets worse, not better. 4. Temperature and cooling Fourth. Nozzle temperature is the strongest single lever on interlayer strength within a given material (Chapter 2). Reduced part cooling helps interlayer bonding and hurts overhangs. An enclosure helps materials that want one. These are real effects with real trade-offs — but they are refinements on top of the first three decisions, not substitutes for them. 5. Infill density and pattern Fifth, and well below where beginners put it. - Density. Beyond roughly 40–50 %, additional infill buys little strength for a lot of time and material; the curve flattens. Below about 15 % the part becomes noticeably soft and top surfaces suffer. For most functional parts, 20–30 % infill with 4–6 perimeters outperforms 50 % infill with 2 perimeters, using less material.- Pattern. Matters more than density in some load cases. Gyroid is isotropic, self-supporting, prints fast and is a strong general default. Cubic and triangular are stiff and good under multi-axis load. Grid creates crossing points where the nozzle collides with previously-laid material. Lines/rectilinear is weak in one direction. Concentric is right under flexible parts.- Special case: a fully solid part (100 % infill) is not automatically the strongest thing you can print. Over-extrusion in a solid part introduces internal stress and can leave voids; and the part still fails at layer interfaces. Solid is heavy, slow and often not better than thick perimeters plus moderate infill. 6. Layer height Sixth. Thicker layers bond somewhat better and print faster; thinner layers give finer detail and better surface. Its effect on strength is real but small next to the levers above. > Common pitfall: the reflex "print it at 100 % infill so it's strong". It costs hours and grams, and it usually loses to reorienting the part and adding two perimeters — which costs nothing. Chapter 4 — Stress concentration and design patterns The notch effect A sharp internal corner is a stress concentrator. The local stress at the corner can be several times the nominal stress in the surrounding material, and it rises as the corner radius falls — approaching, in the idealised limit of a perfectly sharp corner, an unbounded value. Real materials do not see infinite stress, but the multiplication is large and real, and it is where cracks start. Printed parts are unusually vulnerable to this for two reasons: 1. PLA and filled composites are notch-sensitive — brittle materials do not locally yield to blunt the peak, so the concentration translates directly into a crack.2. A layer interface at a sharp internal corner combines a stress raiser with the weakest plane in the part. That combination is the single most common origin of a printed-part fracture. Fillets: the cheapest strength upgrade available Adding a fillet to a loaded internal corner costs one CAD feature, a negligible amount of material, and essentially no print time. It reduces the peak stress substantially. There is no other change with that ratio of benefit to cost. Practical guidance: - Fillet every loaded internal corner. A radius comparable to the local wall thickness is a reasonable default; larger is better, up to what the geometry allows.- A larger radius is better than a smaller one. The benefit is strongly nonlinear at small radii — going from sharp to a small fillet buys most of the improvement.- Fillet transitions between sections too. A sudden change from thick to thin is a stress raiser even without a corner. Taper it.- Watch the printing rules (Post 06): fillet vertical and top edges freely; chamfer bottom edges.- Do not fillet a corner that needs to mate flush with something. Chamfer that one, or add a relief groove on the mating part. Chapter 7 — Testing, and when not to print Test the part, not a coupon Standardised tensile coupons (ISO 527, ASTM D638) exist for a reason: they let laboratories compare materials on equal terms. They are the right tool for material comparison and the wrong tool for answering "will my bracket hold". A coupon tells you about a dogbone specimen printed in one orientation. Your part has a specific geometry, specific stress concentrations, a specific orientation, a specific load path, specific infill, and a specific set of fillets and section changes. Those interactions dominate. Test the actual part, loaded the way it will actually be loaded. A repeatable comparative test rig You are not trying to produce absolute engineering data. You are trying to answer "is A better than B on my machine". That requires only repeatability and a controlled comparison — both achievable with cheap equipment. The minimum viable rig: 1. A fixture that holds the part exactly the same way every time. This is the most important part. Print a jig that clamps the part in one repeatable position. Variation in how the part is held will swamp everything else.2. A way to apply load progressively and measure it. Options in ascending order of cost: a bucket you add water or sand to and then weigh; a luggage scale or fish scale pulled by hand; a hanging digital scale with a peak-hold function; a lever arm with hanging weights; an inexpensive load cell with an amplifier logging to a microcontroller.3. A record. Load at first audible crack, load at failure, where it failed, a photograph of the fracture. The disciplines that make the results mean something: - Change exactly one variable at a time. Same spool, same day, same profile except the one thing being tested.- Print all specimens in the same batch, on the same plate where possible, so filament and machine state are shared.- n ≥ 3 per condition. Five is better. Printed parts scatter; a single specimen tells you nothing, exactly as in Post 05.- Report the spread, not just the mean. If A's mean beats B's by less than the within-group spread, you have not shown anything.- Note the temperature. Polymer strength is temperature-dependent, so a test in a cold garage is not comparable to one in a warm room. Anisotropy test procedure Print the same simple beam three times in each of three orientations — flat, on-edge, and upright — with everything else identical. Break each in three-point bending in your fixture. The upright specimens fail across layers; the others do not. The ratio of mean failure loads is your anisotropy figure, for that material and profile. > Common pitfall: comparing A against B when the two batches were printed on different days, from different spools, or with the fixture reassembled in between. Any of those can shift the result by more than the effect you are trying to measure, and the comparison then tells you about the day rather than about the change. Every escape, double and positional shot uses a cushion. "Angle in equals angle out" is a good start, but pace and spin bend it. This lesson covers how the cushion meets the ball, the mirror point and when it works, how pace and spin bend the line, how far the white comes back off a cushion, running and check side (and when they matter), two-cushion routes, a ball on the cushion, and the white touching the cushion. > Series map. ★ Basics: 01 Cue action and elevation → 02 Aiming and potting → 03 Tip and power → 04 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions (this post) → 08 Doubles · ★★★ Advanced: 09 Escaping snookers → 10 Multi-ball shots → 11 Safety → 12 Position play · ★★★★ Hard: 13 Break-building → 14 Special shots · Reference: 15 Rules, tactics and practice · ★★★★★ Pro: 16 Cue-ball control. Learning Outcome - Know where the cushion touches the ball, and why it takes spin off.- Find a cushion point with the mirror point, and adjust it for pace and spin.- Control how far the white comes back off a cushion.- Use running side and check side, and know when side makes a big difference.- Plan a two-cushion route.- Pot a ball sitting on the cushion, and play the white when it touches the cushion. Chapter 1 — How the cushion works 1 · Where the cushion meets the ball The cushion's nose is a little above the middle of the ball, about 64% of its height in the model. So the push from the cushion points slightly down, pressing the ball into the cloth during the hit. The cloth then grips it, and most of the white's spin comes off at the cushion. This is why, after a cushion, spin effects are smaller than you might expect, and why the angle off the cushion is the thing that changes. 2 · The mirror point, and when it works To send the white off a cushion to a spot: reflect the spot behind the cushion (the mirror point) and aim at it. That works when the white is still sliding at the cushion. A white that is already rolling comes off a little flatter and runs on. | Hit | Where it goes (model, 80 cm to the cushion) ||-----|---------------------------------------------|| 1 · power 3, centre ball (rolling by the cushion) | about 12 cm past the target ✗ || 2 · the same aim, power 4 (still sliding) | within 4 cm ✓ || 3 · power 3, aim about 5 cm shorter along the cushion | on target ✓ | Two good answers: play it firmer, or keep the slow pace and aim shorter. Choose by where you want the white to stop. > Trick: reflect the target in the line where the white's centre touches the cushion, half a ball out from the rubber, not in the rubber itself. 3 · Pace and spin bend the line The same line in at 45°, four hits: | Hit | Off the cushion at… | Then ||-----|---------------------|------|| slow roll (power 2, a little above centre) | 35° | almost straight || firm roll (power 4, a little above centre) | 39° | straight || stun (power 3, below centre, sliding at the cushion) | 47° | straight: angle in = angle out || screw (power 4, below centre, still spinning back) | 48° | bends away, to 52° | A rolling white comes off flatter (the slower, the flatter), a stun follows the mirror, and backspin makes it steeper and bends it away. 4 · Straight in and back: the tip sets the distance Four whites, 60 cm from the cushion, straight in at power 2. Only the tip changes (model): | Tip | Comes back from the cushion ||-----|-----------------------------|| top (7/10 height) | 117 cm || centre | 56 cm || a little low | 23 cm || low | 8 cm | A high tip rolls from the start and arrives with the most pace. A low tip spends pace on the cloth while its backspin wears off (post 03), so it arrives slowly and dies near the cushion. > Trick: to leave the white tight on a cushion (a good safety, post 11), go straight in with a low tip and soft pace. Chapter 2 — Side at the cushion 5 · Running side and check side - Running side makes the white run on: it comes off wider (flatter to the cushion) and keeps more pace.- Check side holds it: it comes off narrower (squarer) and slower. Which is which? Look at the way the white will turn off the cushion. Here it turns right, so right-hand side is running side. 6 · Side matters most when you hit the cushion square The same side, two lines in (model, power 3, side 0.6 R): | Line in | Check | Plain | Running | Side changes it by ||---------|-------|-------|---------|--------------------|| 30° (shallow) | 17° | 18° | 14° | 3° || 60° (square-ish) | 62° | 55° | 33° | 29° | On a shallow line, the white is already sliding along the cushion and side has little to add. Coming in squarer, side changes everything. So when you need side for the angle, come into the cushion squarer. 7 · Watch it: running side opens the angle <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b07clipside.webp" alt="Clip: the white is played into the top side cushion with right-hand running side at power 3. It comes off wide and runs on down the table towards the far cushion"></div> Right-hand side, power 3. Off the cushion, the white runs on down the table instead of coming back across. 8 · Two cushions for position The same aim, into the top cushion near the corner and off the end cushion, power 3 (model): | Tip | Where the white ends ||-----|----------------------|| check side | stays near the end cushion, after two cushions || plain (a touch of top) | lower down the right half, after three || running side | right across into the other half, after three | Each cushion multiplies a small change in the angle. That makes two-cushion routes powerful for position, and also less accurate: pick the route with the widest landing area, not the cleverest one. Chapter 3 — Balls on the cushion 9 · Potting a ball on the cushion <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b07cliprail.webp" alt="Clip: a red sits against the top side cushion 70 cm from the corner pocket. A yellow mark shows the point on the cushion just in front of it. The white hits the cushion and the red at the same moment, and the red runs along the cushion into the corner pocket"></div> A red frozen on the cushion is hard to pot along it. If the white hits the red first, the red bounces off the cushion and away from the pocket. Hit the cushion and the red at the same moment (the cushion a hair first is fine). In the clip, power 3, the white's centre aims about 4 cm to the cushion side of the red's centre, and the window is tiny. > Common pitfall: playing it soft "to be safe". In the model, at power 2 no aim potted this red; at power 3 the "together" aim did. 10 · The white touching the cushion When the white touches the cushion and you cue away from it, the cushion and the rail are right behind the white: - The cue has to pass over the cushion, so the tip can only reach the top of the white: at least about 0.55 R above centre (model sizes).- The cue rises about 12° to clear the rail's edge. So from the cushion, you play with topspin: no screw, no real stun. Plan the next shot for a white that follows, and keep the side small (a raised cue with side swerves, post 05).
