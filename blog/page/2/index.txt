e:T4366,A language model knows nothing about your own notes. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) fixes that without any training: when a question arrives, find the few passages of your documents that answer it, put them in the prompt, and ask the model to answer from those passages, with citations. This post builds a complete RAG tool in about 180 lines of Python, one piece per chapter: load Markdown files, chunk them by heading, embed them with a free local model, store the vectors in a NumPy file, retrieve the top matches, and generate a cited answer with the Anthropic Messages API. Then it measures retrieval with a small hand-written test set (hit rate and MRR), improves it with hybrid search and reranking, and covers the ways RAG fails. The running example is "ask questions about your own notes", for instance a blog's posts folder. It builds on the embeddings from post 17.

Learning Outcome

- Explain what RAG is, when it helps, and when something simpler is better.- Chunk Markdown by heading, with size limits and overlap, without breaking on code comments.- Embed chunks, store them as a NumPy matrix, and retrieve the top-k by cosine similarity.- Build a prompt that makes the model cite sources and treat documents as data.- Evaluate retrieval with hit rate@k and MRR on a hand-written question set.- Improve retrieval with BM25 + vectors (reciprocal rank fusion), a cross-encoder reranker, and metadata filters.- Recognise the main failure modes: bad chunks, stale index, prompt injection inside documents.

Chapter 1 — RAG in one picture

Terminology

| Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Chunk | A small piece of a document, the unit you embed and retrieve. || Index | The stored chunks plus their vectors. || Retrieval | Finding the chunks most related to a question. Top-k = the best k of them. || Grounding | Making the model answer from supplied text instead of its own memory. || BM25 | A classic keyword-matching score, used by search engines for decades. || Reranker | A slower, more accurate model that re-orders a short list of candidates. || Hit rate@k, MRR | Two simple retrieval scores (Chapter 6). |

The name comes from Lewis et al. (NeurIPS 2020), who combined a model's built-in ("parametric") memory with a searchable document index. Today the word covers any system that retrieves text and puts it in the prompt.

Why not just paste everything into the prompt? Sometimes you should. If your documents fit in a few thousand tokens, paste them and use prompt caching (post 17). RAG earns its place when the collection is too big for the context window, or when stuffing it in would be slow, costly, or hurt accuracy (the "context rot" from post 17). It also makes answers checkable: every claim points to a chunk.

When RAG is the wrong tool: questions that need every document at once ("how many posts mention Docker?") need a query or a script, not top-5 retrieval.

Why chunk, and how big

- The embedding model has an input limit. The  model card says input longer than 256 word pieces is truncated. Anything past the limit is silently ignored.- One vector should mean one topic. A vector for a whole long post is a blurry average. A vector for one section is sharp.- Smaller chunks retrieve more precisely but carry less context. Larger chunks carry more context but match less precisely.- Overlap repeats a little text between neighbouring chunks, so a sentence cut at a boundary still appears whole in one of them.

This post uses 800 characters with 150 of overlap. That is a rule of thumb for English prose staying under 256 word pieces. To check a chunk, count its word pieces with .

Chapter 2 — Streaming with Suspense

What streaming does

The App Router streams HTML. React sends the shell first with Suspense fallbacks in place, keeps the connection open, and later sends each boundary's content with a small inline script that swaps it in. The same happens for the RSC payload on client navigations. The user sees useful content as soon as the fastest part is ready instead of when the slowest is.

Status codes after the first byte

Once the first chunk is sent, the HTTP status line and headers are gone. If , ,  or  runs inside a boundary that already streamed, the response status stays 200. Next.js handles the UI (and inserts a  robots meta tag for a streamed not-found), but monitoring that counts 404s by status code will miss them.

> Trick: call  for a missing resource before any Suspense boundary (at the top of the page, after the lookup), so the real 404 status is sent. Only data that is allowed to fail softly belongs inside the streamed parts.

Buffering anywhere between the server and the browser collapses a stream into one late response. Self-hosted, disable Nginx buffering (Next.js docs suggest the  header) and check that load balancers support chunked transfer or HTTP/2 streaming. Some cloud load balancers buffer by default.

Chapter 4 — Partial Prerendering and Cache Components

From "static or dynamic" to "both, per component"

Partial Prerendering (PPR) was introduced as an experiment in Next.js 14 (2023), offered as an incremental opt-in ( per route) in 15, and folded into Cache Components in 16, which removed the  flag. With , PPR is simply how the App Router renders.

The model: at build time Next.js renders every route as far as it can. Everything that is static or cached () becomes the static shell. Every Suspense boundary whose content needs request data or uncached data becomes a dynamic hole. At request time, the shell is sent immediately (it can come from a CDN) and the holes are rendered and streamed into the same response.

The three errors you will meet

With Cache Components, Next.js validates every route in development and at build time. Every piece of data must be one of: cached (), inside a Suspense boundary (a dynamic hole), or static. The errors map to those rules:

Chapter 6 — Node.js vs Edge runtime

The Edge runtime is a restricted, Web-API-only environment (no , no native Node.js modules, no , no  or , no ISR) designed to start fast and run close to users. The Node.js runtime has the full Node.js API and is the default.

By 2026 the balance has moved decisively to Node.js:

-  runs only on Node.js (16.0).- Cache Components requires the Node.js runtime (16.0).- The route segment option  is marked deprecated in the docs (as of 16.3), with the advice to remove it; the error page  says the Node.js runtime needs no replacement.- Next.js 16.3 replaced web streams with native Node.js streams in the App Router rendering layer and reports up to 22% more requests handled under load.

| Concern | Node.js runtime | Edge runtime ||---|---|---|| APIs | Full Node.js | Web APIs subset +  polyfill || npm packages | All | ESM, no native Node.js APIs || ISR / Cache Components | Yes | No || Status in 16 | Default everywhere |  deprecated;  (Edge) deprecated || Where the latency goes | Near your database | Near the user, far from the database |

The latency row is the one people forget: a function at the edge that makes three sequential queries to a database in one region pays the long round trip three times. Rendering next to the data and caching the output at the CDN usually wins.

Chapter 1 — Saying what you mean

Most confusion about printer accuracy is vocabulary confusion. These terms are not synonyms and the rest of the post keeps them strictly distinct.

Terminology

- Nominal size — the number in the CAD model. A design intent, not a prediction.- Actual size — what a specific physical feature measures, on a specific instrument, at a specific time and temperature.- Deviation (error) — actual minus nominal, signed. Sign matters: a hole 0.15 mm undersize and a hole 0.15 mm oversize are opposite problems with opposite fixes.- Accuracy (formally, trueness) — how close the mean of many measurements is to the true value. Accuracy is about the centre of the distribution.- Precision — how tightly repeated results cluster, independent of whether they cluster in the right place. Precision is about the spread.- Repeatability — spread when the same operator measures the same feature with the same instrument under the same conditions, or when the same machine prints the same part repeatedly without changes.- Reproducibility — spread when something legitimately changes: a different operator, a different instrument, a different day, a new spool of the same filament, the same model on a different printer.- Resolution — the smallest increment an instrument can display, or the smallest increment a machine can command. It is a property of the readout or the motion system, and it bounds nothing else.- Uncertainty — the interval within which the true value plausibly lies, given the instrument, the method and the operator. Always larger than the resolution.- Tolerance — the permitted deviation, decided by the designer from the function of the part. A tolerance is a requirement, not a measurement.- Systematic error (bias) — a repeatable, signed offset. Can be measured and compensated.- Random error — scatter with no fixed sign. Can be characterised statistically and reduced by averaging, but not compensated away.

The four-quadrant picture, stated plainly

| | Precise | Not precise ||---|---|---|| Accurate | every part right, every time — the goal | on average right, individually unpredictable || Not accurate | every part wrong by the same amount — compensable | every part wrong by a different amount — the hard case |

The bottom-left quadrant is the most important thing in this post. A machine that is precise but not accurate is a good machine: the error is systematic, you measure it once, and you compensate for it. A machine that is imprecise cannot be fixed by compensation, because there is no single number to compensate by. So the first diagnostic question is never "is it accurate?" — it is "is it repeatable?"

Why "0.05 mm resolution" is a meaningless accuracy claim

Marketing copy quotes numbers like "0.0125 mm XY resolution". That figure is a motion increment: the linear distance corresponding to one microstep of a stepper motor, derived from the motor's step angle, the microstepping setting, and the belt pitch or lead screw pitch. It is arithmetic on the kinematics.

It says nothing about:

- whether the belt is tensioned identically in both directions (backlash),- whether the frame is square,- whether the extruder delivers the commanded volume of plastic (flow),- whether the extrusion width matches what the slicer assumed,- whether the filament is the diameter the slicer was told,- how much the part shrinks as it cools,- whether the motor actually reaches every commanded microstep under load, which at high microstepping ratios it frequently does not.

A stepper motor's ability to command a 0.0125 mm move does not mean the toolhead goes there, and even if it did, the plastic that ends up on the plate is not the toolhead path. Treat advertised resolution as a specification of the controller, not of the part.

> Watch out: the same confusion appears on the CAD side. A modeller displaying 20.0000 mm is showing you the precision of a floating-point representation, not a claim about anything physical. Digits in a CAD field are free; micrometres on a plate are not.

Chapter 2 — The instrument is part of the experiment

Before measuring error, establish that the measurement itself is not the largest source of error. On printed polymer parts, it very often is.

Calipers versus micrometers

| | Digital calipers | Micrometer ||---|---|---|| Typical display resolution | 0.01 mm | 0.001 mm || Realistic uncertainty in normal use | far worse than the display — commonly a few hundredths of a mm | roughly an order of magnitude better || Measuring force | uncontrolled, entirely operator-dependent | controlled by a ratchet or friction thimble || Reach | external, internal, depth, step | external only (for a standard outside micrometer) || Cost | low | moderate || Right for | almost everything in this post | verifying a critical dimension, or calibrating your calipers' bias |

The decisive difference is measuring force. Calipers have no force control at all: how hard you squeeze determines the reading. On steel this barely matters. On a printed polymer part — a material with a modulus orders of magnitude below steel, and a surface made of rounded extrusion beads rather than a plane — it matters enormously. Squeezing calipers hard on a PLA cube can easily change the reading by more than the deviation you are trying to measure, and on TPU it is hopeless.

A micrometer's ratchet applies a repeatable force. That is why a micrometer is more precise, not the extra digit.

> Trick: to find out how much your grip is worth, measure the same feature ten times deliberately gently and ten times deliberately firmly. The difference between the two means is your personal measuring-force error. Everyone should know this number about themselves. It is usually larger than expected.

The other things that move the reading

- Zero. Close the jaws, check zero, re-zero, before every measurement session and again if you drop the tool. A caliper that drifted 0.03 mm off zero produces a beautifully repeatable, entirely wrong dataset.- Temperature. Polymers have a coefficient of thermal expansion roughly an order of magnitude larger than steel. A part measured warm off the plate and the same part measured cold are different sizes, and your steel calipers are not expanding with it. Let parts equilibrate to room temperature — a couple of hours for anything substantial — and record the room temperature with the data.- Where on the feature. Printed surfaces are not planes. A measurement across the middle of a face, across a corner, at the first layer, and at the top of a 40 mm pillar are four different measurements of the same nominal dimension. Decide a location convention and record it.- Seam and blob. The layer seam is a local bulge. Measuring across a seam gives a systematically larger reading. Either measure away from the seam and say so, or measure across it deliberately and say that.- Bead geometry. The outer surface is a stack of rounded beads, not a flat face. Calipers contact bead crowns; a flat contact averages differently than a knife-edge contact.- Internal features are harder. Caliper internal jaws are stubby and imprecise, and on a hole they measure a chord, not necessarily the diameter. Pin gauges (or, adequately, drill bit shanks of known size, or ground dowel pins) measure a hole far better: the largest pin that enters is a real, functional, repeatable answer.

The measurement protocol

The whole of the rest of this post depends on measurements taken like this:

1. Let the part reach room temperature. Record the temperature.2. Zero the instrument. Verify against a known reference if you have one (a gauge block, a ground dowel pin, a new drill bit shank).3. Deburr nothing and clean nothing that changes the dimension; do remove obvious stringing and the brim.4. Take at least five readings of each feature, re-seating the instrument each time. Rotate the part between readings where the feature allows.5. Record every reading, not just the one you like.6. Report the mean and the spread (range or standard deviation). The mean estimates the bias; the spread estimates your precision.7. If the spread is comparable to the deviation you are chasing, stop — improve the measurement before drawing any conclusion about the printer.

> Common pitfall: measuring once, getting 19.86 on a 20 mm nominal, and concluding "my printer prints 0.14 mm small". One reading contains no information about its own reliability. It could be bias, it could be grip, it could be a seam, it could be the caliper's zero. Five readings tell you which.

Chapter 3 — The error budget

Here is the honest thesis of this post: the deviation of a printed dimension is a sum of contributions, most of which you can name, several of which you can measure, and a few of which you cannot control at all. Chasing accuracy without knowing which term dominates is how people spend weeks tuning the wrong thing.

| # | Contribution | Nature | Sign / effect | What to do about it ||---|---|---|---|---|| 1 | Filament diameter variation | random, batch-systematic | flow varies ⇒ width varies | measure the filament in several places with a micrometer; set the slicer's diameter to the measured mean, not the printed label || 2 | Flow / extrusion multiplier calibration | systematic | too much flow ⇒ external features large, holes small | calibrate flow (Post 01) before anything in this post || 3 | Extrusion width vs slicer geometry | systematic | the toolpath is offset by half the assumed width; if actual ≠ assumed, every wall is offset | verify extrusion width from a single-wall test; set it explicitly rather than leaving it "auto" || 4 | First-layer squish / elephant foot | systematic, local to Z ≈ 0 | first layers bulge outward | first-layer Z offset (Post 01); Note the structural distinction in row 5 versus row 4: thermal contraction scales with size, elephant foot does not. A contraction of 0.3 % is 0.06 mm on a 20 mm feature and 0.6 mm on a 200 mm feature. Elephant foot is roughly a fixed bulge regardless of part size. This is why a single global offset cannot fix both, and it is why Chapter 5's procedure sweeps a range of nominal sizes: only by looking at how error varies with size can you separate the offset term from the scale term.

The general model worth carrying in your head:

Fit that model to your own data and you know which knob to turn. Measure one cube and you cannot separate the terms at all.

Chapter 4 — The systematic errors, and their actual causes

These four account for the large majority of real-world fit failures.

Elephant foot

Symptom: the bottom one to three layers bulge outward. A part that measures correctly at mid-height is oversize at the base, and it will not sit flat in a recess or slide into a slot.

Cause: two things at once. The first layer is deliberately squashed into the plate for adhesion, which displaces material sideways. And the first layers cool against a heated bed that keeps them soft, under the weight and pressure of everything printed above.

Fixes, in the order to try them:

1. Get the first-layer Z offset right (Post 01). Over-squish is the root cause; everything else is compensation.2. Chamfer the bottom edge in CAD — a 0.4–0.6 mm chamfer on the bottom perimeter. This is the most robust fix because it survives moving the model to a different printer, a different slicer or a different profile. It costs one CAD feature.3. Slicer elephant-foot compensation — shrinks the first layer's perimeter by a set amount. Effective, but it is a profile setting that does not travel with the model.4. Reduce bed temperature slightly if the material allows.

Holes come out undersize

Symptom: a 5 mm hole measures under 5 mm, and a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse. This is the single most reported dimensional complaint in FDM, and it has two independent causes that add.

Cause A — polygonal chord error. The slicer works on a triangle mesh. A cylindrical hole in CAD becomes an N-sided polygon on export, and the toolpath follows that polygon. Because the mesh vertices sit on the true circle and the polygon edges cut inside it, the printed hole is inscribed in the nominal circle and is therefore smaller. The error grows as the facet count falls and as the hole gets smaller relative to the facet size. This error is created in CAD export, not in the printer — see Post 06 on chord/deviation tolerance.

Cause B — extrusion overlap on a concave path. When the nozzle traverses a concave (inward-curving) path, the deposited bead's outer material is compressed toward the inside of the curve. The bead does not simply lie along the commanded centreline; it bulges into the hole. Small holes have tighter curvature, so the effect is larger — which is why a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse than a 12 mm one. This is a real physical effect of extruding a finite-width bead along a curve, and it cannot be fixed in CAD export.

Fixes:

1. Export with a tight chord tolerance so the facets are fine (Post 06). This removes Cause A entirely and costs nothing but file size.2. Print, measure, and compensate. Because Cause B depends on hole size, the compensation is a curve, not a constant — hence the sweep in Chapter 5.3. For holes that must fit a fastener or a shaft, drill or ream to size after printing. A printed hole reamed with a standard drill or reamer is dramatically more accurate than any compensation scheme, and this is a legitimate professional answer, not a cop-out.4. Slicer hole compensation where available (Cura's Hole Horizontal Expansion, OrcaSlicer's X-Y hole compensation). Note that PrusaSlicer's XY size compensation is not hole-specific: it grows or shrinks all perimeters, so a positive value that enlarges the outside simultaneously shrinks holes.

> Watch out: never design a hole at exactly the nominal shaft size and expect it to fit. A 3 mm hole for a 3 mm shaft will not accept the shaft on any FDM printer without help. Clearance is designed in, not hoped for — Chapter 6.

External dimensions large, internal features small

Symptom: the outside of the box is 20.15 on a 20 nominal, and the pocket inside it is 19.85 on a 20 nominal. Both errors are the same sign of material excess.

Cause: any excess deposited material grows the part outward from the toolpath in both cases. On an external wall that means larger; on an internal wall it means the cavity is smaller. Over-extrusion, an extrusion width larger than the slicer assumed, or a too-hot first layer all produce this signature.

This is the diagnostic signature of over-extrusion, and it is why both an external and an internal feature must be in the test artefact. Measuring only external features and compensating them makes internal features worse.

X/Y and Z behave differently

Z is not the same problem as X and Y and should never be lumped with them.

| | X / Y | Z ||---|---|---|| Feature size determined by | extrusion width and toolpath geometry | layer height and the number of layers || Quantisation | effectively continuous | discrete — heights land on multiples of layer height || Typical accuracy | worse — the error budget above | often better, because Z steps are mechanically direct (lead screws) and there is no bead-width term || Error mechanism | flow, width, curvature, compensation | first-layer offset (a fixed error on every Z dimension), plus quantisation || Compensation | XY size compensation / hole compensation | design to layer-height multiples; correct the first-layer offset |

The important asymmetry: your first-layer Z offset applies a constant error to every Z dimension in the part, in the same direction, regardless of height. Squish the first layer 0.05 mm extra and every Z dimension is 0.05 mm short. That is a systematic, easily measured, easily corrected error — and it is a completely different animal from the size-proportional errors in X and Y.

> Trick: measure a tall feature's height and a short feature's height on the same print. If both are short by the same amount, it is first-layer offset. If the tall one is short by proportionally more, it is steps/mm or thermal contraction. The pattern of the error tells you the cause.

Chapter 5 — The calibration-cube fallacy, and what to do instead

Why the 20 mm cube proves almost nothing

The 20 mm calibration cube is the most-printed object in the hobby and one of the least informative. Consider what it can and cannot tell you.

What it does test: whether a 20 mm external dimension in X and in Y comes out near 20 mm, once.

What it cannot tell you:

- Anything about internal features. It has no holes. It therefore cannot detect the over-extrusion signature above, which is the most common real cause of fit failure.- Anything about size dependence. One nominal size cannot separate the offset term from the scale term of the error model. A cube that measures 20.10 could be a +0.10 mm offset or a +0.5 % scale error; those need completely different fixes and the cube cannot distinguish them.- Anything about repeatability. One cube is n = 1. It contains no information about spread.- Anything about fits. The actual engineering question is almost never "is a 20 mm face 20 mm?" It is "does this 3 mm pin go into this 3 mm hole with the right amount of friction?" The cube is silent on that.- Anything about your other materials or profiles. The result is specific to the filament, temperature, speed and layer height used.

A printer can produce a perfect 20 mm cube and still fail to produce a hole a shaft will enter. A 20 mm cube validates a 20 mm cube.

Reading your own results

Interpretation, and this is the payoff of the whole chapter:

| What the fit shows | What it means | Where to fix it ||---|---|---|| Large offset, scale ≈ 0, external positive and internal negative | over-extrusion or extrusion width mismatch | flow calibration; then slicer XY size compensation || Large offset, external and internal same sign | toolpath/compensation offset, not flow | slicer XY size compensation || Offset ≈ 0, significant negative scale | thermal contraction, or steps/mm slightly low | scale factor, or fix steps/mm — not an offset || Internal deviation grows sharply as holes get smaller | curvature/overlap effect (Cause B above) | hole-specific compensation, or ream to size || Range comparable to deviation | you are measuring noise | fix the measurement (Chapter 2) before compensating anything || Print A/B/C means differ as much as the deviation | the machine is not repeatable | compensation cannot help — find the mechanical cause first |

> Common pitfall: adjusting steps/mm to fix a dimensional error. Steps/mm is determined by belt pitch, pulley tooth count and microstepping — it is arithmetic, and on a correctly built machine it is already right. If a 25 mm feature is 0.15 mm off, that is almost certainly flow, width or contraction, not steps/mm. Changing steps/mm to compensate for a flow error introduces a scale error to cancel an offset error, which cancels correctly at exactly one size and is wrong everywhere else.

Chapter 6 — Designing fits

The concept, borrowed correctly

Mechanical engineering has a mature vocabulary for how two mating features relate. ISO 286 ("Geometrical product specifications (GPS) — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes") defines it: a hole and a shaft each get a tolerance zone, described by a letter (position of the zone relative to nominal) and a grade number (width of the zone, IT01 through IT18). The classic designations — H7/g6, H7/p6 — come from that system, and fits fall into three classes:

- Clearance fit — the hole is always larger than the shaft. There is always a gap. Parts assemble by hand and can move or rotate.- Transition fit — the tolerance zones overlap. Depending where each part lands within its zone, the assembly may be slightly loose or slightly tight. Used for accurate location with hand assembly.- Interference (press) fit — the shaft is always larger than the hole. Assembly requires force, heating or cooling, and the joint transmits torque by friction.

Now the honest part: ISO 286 fit classes are not directly applicable to FDM printing. Borrow the three concepts; do not copy the values. The reasons are specific:

1. The IT grades assume machining processes with characterised, stable capability. FDM's capability is not that, and it is different for every machine, material and profile.2. The system assumes tolerance zones roughly symmetric about a predictable process centre. FDM's bias is strongly feature-dependent — external and internal features err in opposite directions, and small holes err differently from large ones.3. FDM surfaces are made of rounded beads with layer-scale texture. "Size" on such a surface is less well-defined than on a machined one, which undermines the measurement the system is built on.4. The material is compliant and viscoelastic. A polymer press fit relaxes over time (creep — Post 07); a steel one does not.

So: use "I want a clearance fit here and an interference fit there" as design language. Then determine the actual clearance experimentally.

The fit-test gauge

The right tool is a printed gauge that sweeps clearance in known steps against a known reference. Use a real reference object rather than another printed part, so you are measuring one unknown instead of two: a ground dowel pin, a drill bit shank, an M3 screw, a 608 bearing, a specific connector.

Record the result as a judgement, not a number, because the judgement is what you actually want to reproduce:

| Clearance | Result | Classification ||---|---|---|| 0.00 | | won't enter / needs force / press || 0.05 | | || 0.10 | | || 0.15 | | || 0.20 | | || 0.25 | | || 0.30 | | || 0.40 | | || 0.50 | | free / sloppy |

From one gauge you get your machine's four working numbers: press fit, tight locating fit, sliding fit, free-running fit. Write them into your CAD parameter file as named variables (Post 06) and stop guessing.

Three things that change the answer and must be held constant or re-tested:

- Material. PETG and PLA do not give the same clearances. Neither do two colours (Post 04).- Engagement depth. A 2 mm-deep hole and a 20 mm-deep hole with identical diameters behave completely differently. Test at a realistic depth.- Orientation. A hole printed with its axis vertical (a circle in the layer plane) and one printed horizontally (an arch, with bridging at the top) are different features with different errors. Test the orientation you will use.

> Trick: design a deliberately adjustable interface where you can — a slot instead of a hole, a screw-clamped collar instead of a press fit, a shim pocket. One adjustable feature absorbs the whole tolerance stack of the assembly and turns a metrology problem into a five-second assembly step.

Compensating in CAD versus in the slicer

Both are legitimate. They belong in different places, and mixing them without noticing is how people end up double-compensating.

| Compensate in CAD | Compensate in the slicer ||---|---|| The correction is a design decision: a functional clearance, a bottom chamfer, a wall thickness set to a multiple of extrusion width | The correction is a machine/process characteristic: your printer's systematic offset for this material and profile || Travels with the model to any printer, slicer or person | Lives in the profile; correctly re-applied to every model you print with that profile || Correct for: fit clearances, chamfers, thread reliefs, features sized to nozzle geometry | Correct for: global XY size compensation, hole compensation, elephant-foot compensation || Wrong for: your machine's private bias — you would bake a local defect into a shared file | Wrong for: functional clearances — the slicer setting applies to every feature, including the ones that were already fine |

The rule: design intent goes in CAD, machine bias goes in the slicer. A clearance of 0.2 mm because a shaft must rotate is design intent — it belongs in the model and should be a named parameter. A −0.06 mm XY compensation because your extruder runs slightly hot is machine bias — it belongs in the profile, and it should not follow the model to someone else's printer.

> Watch out: if you compensate in the slicer and then adjust the model because parts still do not fit, you now have two corrections that must be maintained together, and neither is documented. Pick one place per cause, and write down what you did.

Chapter 7 — Stack-up, standards, and the argument

Tolerance stack-up

Errors add along an assembly path. Three parts stacked, each within ±0.15 mm, can be ±0.45 mm out at the end in the worst case. Design a mechanism as a chain of five tightly-toleranced printed features and you have designed something that will not assemble.

Two ways to add up the contributions:

- Worst case (arithmetic sum). Add all the tolerances. Guarantees assembly, and is conservative — it assumes every part is simultaneously at its extreme, which is unlikely.- Statistical (root-sum-square). If the individual errors are independent and roughly centred, the combined spread grows like the square root of the sum of squares — much smaller than the arithmetic sum. Valid only when the errors really are independent and centred, and FDM errors often are neither: a whole batch printed from one spool on one day shares the same bias, which is precisely the correlated case RSS is invalid for.

For hobby and small-batch printing, the honest position is: use worst-case reasoning, and design so that the worst case is absorbed rather than resisted.

Techniques that absorb stack-up, roughly in order of usefulness:

1. Make one dimension in the chain adjustable. A slot, an oversized clearance hole with a washer, a screw-adjusted stop, a shim. One adjustable feature is worth ten tight tolerances.2. Shorten the chain. Print two mating features as one part rather than three parts that must align. This is FDM's genuine superiority over machining — arbitrary geometry is free, so consolidate.3. Locate on a datum, not on an accumulation. Reference every feature to one face rather than each to the previous one, so errors do not accumulate down the chain.4. Use compliance deliberately. A printed snap-fit, a flexure, a slightly springy tab tolerates variation that a rigid interface will not.5. Post-process the one critical feature. Ream the bearing hole. Tap the thread. Face the mating surface flat with sandpaper on a granite tile. One machined feature on an otherwise printed part is cheap and effective.

Where the standards fit

| Standard | What it is | Applicability to FDM ||---|---|---|| ISO/ASTM 52900:2021 | Additive manufacturing — general principles, fundamentals and vocabulary | Directly applicable. The terminology standard for the field; use its vocabulary in anything you write for others || ISO 286-1 / -2:2010 | GPS — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes; the H7/g6 system | Concepts applicable, values not. Borrow clearance/transition/interference as language; determine values experimentally || ISO 2768-1 | General tolerances for linear and angular dimensions — the "f / m / c / v" classes on a title block | A useful comparison point, not a specification you can claim. It was written for machined and formed parts; a printed part is not automatically within any of its classes, and claiming a class on a drawing means you have demonstrated capability || ISO 22081:2021 | General geometrical specifications; supersedes ISO 2768-2 (geometrical part), and supplies rules rather than values | Relevant if you produce drawings to a modern GPS framework || ISO 10303 (STEP) AP242 | Product data interchange | Post 06 — the format for exchanging solid geometry with units intact |

The reason ISO 2768's "medium" class is worth looking at is calibration of expectations: it gives a sense of what a workshop-grade general tolerance looks like at each size range. Compare your measured spread against it and you learn something honest about where your machine sits. What you must not do is print the class in a title block and imply conformance you have not demonstrated.

> Watch out: none of these standards will make your printer more accurate. Their value is in giving you a precise, shared way to say what you require and what you achieved. That is not nothing — most disagreement about printed part quality is people using the same words to mean different things.

The closing argument

Everything above converges on one position, and it is the same position the planned furniture-CAD series will argue about woodworking:

CAD precision does not survive contact with the physical world.

The number in the model is exact. The part is not, and the material is not, and the machine is not, and the measurement is not. Every physical process has a distribution of outcomes, and the designer's job is not to eliminate that distribution — that is impossible — but to design something that works across it.

Three consequences follow, and they are the practical content of the whole post:

1. A design that only works if a tolerance is met is a fragile design. If the assembly requires a 0.1 mm clearance and the process delivers ±0.15 mm, it will fail, and no amount of care changes that. Redesign the interface — make it adjustable, make it compliant, or consolidate the parts — rather than demanding a capability the process does not have.2. Measure your own process, then design inside what it can do. The offset and scale you fit in Chapter 5, and the clearances you found in Chapter 6, are your process capability. They are the design constraint. Someone else's numbers are not data about your machine.3. Where precision is genuinely required, get it from a process that has it. Ream the hole. Tap the thread. Press in a heat-set insert. Use a bought bearing, a bought shaft, a bought fastener. FDM's strength is arbitrary geometry produced quickly and cheaply, not precision — and combining it with a precise process at the two or three points that need precision is a better engineering answer than fighting the printer for a capability it does not have.

The most valuable skill in this entire course is not making an accurate print. It is knowing which dimensions need to be accurate — usually far fewer than beginners assume — and designing so the rest do not have to be.

Chapter 1 — Tokens and tokenizers

Setup

You need Python 3.10 or newer (the  1.x SDK requires it) and, for the API examples, a key from the Anthropic Console.

Terminology

| Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Token | One chunk of text from the model's fixed list: a word, part of a word, a space plus a word, a symbol, or a byte. || Vocabulary | The fixed list of all tokens a model knows. Each token has an integer ID. || Tokenizer | The program that turns text into token IDs and back. It is fixed before training. || BPE | Byte Pair Encoding: build a vocabulary by merging the most frequent neighbouring pieces, over and over. |

Tokens are not words

The model never sees letters or words. It sees a list of integers. A common word may be one token; a rare word may be three or four. Spaces, punctuation and line breaks use tokens too.

A rule of thumb from Anthropic's models page (vendor spec, September 2026): on the tokenizer Claude has used since Claude Opus 4.7, 1M tokens is roughly 555k English words, or 2.5M Unicode characters. Earlier models fit about 750k words in 1M tokens. So the same text gives a different count on a different model.

Tokens matter because almost every limit uses them: price (per million input and output tokens), the context window (Chapter 4),  (the cap on what the model writes) and rate limits.

BPE in one example

Byte Pair Encoding was brought into neural machine translation by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch (ACL 2016) to handle rare words as sequences of smaller units. Training: split every word into characters, count every neighbouring pair, merge the most frequent pair, repeat. Tokenizing: replay the merges in the same order. Many modern tokenizers run the same idea on UTF-8 bytes, so any text can be encoded (see Dev Essentials 01 · Text Encoding).

Why code and non-English text often cost more

Merges are learned from the tokenizer's training data. Whatever is common there gets long tokens.

- Non-English text. Petrov et al. (NeurIPS 2023) tokenized the same text translated into many languages and found lengths that differ by up to 15 times in some cases (independent measurement). A Chinese character is also 3 bytes in UTF-8, so a byte-level tokenizer starts from more pieces.- Code. The Codex paper (Chen et al., 2021) found the GPT-3 text tokenizer "not very effective for representing code", with whitespace the largest waste. Adding tokens for runs of spaces cut code token counts by about 30% (vendor research paper).

Every LLM answer comes from the same loop:

1. Turn the prompt into token IDs.2. Run them through the network. It returns one score (a logit) per vocabulary token.3. Turn the scores into probabilities and pick one token (Chapter 5).4. Append it and repeat. Stop at an end token, a stop sequence or .

A real LLM looks at all earlier tokens, its probabilities come from billions of learned parameters, and it works on subword tokens. The loop is the same.

Attention, at intuition level

Almost every modern LLM is a transformer, from "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017). Its key part is self-attention: for each token, the model decides how much each earlier token matters right now. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired", attention lets "it" pull in information from "animal". Each token makes a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?):

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{dk}}\right)V$$

For text generation, a causal mask stops each token from looking at later tokens. Real models stack many such layers, each with several attention "heads".

What this means for you:

- Output is written one token at a time, which is why APIs offer streaming. On Anthropic's current price list, output tokens cost five times as much as input tokens.- The model cannot go back. An early wrong token shapes everything after it. Reasoning before answering helps (Chapter 8).- Long inputs cost more compute. The paper lists self-attention cost per layer as $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ for sequence length $n$.

Chapter 3 — Training stages (high level)

| Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Pretraining | Next-token prediction on a huge text corpus. Produces a base model. || Instruction tuning (SFT) | Supervised fine-tuning on example requests with good answers. || Reward model | A model trained to score answers, from human rankings. || RLHF / RLAIF | Reinforcement learning from human / AI feedback. |

This is deliberately high level: labs combine and change these steps, and full recipes are not published.

1. Pretraining. The model learns to predict the next token over a very large text collection. It picks up grammar, facts, code patterns and reasoning habits, but does not reliably follow instructions yet.2. Instruction tuning. Fine-tuning on demonstrations: a request, then a good response.3. Preference training. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) describes classic RLHF: supervised fine-tuning on labeller demonstrations, then a reward model trained on rankings of model outputs, then reinforcement learning against it. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) replaces much human labelling for harmlessness: the model critiques and revises its own answers against written principles, and a preference model trained on AI comparisons gives the reward (RLAIF).

In practice:

- Knowledge has a cutoff. Anthropic lists a "reliable knowledge cutoff" per model (vendor spec): for example Feb 2025 for Claude Haiku 4.5 and Jun 2026 for Claude Opus 5.5.- The model knows nothing about your private code or data. You supply it through context: prompts, files, retrieved documents (post 19), tool results (post 18).

Chapter 4 — The context window

The context window is everything the model can read while writing a response, including the response itself. Anthropic's docs call it the model's "working memory". It counts the system prompt, every message (including tool results, images and documents), tool definitions, and this turn's output including any thinking.

The API is stateless. The model does not remember your last request, so a chat app resends the whole history every turn. Turn 10 pays for turns 1 to 9 again (Exercise 4 does the maths).

Sizes change often. As of September 2026 (vendor spec), current Claude Opus, Sonnet and Fable models have a 1M-token window and up to 128k output tokens per request; Claude Haiku 4.5 has 200k and 64k. When a request does not fit, the Anthropic API does this (vendor spec):

Chapter 5 — Sampling: temperature, top-k and top-p

Softmax turns logits $zi$ into probabilities. Temperature $T$ divides the logits first:

$$pi = \frac{e^{zi / T}}{\sumj e^{zj / T}}$$

$T < 1$ sharpens the distribution, $T > 1$ flattens it, and as $T$ approaches 0 sampling approaches greedy decoding (always the top token). Watch it with made-up logits for the word after "The cat sat on the":

Two filters cut the unlikely tail before sampling: top-k keeps the $k$ most likely tokens; top-p (nucleus sampling, Holtzman et al., ICLR 2020) keeps the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least $p$.

Why the same prompt gives different answers: above temperature 0 every token is a random draw, and one different early token changes everything after it. Even at 0, Anthropic's API reference says results will not be fully deterministic.

Chapter 7 — Why hallucinations happen and how to reduce them

A hallucination is fluent, confident text that is false or not supported by the given sources. Causes:

- The goal is a plausible next token, not a true statement. A made-up citation can look very plausible.- Training and tests reward guessing. Kalai et al. (2025, "Why Language Models Hallucinate") argue that training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, like an exam with no penalty for wrong answers.- Missing knowledge: after the cutoff, private, or rare.- Missing or buried context: not in the prompt, or lost in a very long one.

Fixes. The first five follow Anthropic's "Reduce hallucinations" guide and work with any model:

| Technique | How ||-----------|-----|| Allow "I don't know" | "If the documents do not contain the answer, say so." || Ground in sources | Put the documents in the prompt; tell the model to use only them. || Quote first | For long documents, extract word-for-word quotes, then answer from them. || Cite and check | One citation per claim; retract any claim without a supporting quote. || Compare several runs | Answers that disagree across runs are a warning sign. || Verify with tools | Run the code and tests; check official docs for any API or flag you do not know. |

The AI Prompting post covers prompt structure and evaluation in depth; post 18 applies "verify with tools" to coding.

Chapter 2 — Pyramid or trophy?

Both are models, not laws. They are opinions about where to spend effort.

| Layer (top to bottom) | Test pyramid (Cohn) | Testing trophy (Dodds) ||-----------------------|---------------------|------------------------|| End-to-end / UI | very few | few || Service / integration | some | most effort || Unit | most tests | some || Static (types, lint) | not part of the model | the base layer |

- Test pyramid. Mike Cohn described it in Succeeding with Agile (2009); Martin Fowler's 2012 "TestPyramid" article is a common reference. Many fast unit tests at the bottom, fewer service tests, very few slow UI tests on top.- Testing trophy. Kent C. Dodds sketched it in 2018, building on Guillermo Rauch's line: "Write tests. Not too many. Mostly integration." Its layers are static analysis, unit, integration and E2E, with the biggest share on integration, because those tests are closest to how the software is used while still being fairly fast.

Both agree on the important parts: E2E tests are few and precious, static checks (types, lint) are cheap wins, and every test must earn its cost. The trophy fits web apps where most bugs live between components. The pyramid fits libraries and logic-heavy code. Testcontainers (Chapter 6) makes integration tests cheaper than they used to be, which pushes many teams toward the trophy shape.

Chapter 1 — The polymer physics that predicts printing behaviour

Almost everything a filament does on the machine follows from two thermal transitions and one question about molecular order.

Terminology

- Glass transition temperature (Tg) — the temperature range over which an amorphous polymer (or the amorphous fraction of a semi-crystalline one) changes from a rigid, glassy solid into a soft, rubbery one. It is not a phase change with latent heat; it is a change in molecular mobility. Tg is a range, and the number a datasheet reports depends on the measurement method (DSC, DMA) and the heating rate.- Melting temperature (Tm) — the temperature at which crystalline regions melt. Only semi-crystalline polymers have a true Tm. Purely amorphous polymers soften progressively and have no sharp melting point.- Amorphous polymer — chains arranged with no long-range order, frozen in a tangle. Examples used in printing: ABS, ASA, PC, PETG (largely amorphous by design), most photopolymer resins after cure.- Semi-crystalline polymer — contains ordered crystalline domains embedded in amorphous regions. Examples: PLA (slow-crystallising, usually printed nearly amorphous), the nylons (PA6, PA12), PP, PEEK.- Heat deflection temperature (HDT) — the temperature at which a standard test bar deflects a specified amount under a specified load. A load-dependent engineering figure, not a material constant; always quoted with its stress level (commonly 0.45 MPa or 1.8 MPa).- Crystallinity — the mass fraction of the polymer that is in ordered crystalline domains. It changes with cooling rate and with annealing, and it drives stiffness, chemical resistance, opacity and shrinkage.

Why Tg, not Tm, sets the service temperature

A printed part carries load through solid polymer. Above Tg the amorphous fraction becomes rubbery: modulus drops by orders of magnitude, and a part that was rigid at room temperature will sag, creep and lose dimensional stability long before anything melts. This is why a PLA phone mount fails in a car parked in the sun even though PLA does not melt until far higher: the interior of a closed car in direct sun readily reaches the low end of PLA's Tg range.

Three consequences worth internalising:

1. Compare service temperature against Tg, with margin. Treat Tg as the ceiling, and derate further if the part is loaded — polymers creep faster as they approach Tg (Post 07).2. Tm is a printing parameter, not a service parameter. It tells you roughly where the extruder must operate. It tells you nothing useful about whether a bracket survives on an engine bay.3. HDT is closer to what you want but still not it. HDT is measured at a specific stress on a specific bar geometry, in a solid moulded specimen — not a 3-perimeter, 15 %-infill print with anisotropic layer bonding.

> Watch out: the Tg values circulating in the hobby community for a given material vary by 10–20 °C between sources, because different grades, different additives and different measurement methods genuinely give different numbers. Use them as a family ranking (PLA < PETG < ABS/ASA < PC), and use the specific spool's technical datasheet for anything that matters.

Amorphous vs semi-crystalline, and what it predicts

| Behaviour | Amorphous (ABS, ASA, PC, PETG) | Semi-crystalline (PA, PP, PLA when crystallised, PEEK) ||---|---|---|| Transition on cooling | gradual stiffening through Tg | crystallisation, with a volume step || Shrinkage on cooling | lower, mostly thermal contraction | higher — crystallisation densifies the material || Warping tendency | moderate to high (ABS) | high (PA, PP), or low if crystallisation is suppressed (PLA) || Chamber/enclosure benefit | large — keeps the part above the stress-relief range | large, and often needed for even crystallisation || Optical | typically transparent-capable | typically opaque or translucent || Solvent resistance | lower (ABS dissolves in acetone) | usually higher || Layer bonding | good — chains interdiffuse readily near Tg | can be poorer; crystals resist interdiffusion |

PLA is the instructive special case: it is chemically semi-crystalline but crystallises slowly, so a normally-printed PLA part is mostly amorphous. That is exactly why PLA barely warps and why annealing (Chapter 6) can change its properties so dramatically — annealing is what finally lets the crystals form.

| Material | Nozzle (°C, typical) | Bed (°C, typical) | Enclosure | Cooling fan | Notes ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| PLA | 190–220 | 50–60 | not required; a hot enclosure can cause heat creep | high, often 100 % | most forgiving material in existence || PETG | 230–250 | 70–85 | optional | low–moderate (30–50 %) | too much cooling kills layer adhesion || ABS | 235–260 | 90–110 | strongly recommended | off or minimal | warps badly without one || ASA | 240–265 | 90–110 | strongly recommended | off or minimal | ABS-like, formulated for UV || TPU (85A–95A) | 210–240 | 30–60 | not required | low–moderate | slow, direct drive strongly preferred || Nylon / PA12 | 240–270 | 60–100 | recommended | low | extremely hygroscopic || Nylon / PA6 blends | 250–290 | 80–110 | recommended | low–off | higher Tm, worse moisture behaviour || PC / PC blends | 260–310 | 100–120 | required | off | high temperature, high warp || CF / GF composites | base polymer's window, often +5–15 | as base polymer | as base polymer | as base polymer | hardened nozzle mandatory |

Character

| Material | Tg (typical range) | Shrinkage / warp | Stiff vs tough | UV | Chemical | Contact caveats ||---|---|---|---|---|---|---|| PLA | 55–65 °C | very low | stiff, low impact, notch-sensitive | poor long-term outdoors | poor vs solvents; hydrolyses when hot+wet | often food-safe as a polymer; the printed part is not — see below || PETG | 75–85 °C | low–moderate | moderately stiff, noticeably tougher than PLA | fair | good vs water and dilute acids | same caveat; PET family is common in food packaging || ABS | 100–110 °C | high | moderate stiffness, good impact | poor — yellows and embrittles | soluble in acetone (feature for smoothing) | not for food contact; styrene emissions || ASA | 100–110 °C | high | similar to ABS | good — the reason ASA exists | similar to ABS | outdoor parts || TPU | varies widely with hardness | low | flexible; energy-absorbing, high elongation | fair | good vs oils/greases (grade-dependent) | skin contact grades exist; verify per grade || Nylon / PA | grade-dependent, and drops sharply when wet | high | tough, ductile, abrasion-resistant | fair | excellent vs oils/fuels | absorbs water in service, not just on the spool || PC | 145–150 °C | high | very stiff and strong, good impact | poor unless UV-stabilised | attacked by some solvents; stress-cracks | high service temperature || CF composite | base polymer's Tg | lower than base — fibre restrains contraction | much stiffer, less ductile, more brittle | as base | as base | abrasive; fibres at broken edges || GF composite | base polymer's Tg | lower than base | stiffer than base, less brittle than CF | as base | as base | abrasive |

> Watch out — "food safe" is a system property, not a material property. Even where the base polymer is approved for food contact, a printed part is not: the layer lines are a colonisable surface that cannot be reliably cleaned, the brass in a standard nozzle may contain lead, and the pigments and processing aids in a hobby spool are rarely documented to food-contact standards. If a part must contact food or skin, the defensible routes are a documented food-contact-certified filament plus a food-safe coating or liner, or printing a mould and casting the contact part in a certified material. "PLA is made from corn" is not an argument.

> Trick: TPU is sold by Shore hardness, not by a trade name — the scale is what to shop by. Shore 85A is soft and gasket-like and is genuinely hard to print; 95A is the common general-purpose grade; 98A prints almost like a stiff plastic; Shore 60D is harder still and behaves close to a rigid filament. If a TPU print fails on retraction and stringing, the fix is usually a lower Shore number needing a shorter, more constrained filament path — or moving up in hardness.

Chapter 3 — Warping, shrinkage and the enclosure question

Warping is not a mystery and it is not a bed-adhesion problem in origin — bed adhesion is only where the symptom appears.

The physics

Every polymer contracts as it cools. In a printed part, that contraction happens unevenly in time: the layer being deposited is at nozzle temperature, the layer below is somewhere between, and the first layer has been sitting on a heated plate for an hour. Each new layer solidifies and then tries to contract while bonded to a layer beneath that has already finished contracting and will not move. The result is a stress field locked into the part, tensile in the newer material, and it grows with every layer.

That locked-in stress has to go somewhere. It is relieved by:

- Curling — the free edges of the part lift, which is geometrically the cheapest way to relieve it. Corners lift first because they have two free directions.- Delamination — a layer interface splits, usually on a tall thin part or at a stress raiser.- Bed release — the whole part peels off the plate.

Two independent quantities drive the magnitude: the total contraction from nozzle to room temperature (roughly the coefficient of thermal expansion times the temperature drop, plus any crystallisation shrinkage) and the temperature gradient through the part while printing.

Why ABS needs an enclosure and PLA does not

Run the comparison:

- PLA is printed around 200 °C and, being effectively amorphous as printed, contracts modestly. Its Tg is around 55–65 °C, so the part becomes rigid at a temperature only a little above the bed, meaning the stress-accumulating window is short and the accumulated stress is small. An enclosure adds nothing and can hurt: it raises the temperature around the heatsink and hotend, promoting heat creep and jams.- ABS is printed around 250 °C, has a Tg around 100–110 °C, and contracts substantially. Its Tg is far above room temperature, so the part spends a long stretch of its cooling path below Tg — rigid, storing stress rather than relaxing it. An enclosure holds the chamber high enough that the part stays near or above Tg — mobile, able to relax stress — for longer, and it removes the gradients caused by room draughts. That is the mechanism: the enclosure is a stress-relief oven, not a heater for the plastic.

Consequences that follow directly:

| Symptom | Mechanism | Fix in order of leverage ||---|---|---|| Corners lift on a large flat ABS part | high contraction plus draught-driven gradients | enclosure; raise chamber temperature; brim; reduce part footprint or split the part || Part releases mid-print, PLA | adhesion, not warp | clean the plate (IPA, then soap and water for oils); first-layer squish (Post 01) || Tall PC part delaminates near the top | chamber cools with height; interface bonding falls | enclosure with an actively warm chamber; raise nozzle temperature; reduce cooling || Nylon curls despite an enclosure | crystallisation shrinkage plus moisture | dry the filament (Chapter 4) before blaming warp || Fibre-filled version of the same polymer warps less | fibres restrain matrix contraction | expected — this is a genuine benefit of CF/GF grades |

> Common pitfall: adding a brim or raft to fight ABS warp. It buys a little and hides the cause. The stress is still in the part; you have merely bonded it harder to the plate, which converts corner lift into internal stress or a delamination crack higher up. Fix the thermal environment.

> Trick: you can see the locked-in stress. Print a thin ABS bar without an enclosure, let it cool fully, then cut it lengthwise with a sharp blade. The two halves spring apart. A bar printed in a warm enclosure moves much less. This is a five-minute experiment that makes the whole chapter concrete.

Chapter 4 — Moisture, the invisible variable

Which polymers care

Hygroscopic means the polymer absorbs water from air into its bulk, not just onto its surface. In rough order of severity for printing filaments:

| Severity | Materials | Practical consequence ||---|---|---|| Extreme | PVA, PA (nylon), PC | unprintable within hours to days of open-air exposure in a humid room || High | TPU, PETG, ASA | visible surface and strength degradation over days to weeks || Moderate | ABS, PLA | degrades over weeks to months; PLA additionally hydrolyses when hot and wet, permanently shortening chains || Low | dried, sealed, unopened spools of anything | the state you want to maintain |

The hydrolysis point matters and is often missed: for PLA and the polyesters, water is not just a bubble source. At extrusion temperature, absorbed water chemically cleaves the polymer chains. Drying removes the water but does not undo the chain scission. Wet-then-dried PLA prints acceptably and is measurably weaker than it should be.

What wet filament looks and sounds like

| Sense | Signature ||---|---|| Sound | intermittent popping, crackling, hissing at the nozzle — steam escaping || Extrusion | visible steam or micro-bubbles; the strand from a hand-purge is foamy or wavy rather than smooth || Surface | rough, matte, pitted; PETG turns hazy and blistered rather than glossy || Stringing | dramatically worse, and unresponsive to retraction tuning || Dimensions | inconsistent extrusion width; unpredictable flow calibration (this contaminates the Post 05 measurements) || Strength | reduced, often severely, especially interlayer || Cross-section | cut a purged strand: voids and bubbles inside the bead |

> Common pitfall: tuning retraction for hours to chase stringing that is caused by moisture. Before any retraction tuning session, do the hand-purge test: extrude 100 mm by hand and look and listen. If it pops, dry the spool first and start the tuning session over.

Drying

Drying is a diffusion process: temperature sets the rate, time lets the water get out of the core of the spool. Getting the temperature wrong in the other direction fuses the spool.

Published starting points — again, the spool's own datasheet wins, especially for filled and specialty grades:

| Material | Typical drying temperature | Typical time | Note ||---|---|---|---|| PLA | 40–50 °C | 4–8 h | stay well below Tg or the spool welds to itself || PETG | 60–70 °C | 4–8 h | || ABS / ASA | 65–80 °C | 4–6 h | || TPU | 45–55 °C | 4–8 h | soft; low temperature, longer time || PVA | 45–55 °C | 6–12 h | re-wets almost immediately after || Nylon / PA | 70–90 °C | 8–12 h+ | manufacturers commonly specify the upper end || PC | 80–100 °C | 6–12 h | |

The universal rule that makes those numbers safe: dry below the material's Tg, with margin, or the spool deforms and the windings fuse. That is why nylon tolerates 80–90 °C and PLA does not — nylon's amorphous fraction is not the load-bearing part of a spool wound from a semi-crystalline polymer, while PLA at 70 °C is above Tg and structurally soft.

Equipment, ranked by how well it works:

1. Purpose-built filament dryer — controlled temperature, adequate airflow, can print directly from the box. The right answer if you print hygroscopic materials at all.2. Food dehydrator — cheap, good airflow, often needs the shelves cut out to fit a spool. Verify the actual temperature with a separate thermometer; dial markings on cheap units are frequently wrong by 10 °C or more.3. Convection oven — workable if it can hold a low temperature accurately. Many domestic ovens cannot regulate below 70–80 °C and cycle far above the setpoint. Verify with a thermometer before trusting it with a spool.4. The printer's heated bed with a box over it — a last resort, poorly controlled.

Silica gel in a sealed box maintains dryness; it does not dry a wet spool in any reasonable time. Dry first, then store.

Storage that actually works

- Sealed container (vacuum bag or gasketed box) with indicating desiccant, plus a cheap hygrometer inside so you can see whether the system works. Target the lowest relative humidity you can maintain and hold; single digits is achievable in a good sealed box with fresh desiccant.- Regenerate desiccant on a schedule (bake per its instructions) — spent desiccant looks identical to fresh unless it is the indicating kind.- Dry boxes that feed the printer directly, for nylon, PC, PVA and TPU. For these materials, "dry it before printing" is not enough; a 20-hour print will re-absorb moisture from the room.- Sealed bags on unopened spools are only as good as the desiccant packed with them. A spool that has sat in a warehouse for two years is not automatically dry.

Chapter 5 — Abrasives, multi-material adhesion, and soluble supports

Abrasive filaments and nozzle wear

Carbon fibre, glass fibre, metal-filled, wood-filled, glow-in-the-dark (glass or strontium aluminate particles) and many "sparkle" or "silk-matte" filaments contain hard particles. A standard brass nozzle is soft; those particles cut it. The failure is insidious: the orifice grows and goes out-of-round gradually, so extrusion width drifts, first layers degrade, and dimensional accuracy quietly gets worse over weeks — exactly the sort of slow drift that invalidates the calibration you did in Post 01.

| Nozzle material | Abrasion resistance | Thermal conductivity | Notes ||---|---|---|---|| Brass | poor | excellent | fine for PLA/PETG/ABS; wears fast on any filled filament || Hardened steel | good | lower — may need +5–10 °C | the common budget answer || Steel with wear coating | good | lower | varies enormously by product || Tungsten carbide / ruby-tipped | excellent | varies by construction | expensive; the long-run answer for a fibre-heavy workflow |

> Trick: keep one nozzle dedicated to abrasives and one to everything else, and label them. Swapping a nozzle is a ten-minute job; re-running the whole flow and dimension calibration because a nozzle silently opened up is not.

Glow-in-the-dark filament deserves a specific warning: it is often more abrasive than carbon fibre, and it is frequently bought by beginners who have no idea it is abrasive at all.

Adhesion between materials

Multi-material printing (two extruders, a tool-changer, or a single-nozzle multi-material unit) only works if the two polymers bond at their interface, and polymer-to-polymer bonding is chemistry, not wishing.

| Pair | Bonding | Use ||---|---|---|| PLA + PLA (different colour) | perfect | colour changes || PLA + PETG | poor — bonds weakly | exploited deliberately: PETG makes a good breakaway support interface for PLA, and vice versa || PETG + PETG | perfect | || ABS + ABS / ASA | perfect | || TPU + PLA/PETG | moderate; mechanical interlocking helps more than chemistry | overmoulded grips — design a dovetail or through-hole so the bond is mechanical || PLA + PVA | good | soluble supports for PLA || ABS/HIPS | good | HIPS is the classic soluble support for ABS || Nylon + almost anything else | poor | nylon bonds well to nylon and little else |

> Trick: the poor PLA/PETG bond is the single most useful multi-material trick available. Print a support interface layer in the other material and supports peel off by hand with a genuinely clean surface underneath — no solvent, no dissolving tank.

Soluble supports and their own moisture problems

- PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) — dissolves in plain water. Compatible with PLA and (with care) PETG. It is the most hygroscopic common filament by a wide margin: it will absorb enough moisture to become unprintable within hours in a humid room, and wet PVA foams, oozes, jams and clogs. PVA requires a dry box feeding the printer, not merely a dried spool. It also degrades in the hotend if held hot and idle.- HIPS (high-impact polystyrene) — dissolves in d-limonene, not water. Compatible with ABS, and prints under ABS-like conditions. The solvent is flammable, has a strong odour, and needs ventilation and a sealed dissolving container.- Breakaway supports — proprietary non-soluble support filaments designed to bond weakly and snap off. No solvent, no tank, no moisture management; they cost geometry flexibility versus a truly soluble support.

Practical decision: soluble supports are worth their cost and hassle when the geometry is genuinely internal — enclosed channels, captive mechanisms, deep undercuts. For everything else, revisit orientation (Post 03) first. Support material is a solution to a geometry problem you may not need to have.

Chapter 6 — Properties you set after the print: annealing, post-cure, pigment

Annealing

Annealing is holding a printed part at an elevated temperature — above Tg but below the melting/softening point — for a period, then cooling it slowly. For a semi-crystalline polymer printed too fast to crystallise (PLA is the archetype), it does three things at once:

1. Increases crystallinity. Chains gain mobility above Tg and organise into crystalline domains. Stiffness, heat resistance and chemical resistance rise. Annealed PLA can retain useful stiffness meaningfully above its as-printed service ceiling — how much depends on the grade, the additives (nucleating agents in "HT" PLA grades are there specifically to make this work) and the schedule.2. Relieves internal stress. The locked-in stress from Chapter 3 relaxes. Good for stability, and it is why the part changes shape.3. Improves interlayer bonding somewhat. Chains interdiffuse across layer interfaces given mobility and time, reducing the Z-direction weakness of Post 07 — reducing, not eliminating.

The cost is dimensional change, and it is not small. Crystallisation densifies the polymer, so the part shrinks — and it shrinks anisotropically, because the print's own layer structure and residual stress field are anisotropic. Parts also sag under their own weight if unsupported above Tg.

Practical annealing procedure, if you decide the part needs it:

1. Decide first whether it does. Annealing is worth it for heat-exposed and stiffness-critical PLA parts. It is not worth it for anything that has to fit something else, unless you are prepared to re-measure and iterate.2. Support the part fully — bury it in dry sand, salt or plaster, or lay it on a flat plate with nothing overhanging.3. Hold above Tg but below softening: a common starting window for PLA is roughly 80–110 °C. Longer at a lower temperature is safer than shorter at a higher one.4. Cool slowly in the oven. Quenching re-introduces the stress you just removed.5. Measure the same features before and after and record the change as a percentage. That percentage is your machine-plus-material-plus-schedule constant, and you scale the CAD model by it next time. It is not a number anyone can give you — Post 05 gives the measurement discipline.

> Watch out: annealing an amorphous polymer (ABS, PETG, PC) does not increase crystallinity, because there is no crystalline phase to grow. It still relieves stress, which can be useful, and it still causes dimensional change. Do not expect the stiffness gain that PLA shows.

Resin families and post-cure

Vat photopolymerisation (Post 08 in the extension plan will go deep) has a materials landscape of its own. The critical structural fact: for photopolymer resin, a large share of the part's final mechanical properties is set by post-processing, not by printing. A green part straight out of the vat is under-polymerised, soft, and full of uncured monomer. Wash and post-cure are part of the process, not optional finishing.

| Family | Character | Typical use ||---|---|---|| Standard / "general purpose" | high detail, hard, brittle | display models, miniatures, figures || Tough / "ABS-like" | more impact energy absorbed, lower detail fidelity, softer | prototype functional parts || Water-washable | cleans in water instead of IPA | convenience — but generally lower mechanical properties, and the wash water is contaminated waste, not drain-safe || Engineering (high-temp, rigid, castable, flexible, dental) | narrow, specified properties | the specified job, per the vendor's validated workflow |

Post-cure variables that change the result: cure time, cure temperature (warm post-cure typically outperforms room-temperature cure), wavelength, whether the part is cured in water or in air (oxygen inhibits surface cure — curing submerged is a common trick), and part thickness. Vendors publish validated schedules; deviating from them makes their published mechanical data inapplicable to your part.

> Common pitfall: treating resin mechanical datasheets as if they described your part. Those numbers come from specimens cured on the vendor's validated schedule. An under-cured part is dramatically weaker; an over-cured part is more brittle. Post-cure is the variable, and it is yours to control.

Colour, pigment and additives

The single most under-appreciated fact about filament: colour is not cosmetic. The pigments, fillers and processing aids change the polymer's behaviour.

- Thermal behaviour. Pigment loading changes melt viscosity and thermal conductivity. It is normal for two colours from the same vendor and the same product line to want different nozzle temperatures — sometimes by 10 °C or more.- Crystallisation. Some pigments act as nucleating agents. This changes crystallisation rate, which changes shrinkage and warping. Black and white parts of the same nominal material genuinely do not always behave the same.- Abrasion. Metallics, "silk", glitter and glow additives are abrasive. See Chapter 5.- Strength. High filler loading dilutes the polymer matrix. Heavily filled decorative filaments are usually weaker than the plain version of the same material.- Surface finish. Matte finishes come from filler particles that scatter light; they also hide layer lines, which is genuinely useful and is a materials effect, not a slicer effect.- UV behaviour. Carbon black is an effective UV absorber; black parts often outlast light-coloured ones of the same polymer outdoors. This is not a reason to skip ASA for an outdoor part, but it is a real effect.

> Trick: when you switch colour within the same product line, re-run a temperature tower and a flow check before assuming your profile transfers. It costs twenty minutes and explains a surprising number of "the printer suddenly got worse" reports.

Chapter 7 — Choosing a material, and printing it safely

The decision tree

Start from the requirement, not the material. Read down until a branch matches.

Two meta-rules that outrank the tree:

1. Prototype in PLA regardless of the final material. Iterate the geometry cheaply and fast, then reprint the final version in the material the job needs. Changing material changes dimensions, so re-verify the fit (Post 05) — but do the geometry iteration in the easy material.2. Prefer the easiest material that meets the requirement. Every step up in material difficulty costs enclosure, drying, nozzle hardware, tuning time and failed prints. Only pay that when the requirement demands it.

Safety

Printing polymers releases ultrafine particles (UFPs) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs). This is measured, published science, not speculation. Peer-reviewed work characterising desktop FDM printers (Azimi et al., Environmental Science & Technology, 2016) found that emission rates and species depend strongly on filament and nozzle temperature: styrene dominates for ABS and HIPS, caprolactam for nylon-based and composite filaments, and lactide for PLA — with ABS emitting substantially more overall than PLA, and lower nozzle temperatures producing lower emission rates. ANSI/CAN/UL 2904 is the standard test method for characterising these emissions, and some printers and filaments are certified against it.

The proportionate response:

| Situation | Measure ||---|---|| Any printer, any material | do not print in a bedroom or an unventilated occupied room || PLA/PETG in a well-ventilated workshop | adequate room ventilation is a reasonable baseline || ABS/ASA/nylon/PC, or any long print | enclosure with a filter (HEPA for particles plus activated carbon for VOCs — a HEPA filter alone does nothing for VOCs), or vent the enclosure outdoors || Printing near where people sleep or work all day | vent outdoors, or move the printer || Any material, any enclosure | run the fan, and note that an enclosure without filtration concentrates emissions and then releases them when opened |

Resin adds a different and more direct hazard class:

- Uncured resin is a skin sensitiser. Nitrile gloves, always. Sensitisation is cumulative and can be permanent: people who handled resin carelessly for a year and then developed a reaction do not get to go back.- Eye protection when handling resin or IPA.- Ventilation — resin and IPA vapour both need it.- Never pour uncured resin, resin-contaminated IPA, or water-washable rinse water down a drain. Cure the waste solid under UV or sunlight first, then dispose of it as solid plastic waste, per local rules.- Cure everything, including failed prints, supports, drips and the paper towels. Cured resin is inert; uncured resin is not.

Also mechanical and thermal, and easy to forget: hotends run at 200–300 °C, beds at up to 120 °C, and a printer with an unattended thermal runaway is a fire source. Verify that thermal runaway protection is enabled in firmware, keep the machine off carpet and away from anything flammable, and treat a smoke alarm in the print room as mandatory equipment rather than an optional extra.
