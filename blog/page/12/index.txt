Chapter 1 — Why postprocess

The raw render is "physically correct" but rarely cinematic. Postprocess passes simulate camera and human-vision effects:

- Bloom — bright pixels bleed into surrounding area; mimics camera lens glare.- Depth of field (DOF) — pixels at the focus distance are sharp; others blurred.- Screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) — darken creases the global lighting missed.- Outline — highlight selected objects with a coloured edge.- God rays / volumetric light — visible shafts of light through atmosphere.- Motion blur — fast objects smear.- Anti-aliasing — smooth jagged edges (geometry AA in WebGL is limited).- Tone mapping — squash HDR values into the displayable 0-1 range.- Colour grading — LUT / curve adjustments for a chosen mood.

Each is a full-screen pass. Each costs ms. Use sparingly on mobile (Post 08).

Chapter 1 — Pixel ratio (the biggest free win)

A modern phone may report  — meaning a 400×800 logical viewport renders at 1200×2400 actual pixels. That's 9× the pixels of a desktop running the same canvas at DPR=1.

Capping at 2 is the standard. The visual difference between DPR=2 and DPR=3 is essentially invisible; the performance difference is dramatic.

For aggressive optimisation, cap at 1.5 or 1 on detected low-end devices. Combine with a quality slider in your UI.

Chapter 1 — The draw-call budget

Every  in the scene typically produces one draw call. Each draw call has CPU overhead: bind shader, set uniforms, set attribute pointers, issue the GPU command. Modern GPUs draw millions of triangles per frame easily; CPUs choke on thousands of draw calls per frame.

Rough targets for 60 fps:

| Device | Safe draw-call count ||--------|----------------------|| Desktop / high-end laptop | ≤ 1,000 || Mid-range mobile (mid-2020s) | ≤ 100-200 || Low-end mobile / older hardware | ≤ 50-100 |

Triangle counts matter much less than draw-call counts. 1 mesh with 500k triangles renders fine; 5,000 meshes with 100 triangles each will not.

If  ≫ 200 and the scene is laggy, the problem is the draw-call count, not GPU rasterisation. The fix is one of the patterns in this post.

When BatchedMesh underperforms

The current Three.js BatchedMesh emulates instancing via  rather than . For workloads of "many copies of the same geometry", InstancedMesh is faster. For "many different geometries, same material", BatchedMesh wins.

Chapter 4 — Geometry merging (one big mesh)

For static scenery where you don't need per-object updates, merge geometries into a single big mesh:

1 draw call, fully baked. No per-object animation possible — to move one cube, you'd modify the whole merged geometry. Use for static decoration (walls, ground details, fixed environment).

Multi-material merge

Groups define which sub-range uses which material — still one geometry but multiple draw calls (one per group). Useful for merging objects that genuinely need different materials while keeping vertex data shared.

Chapter 7 — Frustum culling + occlusion culling

Three.js culls per-mesh: meshes whose bounding sphere is outside the camera frustum are skipped.

For  and , frustum culling tests the entire batch's bounding sphere — if any part is in view, the whole batch renders. To get per-instance culling, you have two choices:

1. Manual chunking — split a 10k-instance forest into 100 chunks of 100 instances. Each chunk has its own InstancedMesh with a tighter bounding sphere.2. GPU culling with  — newer Three.js supports per-instance visibility for BatchedMesh.

For occlusion culling (skipping objects hidden by walls), Three.js doesn't ship native support. Libraries like three-mesh-bvh can accelerate visibility queries; production engines often use a custom hierarchical-Z buffer approach.

13:T8500,Most vectors change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special directions do not: the matrix only stretches, shrinks or flips them. Those directions are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues. If a matrix has enough eigenvectors to form a basis, then in that basis it is diagonal — every hard question about the matrix (its powers, its long-run behaviour, the differential equation it drives) splits into $n$ independent one-dimensional questions. This post builds the theory with proofs, then uses it on Fibonacci numbers, a Markov chain, a system of differential equations, and the power-iteration algorithm. It relies on the determinant and on change of basis from Linear Algebra 03.

> Series map: 00 Vectors & Linear Systems → 01 Matrix Algebra, Inverses & LU → 02 Vector Spaces, Basis & Dimension → 03 Linear Maps, Change of Basis & Determinants → 04 Eigenvalues & Diagonalization (this post) → 05 Inner Products, Spectral Theorem & SVD.

Learning Outcome

- Define eigenvalues, eigenvectors and eigenspaces, and describe them geometrically for common maps of the plane.- Find eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial and eigenvectors from null spaces, for $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ matrices.- Distinguish algebraic from geometric multiplicity, and prove that eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent.- Decide whether a matrix is diagonalizable, and write $A = PDP^{-1}$ when it is.- Compute $A^k$ and solve difference equations (Fibonacci, Markov chains) by diagonalization.- Handle complex eigenvalues, and use trace = sum and determinant = product of the eigenvalues as checks.- State and use the Cayley–Hamilton theorem; recognise a defective matrix and state the Jordan form.- Solve $x' = Ax$ with $e^{At} = Pe^{Dt}P^{-1}$, and explain how power iteration finds the dominant eigenvalue.

Chapter 1 — Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Terminology

- Eigenvector of a square matrix $A$ (or a linear operator $T: V \to V$) — a nonzero vector $v$ with $Av = \lambda v$ for some scalar $\lambda$.- Eigenvalue — a scalar $\lambda$ for which such a nonzero $v$ exists. "Eigen" is German for "own" or "characteristic".- Eigenspace $E\lambda = \{v : Av = \lambda v\} = N(A - \lambda I)$ — all eigenvectors for $\lambda$, together with $0$. It is a subspace.- Spectrum — the set of all eigenvalues of $A$.- Diagonalizable — similar to a diagonal matrix: $A = PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal and $P$ invertible.

The zero vector is excluded as an eigenvector because $A0 = \lambda 0$ holds for every $\lambda$ and would say nothing. The eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$, on the other hand, is allowed: $Av = 0v$ with $v \neq 0$ means $v$ is in the null space, so $0$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $A$ is singular.

What it means geometrically

$Av = \lambda v$ says: the line through $v$ is mapped into itself. Along that line, $A$ acts as multiplication by the number $\lambda$. If $\lambda > 1$ the line is stretched, $0 < \lambda < 1$ shrinks it, $\lambda < 0$ flips it, $\lambda = 0$ collapses it to the origin.

The maps of the plane from Linear Algebra 03:

| Map | Eigenvalues and eigen-directions ||-----|----------------------------------|| Projection onto a line $L$ | $1$ on $L$ (kept), $0$ on the perpendicular line (killed) || Reflection in a line $L$ | $1$ on $L$, $-1$ on the perpendicular line || Rotation by $\theta$, $0 < \theta < 180°$ | no real eigenvector — every direction turns; complex eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$ (Chapter 6) || Shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $k \neq 0$ | only $1$, only along the $x$-axis — not enough eigenvectors (Chapter 4) || Scaling $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$ | $a$ along $e1$, $b$ along $e2$ |

Running example. Throughout the post,

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Check two directions: $A(1, 1) = (5, 5) = 5(1, 1)$ and $A(1, -2) = (2, -4) = 2(1, -2)$. So $(1,1)$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 5 and $(1, -2)$ is one with eigenvalue 2. A vector off these lines turns: $e1 = (1,0)$ goes to $(4, 2)$, a turn of about $26.6°$ counter-clockwise, and $e2 = (0,1)$ goes to $(1, 3)$, a turn of about $18.4°$ clockwise.

![The map A = [[4,1],[2,3]] applied to vectors of the unit circle: the eigen-directions (1,1) and (1,-2) stay on their own lines and are stretched by 5 and 2, while e1 and e2 are turned; the unit circle becomes an ellipse.](/assets/linalg/diagram04eigendirections.svg)

Chapter 2 — The characteristic polynomial

From a nonlinear problem to a determinant

$Av = \lambda v$ has two unknowns multiplied together ($\lambda$ and $v$), so it is not a linear system. Rewrite it as

$$(A - \lambda I)v = 0, \qquad v \neq 0.$$

A nonzero solution exists exactly when $A - \lambda I$ is singular, which by Linear Algebra 03 happens exactly when its determinant is zero. So:

Theorem 1. $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$.

The function $pA(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ is the characteristic polynomial of $A$. Expanding the determinant with the Leibniz formula shows it is a polynomial of degree $n$ with leading coefficient 1. (Some books use $\det(A - \lambda I) = (-1)^n pA(\lambda)$; the roots are the same.)

The recipe is therefore two steps: (1) find the roots of $pA$; (2) for each root $\lambda$, find the null space of $A - \lambda I$ by elimination.

The shortcut for 2 × 2 matrices

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$,

$$pA(\lambda) = (\lambda - a)(\lambda - d) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\,\lambda + \det A.$$

Worked example. For the running example, $\operatorname{tr}A = 7$ and $\det A = 12 - 2 = 10$:

$$pA(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2).$$

For $\lambda = 5$: $A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2\end{pmatrix}$, whose null space is spanned by $(1, 1)$. For $\lambda = 2$: $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, null space spanned by $(1, -2)$. This matches Chapter 1.

> Trick: After finding a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A - \lambda I$, you do not need to row-reduce. If the first row is $(p, q)$, then $(q, -p)$ (or $(-q, p)$) is in the null space. The second row must be a multiple of the first — if it is not, $\lambda$ was computed wrongly.

A 3 × 3 example with a repeated eigenvalue

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad pB(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 9\lambda - 4 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1)^2.$$

(A quick way to see this: $B = I + J$ where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. $J$ has rank 1, so $0$ is an eigenvalue of $J$ with a two-dimensional eigenspace, and $J(1,1,1) = 3(1,1,1)$. Adding $I$ adds 1 to every eigenvalue.)

- $\lambda = 4$: $B - 4I$ has null space spanned by $(1, 1, 1)$.- $\lambda = 1$: $B - I = J$, every row is $(1, 1, 1)$, so the null space is the plane $x + y + z = 0$, spanned by $(-1, 1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, 1)$.

The eigenvalue 1 is a double root and has a two-dimensional eigenspace. That is the good case, as Chapter 3 explains.

Easy cases and invariance

- Triangular matrices: $\lambda I - A$ is triangular, so $pA(\lambda) = \prod (\lambda - a{ii})$. The eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.- Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial. If $B = P^{-1}AP$ then $\lambda I - B = P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P$, so by the product rule $\det(\lambda I - B) = \det(\lambda I - A)$. So eigenvalues belong to the operator, not to a particular matrix of it (Linear Algebra 03, similarity invariants).- $A$ and $A^T$ have the same characteristic polynomial (because $\det M^T = \det M$), but usually different eigenvectors.

> Common pitfall: Row operations change eigenvalues. Row-reducing $A$ first and then reading off the diagonal gives the pivots, not the eigenvalues. Row-reduce $A - \lambda I$ (to find eigenvectors), never $A$.

Trace and determinant from the eigenvalues

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every polynomial factors completely (fundamental theorem of algebra), so $pA(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda1)\cdots(\lambda - \lambdan)$, with eigenvalues repeated by multiplicity.

Theorem 2. $\operatorname{tr} A = \lambda1 + \dots + \lambdan$ and $\det A = \lambda1 \lambda2 \cdots \lambdan$.

Proof. Put $\lambda = 0$ in $\det(\lambda I - A) = \prod(\lambda - \lambdai)$: $\det(-A) = \prod(-\lambdai)$, that is $(-1)^n \det A = (-1)^n \prod \lambdai$. For the trace, compare the coefficients of $\lambda^{n-1}$. On the right it is $-\sum \lambdai$. On the left, in the Leibniz expansion of $\det(\lambda I - A)$, any term other than the diagonal product uses at most $n - 2$ diagonal entries, so has degree at most $n - 2$ in $\lambda$. The diagonal product $\prod(\lambda - a{ii})$ contributes $-\sum a{ii} = -\operatorname{tr}A$. ∎

Checks: for $A$, $5 + 2 = 7 = \operatorname{tr}A$ and $5 \cdot 2 = 10 = \det A$. For $B$, $4 + 1 + 1 = 6$ and $4 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 4 = \det B$.

Chapter 3 — Multiplicities and independence

Two kinds of multiplicity

For an eigenvalue $\lambda0$:

- Algebraic multiplicity — how many times $\lambda0$ is a root of $pA$ (the power of $(\lambda - \lambda0)$ in the factorisation).- Geometric multiplicity — $\dim E{\lambda0} = \dim N(A - \lambda0 I)$, the number of independent eigenvectors for $\lambda0$.

Theorem 3. $1 \le \text{geometric multiplicity} \le \text{algebraic multiplicity}$.

Proof. The lower bound holds because an eigenvalue has at least one eigenvector. For the upper bound let $g = \dim E{\lambda0}$ with basis $v1, \dots, vg$, and extend it to a basis $v1, \dots, vn$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. With $P = (v1 \cdots vn)$, the first $g$ columns of $P^{-1}AP$ are $\lambda0 e1, \dots, \lambda0 eg$, so

$$P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda0 Ig & X \\ 0 & Y \end{pmatrix}.$$

Similar matrices share the characteristic polynomial, and the block triangular determinant rule gives $pA(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda0)^g \det(\lambda I - Y)$. So $(\lambda - \lambda0)^g$ divides $pA$, and the algebraic multiplicity is at least $g$. ∎

In the example $B$, the eigenvalue 1 has algebraic multiplicity 2 and geometric multiplicity 2. For the shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalue 1 has algebraic multiplicity 2 but geometric multiplicity 1. That gap is exactly what stops a matrix from being diagonalizable.

Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent

Theorem 4. If $v1, \dots, vk$ are eigenvectors of $A$ for distinct eigenvalues $\lambda1, \dots, \lambdak$, then $v1, \dots, vk$ are linearly independent.

Proof. Suppose not. Let $m$ be the smallest index such that $vm$ is a combination of $v1, \dots, v{m-1}$; then $v1, \dots, v{m-1}$ are independent, and $m \ge 2$ since $v1 \neq 0$. Write

$$vm = c1 v1 + \dots + c{m-1} v{m-1}.$$

Apply $A$: $\lambdam vm = c1 \lambda1 v1 + \dots + c{m-1}\lambda{m-1} v{m-1}$. Subtract $\lambdam$ times the first equation:

$$0 = c1(\lambda1 - \lambdam) v1 + \dots + c{m-1}(\lambda{m-1} - \lambdam) v{m-1}.$$

By independence each $ci(\lambdai - \lambdam) = 0$, and since the eigenvalues are distinct, each $ci = 0$. Then $vm = 0$, which is impossible for an eigenvector. ∎

The same argument shows more: if you take a basis of each eigenspace and put them together, the combined list is still independent. So the sum of the geometric multiplicities is the number of independent eigenvectors the matrix has in total.

Corollary 5. An $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. (Chapter 4 explains why independence is all that is needed.)

The converse is false: $I$ has only one eigenvalue and is already diagonal; so is $B$ after a change of basis.

Chapter 4 — Diagonalization

The main theorem

Theorem 6. An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In that case $A = PDP^{-1}$, where the columns of $P$ are the eigenvectors and $D$ is the diagonal matrix of the matching eigenvalues, in the same order.

Proof. Write $P = (v1 \cdots vn)$ and $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda1, \dots, \lambdan)$. Column $i$ of $AP$ is $Avi$ and column $i$ of $PD$ is $\lambdai vi$. So

$$AP = PD \iff Avi = \lambdai vi \text{ for every } i.$$

If there are $n$ independent eigenvectors, $P$ is invertible and $A = PDP^{-1}$. Conversely, if $A = PDP^{-1}$ then $AP = PD$, so the columns of $P$ are eigenvectors, and they are independent because $P$ is invertible. ∎

In the language of Linear Algebra 03: $D$ is the matrix of the same operator in the eigenvector basis. Diagonalizing means finding coordinates in which the map acts on each coordinate separately.

Criterion. Combining Theorems 3 and 4: $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$ if and only if, for every eigenvalue, geometric multiplicity = algebraic multiplicity. It is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ if in addition all eigenvalues are real.

Worked example. For the running example,

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A = PDP^{-1}.$$

For $B$, take $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$; then $P^{-1}BP = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1, 1)$.

> Watch out: The order of the columns of $P$ must match the order of the eigenvalues in $D$. Eigenvectors can be scaled freely (any nonzero multiple is still an eigenvector), so $P$ is never unique — but $D$ is unique up to the order of its entries.

A defective matrix and the Jordan form

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad pC(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 4 = (\lambda - 2)^2.$$

$C - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 1, so the eigenspace is only the line spanned by $(1, -1)$. Algebraic multiplicity 2, geometric multiplicity 1: there is no eigenvector basis, and $C$ is not diagonalizable. Such a matrix is called defective.

What is the best you can do? Take the eigenvector $v1 = (1, -1)$ and solve $(C - 2I)w = v1$; one solution is $w = (1, 0)$ (a generalised eigenvector). With $P = (v1\ w) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$,

$$P^{-1}CP = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Theorem 7 (Jordan normal form, statement only). Every square complex matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix whose blocks are Jordan blocks

$$Jk(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 & & \\ & \lambda & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & \lambda \end{pmatrix} \quad (k \times k),$$

and the blocks are unique up to their order. For each eigenvalue, the number of blocks equals its geometric multiplicity and the total size of its blocks equals its algebraic multiplicity. A matrix is diagonalizable exactly when every block is $1 \times 1$.

The proof takes a full chapter (Axler, Chapter 8); it is planned for a later post in this series.

Chapter 5 — Powers and difference equations

Powers

If $A = PDP^{-1}$ then the inner $P^{-1}P$ pairs cancel:

$$A^k = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})\cdots(PDP^{-1}) = PD^kP^{-1}, \qquad D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda1^k, \dots, \lambdan^k).$$

For the running example:

$$A^k = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot 5^k + 2^k & 5^k - 2^k \\ 2 \cdot 5^k - 2 \cdot 2^k & 5^k + 2\cdot 2^k \end{pmatrix}.$$

Check with $k = 1$: $\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9\end{pmatrix} = A$. With $k = 3$: $\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 258 & 117 \\ 234 & 141 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 86 & 39 \\ 78 & 47 \end{pmatrix}$, which is $A^3$ computed directly. For large $k$ the $5^k$ terms dominate, so $A^k \approx \frac{5^k}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$: every starting vector gets pulled towards the direction $(1, 1)$.

Difference equations

A linear difference equation (discrete dynamical system) is $u{k+1} = Auk$, so $uk = A^k u0$. Expand the start in eigenvectors, $u0 = c1 v1 + \dots + cn vn$ (that is, $c = P^{-1}u0$). Each piece evolves on its own:

$$uk = c1 \lambda1^k v1 + c2 \lambda2^k v2 + \dots + cn \lambdan^k vn.$$

So the long-run behaviour is read from the eigenvalues: if every $\lvert\lambdai\rvert < 1$ then $uk \to 0$; if some $\lvert\lambdai\rvert > 1$ (with $ci \neq 0$) the solution blows up; the eigenvalue of largest modulus decides the dominant direction.

Worked example — Fibonacci numbers

$F0 = 0$, $F1 = 1$, $F{k+1} = Fk + F{k-1}$. Put $uk = (F{k+1}, Fk)$. Then

$$u{k+1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} uk, \qquad u0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The matrix $F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ has trace 1 and determinant $-1$, so $pF(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1$ with roots

$$\varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2} \approx 1.618034, \qquad \psi = \frac{1 - \sqrt 5}{2} \approx -0.618034.$$

For either root, $v = (\lambda, 1)$ is an eigenvector: $F(\lambda, 1) = (\lambda + 1, \lambda) = (\lambda^2, \lambda)$ because $\lambda^2 = \lambda + 1$. Since $\varphi - \psi = \sqrt5$, the start splits as $u0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt 5}\big((\varphi, 1) - (\psi, 1)\big)$. Hence $uk = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\big(\varphi^k(\varphi, 1) - \psi^k(\psi, 1)\big)$, and the second component is Binet's formula:

$$Fk = \frac{\varphi^k - \psi^k}{\sqrt 5}.$$

Because $\lvert\psi\rvert < 1$, the $\psi^k$ term dies away: $Fk$ is the integer nearest to $\varphi^k/\sqrt5$. For $k = 20$, $\varphi^{20}/\sqrt5 \approx 6765.00003$, and $F{20} = 6765$. The ratio $F{k+1}/Fk \to \varphi$. As a check on the matrix form, $F^{10} = \begin{pmatrix} 89 & 55 \\ 55 & 34 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} F{11} & F{10} \\ F{10} & F9 \end{pmatrix}$.

Worked example — a Markov chain

A town's weather is either sunny or rainy. If today is sunny, tomorrow is sunny with probability 0.9; if today is rainy, tomorrow is sunny with probability 0.5. With $xk = (\Pr[\text{sunny on day } k], \Pr[\text{rainy on day } k])$,

$$x{k+1} = Mxk, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.5 \\ 0.1 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Each column of $M$ sums to 1 (a column-stochastic or Markov matrix). Trace $1.4$, determinant $0.45 - 0.05 = 0.4$, so $pM(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 1.4\lambda + 0.4 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 0.4)$.

- $\lambda = 1$: eigenvector $(5, 1)$. Scaled so the entries sum to 1: the steady state $s = (5/6, 1/6)$.- $\lambda = 0.4$: eigenvector $(-1, 1)$.

Start on a rainy day, $x0 = (0, 1) = \frac{1}{6}(5, 1) + \frac{5}{6}(-1, 1)$. Then

$$xk = \begin{pmatrix} 5/6 \\ 1/6 \end{pmatrix} + (0.4)^k \begin{pmatrix} -5/6 \\ 5/6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Check: $x1 = (0.5, 0.5)$, $x2 = (0.7, 0.3)$, $x3 = (0.78, 0.22)$, each equal to $M$ times the previous one. After ten days the gap to the steady state is $(0.4)^{10} \cdot \frac56 \approx 0.0000874$; $x{10} \approx (0.83325, 0.16675)$. The rate of convergence is set by the second eigenvalue, 0.4.

Why 1 is always an eigenvalue of a Markov matrix: the columns of $M$ sum to 1 means $M^T(1, \dots, 1) = (1, \dots, 1)$, so 1 is an eigenvalue of $M^T$, hence of $M$ (same characteristic polynomial). For a Markov matrix with all entries positive, Perron's theorem adds that the eigenvalue 1 is simple and every other eigenvalue has $\lvert\lambda\rvert < 1$, so every starting distribution converges to the same steady state.

Chapter 6 — Complex eigenvalues

A real matrix can have a characteristic polynomial with no real roots. Rotation by $90°$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, has $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 1$, so $\lambda = \pm i$ — no real direction is kept, which is what a rotation should do. Working over $\mathbb{C}$ restores the theory: every $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ complex eigenvalues counted with multiplicity.

For a real matrix, non-real eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs: if $Av = \lambda v$ then conjugating gives $A\bar v = \bar\lambda \bar v$.

Worked example.

$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \sqrt2 \begin{pmatrix} \cos 45° & -\sin 45° \\ \sin 45° & \cos 45° \end{pmatrix}, \qquad pR(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 2.$$

The roots are $\lambda = 1 \pm i$. For $\lambda = 1 + i$: $R - (1+i)I = \begin{pmatrix} -i & -1 \\ 1 & -i\end{pmatrix}$, and by the $2 \times 2$ trick $v = (i, 1)$. Check: $Rv = (i - 1, i + 1)$ and $(1 + i)(i, 1) = (i + i^2, 1 + i) = (i - 1, 1 + i)$. The other eigenpair is $1 - i$ with $\bar v = (-i, 1)$.

Read the geometry from the polar form $1 + i = \sqrt2\, e^{i\pi/4}$: the modulus $\lvert\lambda\rvert = \sqrt 2$ is the scaling and the argument $45°$ is the rotation angle. Each application of $R$ turns the plane by $45°$ and scales by $\sqrt2$, so iterates spiral outwards; after 8 steps, $R^8 = (\sqrt2)^8 I = 16I$. In general a real $2 \times 2$ matrix with eigenvalues $a \pm bi$ ($b \neq 0$) is similar over $\mathbb{R}$ to $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a\end{pmatrix}$, a rotation combined with a scaling by $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

Theorem 2 still holds with complex eigenvalues: $(1 + i) + (1 - i) = 2 = \operatorname{tr}R$ and $(1+i)(1-i) = 2 = \det R$.

Chapter 7 — The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

Theorem 8 (Cayley–Hamilton). Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: if $pA(\lambda) = \lambda^n + c{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c0$, then

$$pA(A) = A^n + c{n-1}A^{n-1} + \dots + c1 A + c0 I = 0.$$

Proof for diagonalizable $A$. For a polynomial $q$, $q(PDP^{-1}) = P\,q(D)\,P^{-1}$ (apply the power rule term by term), and $q(D) = \operatorname{diag}(q(\lambda1), \dots, q(\lambdan))$. With $q = pA$ every diagonal entry is $pA(\lambdai) = 0$. ∎ The general case follows from the Jordan form, or from a density argument (diagonalizable matrices are dense among complex matrices, and $pA(A)$ depends continuously on $A$); see Axler, Chapter 8, or Hoffman and Kunze, Chapter 6.

> Common pitfall: The "proof" $pA(A) = \det(AI - A) = \det(0) = 0$ is wrong. $pA(A)$ is a matrix, obtained by substituting $A$ into a polynomial; $\det(AI - A)$ is a number. The substitution is not allowed inside the determinant.

Worked example. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$, $pA(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$. Indeed

$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 7 \\ 14 & 11 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^2 - 7A + 10I = \begin{pmatrix} 18 - 28 + 10 & 7 - 7 \\ 14 - 14 & 11 - 21 + 10 \end{pmatrix} = 0.$$

Two uses:

- Inverse without elimination. Rearrange: $A(7I - A) = 10I$, so $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}(7I - A) = \frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.- Reducing powers. $A^2 = 7A - 10I$, so every power of $A$ is a combination of $I$ and $A$. For an $n \times n$ matrix, every power is a combination of $I, A, \dots, A^{n-1}$.

For $B$ from Chapter 2, $pB(B) = B^3 - 6B^2 + 9B - 4I = 0$. In fact the smaller polynomial $(\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1)$ already kills $B$: $B^2 - 5B + 4I = 0$. The lowest-degree monic polynomial with $q(A) = 0$ is the minimal polynomial; a matrix is diagonalizable exactly when its minimal polynomial has no repeated roots.

Chapter 8 — Systems of differential equations

The matrix exponential

A linear system of ordinary differential equations with constant coefficients is

$$x'(t) = Ax(t), \qquad x(0) = x0,$$

with $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. In one dimension the answer is $x(t) = e^{at}x0$. The same works for matrices with the matrix exponential

$$e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2!} + \frac{(At)^3}{3!} + \cdots,$$

which converges for every square $A$ and satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}e^{At} = Ae^{At}$. The solution is $x(t) = e^{At}x0$, and it is unique.

If $A = PDP^{-1}$, the power rule applies to each term, so

$$e^{At} = P e^{Dt} P^{-1}, \qquad e^{Dt} = \operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda1 t}, \dots, e^{\lambdan t}).$$

Equivalently, writing $x0 = \sum ci vi$:

$$x(t) = c1 e^{\lambda1 t} v1 + \dots + cn e^{\lambdan t} vn.$$

This is the continuous twin of the difference-equation formula, with $\lambda^k$ replaced by $e^{\lambda t}$. The system is stable ($x(t) \to 0$ for every start) exactly when every eigenvalue has negative real part.

> Watch out: $e^{A + B} = e^A e^B$ only when $AB = BA$. And $e^{At}$ is not the matrix of entry-wise exponentials $e^{a{ij}t}$.

Worked example

Two quantities decay and leak into each other:

$$x1' = -2x1 + x2, \quad x2' = x1 - 2x2, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad x(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Trace $-4$, determinant $3$: $pA(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 3)$. Eigenvectors: $(1, 1)$ for $-1$ and $(1, -1)$ for $-3$. The start is $(1, 0) = \frac12(1,1) + \frac12(1,-1)$, so

$$x(t) = \frac12 e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac12 e^{-3t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad e^{At} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} e^{-t} + e^{-3t} & e^{-t} - e^{-3t} \\ e^{-t} - e^{-3t} & e^{-t} + e^{-3t} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Reading the answer: the difference $x1 - x2$ (the $(1,-1)$ mode) dies fast, like $e^{-3t}$; the total $x1 + x2$ (the $(1,1)$ mode) dies slowly, like $e^{-t}$. Both eigenvalues are negative, so the system is stable. Differentiating confirms $x' = Ax$ and $x(0) = (1, 0)$.

For a defective matrix the Jordan block gives extra polynomial factors: for $C$ from Chapter 4, $e^{Ct}$ contains terms $te^{2t}$ as well as $e^{2t}$. A second-order equation $y'' + a y' + b y = 0$ becomes a first-order system with $x = (y, y')$; Exercise 6 does one.

Chapter 9 — Power iteration

Characteristic polynomials are for hand calculation only. Their roots are extremely sensitive to rounding in the coefficients (Wilkinson's famous example has roots $1, 2, \dots, 20$, and a change of about $2^{-23}$ in one coefficient turns several of them complex), so numerical software never computes eigenvalues that way. The simplest numerical method is power iteration:

1. Start with a vector $x0$ (random, or anything not orthogonal to the target).2. Repeat: $y = Axk$, then $x{k+1} = y / \lVert y\rVert$ (normalise, to avoid overflow).

Why it works. Suppose $A$ is diagonalizable with $\lvert\lambda1\rvert > \lvert\lambda2\rvert \ge \dots$, and $x0 = \sum ci vi$ with $c1 \neq 0$. Then

$$A^k x0 = \lambda1^k \left( c1 v1 + c2 \left(\tfrac{\lambda2}{\lambda1}\right)^k v2 + \dots \right),$$

and every term except the first shrinks like $\lvert\lambda2/\lambda1\rvert^k$. The direction converges to $v1$, and the scale factor converges to $\lambda1$.

Worked example. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$, start at $x0 = (1, 0)$ and normalise so the largest entry is 1:

| $k$ | $y = Ax{k-1}$ | $xk$ | scale (largest entry of $y$) ||---|---|---|---|| 1 | $(4, 2)$ | $(1, 0.5)$ | 4 || 2 | $(4.5, 3.5)$ | $(1, 0.7778)$ | 4.5 || 3 | $(4.7778, 4.3333)$ | $(1, 0.9070)$ | 4.7778 || 4 | $(4.9070, 4.7209)$ | $(1, 0.9621)$ | 4.9070 || 5 | $(4.9621, 4.8863)$ | $(1, 0.9847)$ | 4.9621 |

The direction approaches $(1, 1)$ and the scale approaches 5. The gap $1 - (\text{second entry})$ goes $0.5, 0.222, 0.093, 0.038, 0.015$: each step multiplies it by roughly $0.4 = \lambda2/\lambda1 = 2/5$, as predicted.

Variations: inverse iteration (power iteration on $(A - \sigma I)^{-1}$) finds the eigenvalue closest to a shift $\sigma$. Production libraries (LAPACK, behind ) use the QR algorithm, which is in effect power iteration on a whole basis at once, kept orthonormal. Google's original PageRank is power iteration on a huge Markov matrix.

> Trick: When power iteration converges slowly, the ratio $\lvert\lambda2/\lambda1\rvert$ is close to 1. That ratio, not the size of the matrix, controls the number of iterations.

1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, diagonalize it, and compute its fifth power.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Trace 2, determinant $-3$: $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1)$. Eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ for 3 and $(1, -1)$ for $-1$. With $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ and $D = \operatorname{diag}(3, -1)$, $A^5 = PD^5P^{-1} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 3^5 + (-1)^5 & 3^5 - (-1)^5 \\ 3^5 - (-1)^5 & 3^5 + (-1)^5\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 121 & 122 \\ 122 & 121 \end{pmatrix}$.

</details>

2. Is $T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$ diagonalizable?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

It is triangular, so the eigenvalues are $2, 2, 7$. For $\lambda = 2$, $T - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 5\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 2, so the eigenspace has dimension $3 - 2 = 1$ (spanned by $(1, 0, 0)$). Algebraic multiplicity 2, geometric 1: not diagonalizable.

</details>

3. Prove that if $Av = \lambda v$ then $A^k v = \lambda^k v$ for all $k \ge 1$, and, when $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}v = \lambda^{-1}v$. Why is $\lambda \neq 0$ in the second case?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

By induction: $A^{k+1}v = A(\lambda^k v) = \lambda^k Av = \lambda^{k+1}v$. If $A$ is invertible, $0$ is not an eigenvalue (it would give a nonzero vector in the null space), so $\lambda \neq 0$. Apply $A^{-1}$ to $Av = \lambda v$: $v = \lambda A^{-1}v$, so $A^{-1}v = \lambda^{-1}v$.

</details>

4. Use Cayley–Hamilton to write $A^3$ as a combination of $A$ and $I$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$, and check against the formula for $A^k$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$A^2 = 7A - 10I$, so $A^3 = 7A^2 - 10A = 7(7A - 10I) - 10A = 39A - 70I = \begin{pmatrix} 156 - 70 & 39 \\ 78 & 117 - 70\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 86 & 39 \\ 78 & 47\end{pmatrix}$, the same as in Chapter 5.

</details>

5. In the weather chain, start on a sunny day, $x0 = (1, 0)$. Find $xk$ in closed form and $x2$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$(1, 0) = \frac16(5, 1) - \frac16(-1, 1)$, so $xk = (5/6, 1/6) + (0.4)^k(1/6, -1/6)$. For $k = 2$: $(5/6 + 0.16/6,\ 1/6 - 0.16/6) = (0.86, 0.14)$. Directly: $Mx0 = (0.9, 0.1)$ and $M(0.9, 0.1) = (0.81 + 0.05, 0.09 + 0.05) = (0.86, 0.14)$.

</details>

6. Solve $y'' + 3y' + 2y = 0$ with $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$ by writing it as $x' = Ax$ with $x = (y, y')$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3\end{pmatrix}$, trace $-3$, determinant 2: $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 2)$. Eigenvectors $(1, -1)$ for $-1$ and $(1, -2)$ for $-2$. Solve $c1(1, -1) + c2(1, -2) = (1, 0)$: $c1 = 2$, $c2 = -1$. So $y(t) = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}$. Check: $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = -2 + 2 = 0$.

</details>

7. Explain why power iteration started at $x0 = (1, -2)$ fails for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$, and why this almost never happens in practice.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$(1, -2)$ is the eigenvector for $\lambda2 = 2$, so $c1 = 0$ and every iterate stays on that line: the method returns $\lambda = 2$, not the dominant 5. With a random start, $c1 = 0$ has probability zero, and in floating point rounding errors introduce a small $v1$ component that then grows.

</details>

Chapter 2 — Colour space (the bug that ruins your scene)

PBR shading does its math in linear light space. Image files (JPG, PNG) are stored in sRGB (gamma-encoded for storage efficiency, perception-uniform on monitors).
