Linear Algebra — 03 · Linear Maps, Change of Basis and Determinants
Created2025-07-04|Mathematics Linear Algebra
Up to now a matrix has been a table of numbers that you multiply and row-reduce. This post changes the point of view: a matrix is only a description of a linear map, written in one particular pair of bases. Change the bases and the same map gets a different matrix. That idea explains why rank, trace and determinant are "real" properties of a map while individual entries are not, and it is the doorway to eigenvalues in the next post. The second half of the post builds the determinant from three simple rules, proves the key facts (product rule, invertibility test, Cramer's rule), and shows what the number means geometrically: the factor by which a map scales area or volume, with a sign for orientation. > Series map: 00 Vectors & Linear Systems → 01 Matrix Algebra, Inverses & LU → 02 Vector Spaces, Basis & Dimension → 03 Linear Maps, Change of Basis & Determinants (this post) → 04 Eigenvalues & Diagonalization → 05 Inner Products, Spectral Theorem & SVD. Learning Outcome - Decide whether a given function between vector spaces is linear, and find its kernel and image.- State and prove the rank–nullity theorem for linear maps, and use it to relate injective, surjective and invertible.- Write the matrix of a linear map relative to any bases, and explain why composition of maps is the matrix product.- Convert coordinates between bases, and compute the matrix of a map in a new basis with $P^{-1}AP$.- Recognise rotations, reflections, shears, projections and scalings in $\mathbb{R}^2$ from their matrices.- Define the determinant by its three axioms, compute it by cofactor expansion and by elimination, and prove $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$.- Interpret $\lvert\det A\rvert$ as a volume scaling factor and the sign of $\det A$ as orientation.- Solve small systems with Cramer's rule, and explain why it is not used for large ones. Chapter 1 — Linear maps Terminology - Vector space $V$ over $\mathbb{R}$ — as in Linear Algebra 02: a set with addition and scalar multiplication obeying the eight axioms. Everything in this post also works over $\mathbb{C}$ or any field.- Linear map (linear transformation) $T: V \to W$ — a function that respects addition and scalar multiplication (definition below). When $V = W$ it is also called a linear operator on $V$.- Domain $V$ and codomain $W$ — where inputs live and where outputs are allowed to land.- Kernel (null space) $\ker T = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0\}$.- Image (range) $\operatorname{im} T = \{T(v) : v \in V\}$.- Rank of $T$ — $\dim \operatorname{im} T$. Nullity of $T$ — $\dim \ker T$.- Injective (one-to-one) — $T(u) = T(v)$ implies $u = v$. Surjective (onto) — $\operatorname{im} T = W$. Bijective — both.- Isomorphism — a bijective linear map. $V$ and $W$ are isomorphic, written $V \cong W$, if an isomorphism exists.- $Pn$ — polynomials of degree at most $n$ with real coefficients; $\dim Pn = n + 1$. Definition A function $T: V \to W$ is linear if for all $u, v \in V$ and all scalars $c$: $$T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v), \qquad T(cv) = c\,T(v).$$ The two conditions combine into one: $T(c1 v1 + c2 v2) = c1 T(v1) + c2 T(v2)$. By induction, a linear map sends every linear combination to the same combination of the images. Taking $c = 0$ gives $T(0) = 0$: a linear map always sends zero to zero. Examples: 1. Matrix maps. For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the map $TA: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, $TA(x) = Ax$, is linear because $A(u+v) = Au + Av$ and $A(cv) = cAv$ (Linear Algebra 01).2. Differentiation. $D: P3 \to P2$, $D(p) = p'$. The sum rule and the constant-multiple rule of calculus are exactly the two linearity conditions.3. Integration. $I: C[0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$, $I(f) = \int0^1 f(x)\,dx$, where $C[0,1]$ is the space of continuous functions on $[0,1]$.4. Evaluation. $E: P2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $E(p) = (p(0), p(1))$.5. Transpose. $M \mapsto M^T$ from $m \times n$ matrices to $n \times m$ matrices. Non-examples: - Translation $T(x) = x + b$ with $b \neq 0$: $T(0) = b \neq 0$, so it is not linear. (It is called affine.)- $T(x, y) = (x + y, xy)$: $T(2 \cdot (1,1)) = (4, 4)$ but $2\,T(1,1) = (4, 2)$.- $T(x) = \lvert x\rvert$ on $\mathbb{R}$: $T(-1) = 1 \neq -T(1)$. > Trick: To show a map is not linear, one counterexample is enough — and checking $T(0) = 0$ first catches many cases in one line. To show a map is linear, you must check both conditions for arbitrary inputs. A linear map is fixed by what it does to a basis Theorem 1. Let $b1, \dots, bn$ be a basis of $V$ and let $w1, \dots, wn$ be any vectors in $W$. There is exactly one linear map $T: V \to W$ with $T(bi) = wi$ for every $i$. Proof. Every $v \in V$ has unique coordinates: $v = c1 b1 + \dots + cn bn$ (Linear Algebra 02). Define $T(v) = c1 w1 + \dots + cn wn$. This is well defined because the coordinates are unique. It is linear: if $v$ has coordinates $ci$ and $u$ has coordinates $di$, then $u + v$ has coordinates $ci + di$ and $kv$ has coordinates $kci$, and the formula is linear in the coordinates. It sends $bi$ to $wi$. For uniqueness, any linear $S$ with $S(bi) = wi$ satisfies $S(v) = \sum ci S(bi) = \sum ci wi = T(v)$. ∎ This theorem is the reason matrices work at all: to store a linear map you only need to store $n$ vectors, the images of the basis vectors. Chapter 2 — Kernel, image and rank–nullity Kernel and image are subspaces Proposition 2. For linear $T: V \to W$, $\ker T$ is a subspace of $V$ and $\operatorname{im} T$ is a subspace of $W$. Proof. $T(0) = 0$, so $0$ is in both. If $T(u) = T(v) = 0$ then $T(u + cv) = 0 + c \cdot 0 = 0$, so the kernel is closed under addition and scaling. If $w1 = T(v1)$ and $w2 = T(v2)$ then $w1 + c w2 = T(v1 + c v2)$ is also in the image. ∎ For a matrix map $TA$, the kernel is the null space $N(A)$ and the image is the column space $C(A)$ from Linear Algebra 02. Proposition 3. $T$ is injective if and only if $\ker T = \{0\}$. Proof. If $T$ is injective and $T(v) = 0 = T(0)$, then $v = 0$. Conversely, if $\ker T = \{0\}$ and $T(u) = T(v)$, then $T(u - v) = 0$, so $u - v = 0$. ∎ > Watch out: "The only solution of $T(v) = 0$ is $v = 0$" is a statement about one equation, but it controls every equation $T(v) = w$: each one then has at most one solution. This is the map version of "no free variables" from Linear Algebra 00. Rank–nullity for linear maps Linear Algebra 02 proved $\operatorname{rank} A + \dim N(A) = n$ for an $m \times n$ matrix by counting pivot and free columns. Here is the version for maps, with a proof that uses no matrices at all. Theorem 4 (rank–nullity). Let $V$ be finite-dimensional and $T: V \to W$ linear. Then $\operatorname{im} T$ is finite-dimensional and $$\dim V = \dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T.$$ Proof. $\ker T$ is a subspace of the finite-dimensional space $V$, so it has a basis $u1, \dots, uk$ (possibly $k = 0$). By the basis-extension theorem of Linear Algebra 02, extend it to a basis $u1, \dots, uk, w1, \dots, wr$ of $V$, so $\dim V = k + r$. We show that $T(w1), \dots, T(wr)$ is a basis of $\operatorname{im} T$; then $\dim \operatorname{im} T = r$ and we are done. Spanning. Any vector in the image is $T(v)$ for some $v = \sum ai ui + \sum bj wj$. Since $T(ui) = 0$, we get $T(v) = \sum bj T(wj)$. Independence. Suppose $\sum cj T(wj) = 0$. Then $T\left(\sum cj wj\right) = 0$, so $\sum cj wj \in \ker T$ and can be written as $\sum di ui$. Then $\sum cj wj - \sum di ui = 0$ is a linear relation among the basis vectors of $V$, so every $cj$ (and every $di$) is zero. ∎ Worked example. $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $T(x, y, z) = (x + 2y - z,\ 2x + 4y + z)$. Its matrix is $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Solving $T(v) = 0$: adding the two equations gives $3x + 6y = 0$, so $x = -2y$, and then $z = x + 2y = 0$. The kernel is the line spanned by $(-2, 1, 0)$, so the nullity is 1. Rank–nullity forces $\dim \operatorname{im} T = 3 - 1 = 2$, so the image is all of $\mathbb{R}^2$: $T$ is surjective but not injective. Worked example (no matrix in sight). $D: P3 \to P2$, $D(p) = p'$. The kernel is the constant polynomials (dimension 1). Every $q \in P2$ is the derivative of some cubic, so the image is $P2$ (dimension 3). Check: $4 = 1 + 3$. Consequences Corollary 5. Let $T: V \to W$ be linear with $V, W$ finite-dimensional. 1. If $\dim V > \dim W$, then $T$ is not injective (the kernel has dimension at least $\dim V - \dim W > 0$).2. If $\dim V < \dim W$, then $T$ is not surjective (the image has dimension at most $\dim V$).3. If $\dim V = \dim W$, then $T$ injective $\iff$ $T$ surjective $\iff$ $T$ is an isomorphism. Proof of 3. Injective means $\dim \ker T = 0$, which by rank–nullity means $\dim \operatorname{im} T = \dim V = \dim W$, which means $\operatorname{im} T = W$ (a subspace of full dimension is the whole space). ∎ Part 3 is the abstract form of the invertible matrix theorem from Linear Algebra 01: for a square matrix, "only the trivial solution" and "a solution for every right-hand side" are the same condition. Theorem 6 (classification by dimension). Two finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. In particular every $n$-dimensional real space is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$. Proof. An isomorphism sends a basis to a basis (it is injective, so independence is kept, and surjective, so the images span), so dimensions agree. Conversely, if $\dim V = n$ with basis $b1, \dots, bn$, the coordinate map $v \mapsto (c1, \dots, cn)$ is linear, injective and surjective. Composing two such maps gives $V \cong \mathbb{R}^n \cong W$. ∎ The inverse of an isomorphism is automatically linear: if $T(u') = u$ and $T(v') = v$, then $T(u' + cv') = u + cv$, so $T^{-1}(u + cv) = T^{-1}(u) + cT^{-1}(v)$. > Common pitfall: Isomorphic does not mean equal. $P2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ are isomorphic, but the isomorphism depends on a choice of basis. There is no single "correct" identification — which is exactly why the next chapter keeps track of bases. Chapter 3 — The matrix of a linear map Coordinates Fix an ordered basis $\mathcal{B} = (b1, \dots, bn)$ of $V$. The coordinate vector of $v$ is the column $[v]{\mathcal{B}} = (c1, \dots, cn)^T$ with $v = \sum ci bi$. The map $v \mapsto [v]{\mathcal{B}}$ is the isomorphism $V \to \mathbb{R}^n$ of Theorem 6. Order matters: swapping $b1$ and $b2$ swaps the first two coordinates. The matrix relative to two bases Let $T: V \to W$ be linear, with basis $\mathcal{B} = (b1, \dots, bn)$ of $V$ and $\mathcal{C} = (c1, \dots, cm)$ of $W$. The matrix of $T$ relative to $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ is the $m \times n$ matrix $$[T]{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = \Big( [T(b1)]{\mathcal{C}} \ \ [T(b2)]{\mathcal{C}} \ \ \cdots \ \ [T(bn)]{\mathcal{C}} \Big).$$ Column $j$ = the $\mathcal{C}$-coordinates of the image of the $j$-th basis vector. With it, applying $T$ becomes matrix multiplication on coordinates: $$[T(v)]{\mathcal{C}} = [T]{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}\, [v]{\mathcal{B}}.$$ Why: if $v = \sum xj bj$ then $T(v) = \sum xj T(bj)$, and taking $\mathcal{C}$-coordinates (a linear operation) gives $\sum xj [T(bj)]{\mathcal{C}}$, which is the matrix times the column $x$. The arrow notation $\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}$ reads right to left: coordinates in $\mathcal{B}$ go in, coordinates in $\mathcal{C}$ come out. When $V = W$ and the same basis is used on both sides, write $[T]{\mathcal{B}}$. Worked example. $D: P3 \to P2$ with bases $(1, x, x^2, x^3)$ and $(1, x, x^2)$. The images of the basis vectors are $0,\ 1,\ 2x,\ 3x^2$, whose coordinates are the columns: $$[D] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Check with $p = 1 + 2x - x^2 + 4x^3$: $[D]\,(1, 2, -1, 4)^T = (2, -2, 12)^T$, and indeed $p' = 2 - 2x + 12x^2$. The matrix has rank 3 and a one-dimensional null space spanned by $(1,0,0,0)^T$ — the constant polynomial 1 — matching the rank–nullity count above. Composition is the matrix product Theorem 7. Let $T: U \to V$ and $S: V \to W$ be linear, with bases $\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}$ of $U, V, W$. Then $$[S \circ T]{\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = [S]{\mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{C}}\ [T]{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}.$$ Proof. For every $u \in U$: $[S(T(u))]{\mathcal{D}} = [S]\,[T(u)]{\mathcal{C}} = [S]\,[T]\,[u]{\mathcal{B}}$. Taking $u = bj$ (so $[u]{\mathcal{B}}$ is the $j$-th standard column) shows that column $j$ of $[S \circ T]$ equals column $j$ of $[S][T]$. ∎ This is why matrix multiplication is defined the strange row-times-column way: it is the only definition that makes "do $T$, then do $S$" correspond to a product. It also explains why the product is associative (composition of functions is) and generally not commutative (doing things in a different order usually gives a different result). The order is right to left: in $ST$, $T$ acts first. Two consequences follow at once. $T$ is an isomorphism if and only if its matrix (in any bases) is invertible, and then $[T^{-1}]{\mathcal{B} \leftarrow \mathcal{C}} = \left([T]{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}\right)^{-1}$. And the rank of $T$ equals the rank of any matrix that represents it. Chapter 4 — Change of basis and similarity The change-of-basis matrix Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathcal{B} = (b1, \dots, bn)$ another basis. Put the new basis vectors, written in standard coordinates, into the columns of $$P = P{\mathcal{E} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}} = \big( b1 \ \ b2 \ \ \cdots \ \ bn \big).$$ Then $v = \sum ci bi = P\,[v]{\mathcal{B}}$, that is $$[v]{\mathcal{E}} = P\,[v]{\mathcal{B}}, \qquad [v]{\mathcal{B}} = P^{-1}[v]{\mathcal{E}}.$$ $P$ is invertible because its columns are a basis. $P$ is simply the matrix of the identity map, $[\mathrm{id}]{\mathcal{E} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}$: it takes $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates in and gives $\mathcal{E}$-coordinates out. > Common pitfall: The matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors converts new coordinates to old, not old to new. To get new coordinates you must solve $P c = v$ (or apply $P^{-1}$). Writing the arrow $\mathcal{E} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}$ on every change-of-basis matrix prevents this mistake. The same map in a new basis Let $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ have standard matrix $A = [T]{\mathcal{E}}$. Its matrix in the basis $\mathcal{B}$ is found by composing: convert $\mathcal{B}$-coordinates to standard ($P$), apply $T$ ($A$), convert back ($P^{-1}$): $$[T]{\mathcal{B}} = P^{-1} A P.$$ This is Theorem 7 applied to $T = \mathrm{id} \circ T \circ \mathrm{id}$. For a map between different spaces, with change-of-basis matrices $P$ on the domain and $Q$ on the codomain, the rule is $[T]{\mathcal{C}' \leftarrow \mathcal{B}'} = Q^{-1}\,[T]{\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}}\,P$. Worked example. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and take the basis $b1 = (1, 1)$, $b2 = (1, -1)$. Then $$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$ You can see the diagonal answer directly: $Ab1 = (3, 3) = 3b1$ and $Ab2 = (1, -1) = b2$. In the new basis the map just stretches the $b1$ direction by 3 and leaves the $b2$ direction alone. Now track one vector. $v = (5, 1)$ has $[v]{\mathcal{B}} = P^{-1}v = (3, 2)$, because $3(1,1) + 2(1,-1) = (5, 1)$. In new coordinates the map gives $\operatorname{diag}(3,1)\,(3,2)^T = (9, 2)^T$, and $9(1,1) + 2(1,-1) = (11, 7) = Av$. The two routes agree. Choosing a basis that makes the matrix diagonal is the whole subject of Linear Algebra 04: $b1$ and $b2$ here are eigenvectors. Similarity Square matrices $A$ and $B$ are similar if $B = P^{-1}AP$ for some invertible $P$. Similar matrices are the same linear operator written in two bases. Similarity is an equivalence relation (reflexive with $P = I$, symmetric with $P^{-1}$, transitive with $PQ$). Anything defined for the operator rather than the table of numbers must be the same for similar matrices. Such quantities are called similarity invariants: | Invariant | Why it survives $A \mapsto P^{-1}AP$ ||-----------|--------------------------------------|| Rank | rank of the map, which does not depend on coordinates || Trace | $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$, so $\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}(AP)) = \operatorname{tr}((AP)P^{-1})$ || Determinant | product rule (Chapter 6): $\det P^{-1} \det A \det P = \det A$ || Characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues | Linear Algebra 04 | The trace identity is a one-line calculation: $\operatorname{tr}(XY) = \sumi \sumj x{ij} y{ji} = \sumj \sumi y{ji} x{ij} = \operatorname{tr}(YX)$. Check the example: $\operatorname{tr}A = 4 = 3 + 1$. > Watch out: Equal invariants do not prove similarity. $I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ have the same rank, trace and determinant, but only $I$ is similar to $I$ (because $P^{-1}IP = I$ for every $P$). Chapter 5 — Geometry of maps in the plane The quickest way to read a $2 \times 2$ matrix: its columns are where $e1 = (1,0)$ and $e2 = (0,1)$ land. Draw those two arrows and you have drawn the map, because the unit square goes to the parallelogram they span and every grid line goes to a parallel grid line. | Map | Matrix | What it does | det ||-----|--------|--------------|-----|| Rotation by $\theta$ | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ | turns every vector by angle $\theta$ counter-clockwise | $1$ || Reflection in the line at angle $\theta$ | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}$ | mirror image; vectors on the line stay put | $-1$ || Shear | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | slides each point sideways by $k$ times its height | $1$ || Projection onto the line at angle $\theta$ | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta & \cos\theta\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}$ | drops each point perpendicularly onto the line | $0$ || Scaling | $\begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & b \end{pmatrix}$ | stretches $x$ by $a$ and $y$ by $b$ | $ab$ | Where these come from: - Rotation. $e1$ goes to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and $e2$ (at angle $90°$) goes to $(\cos(\theta + 90°), \sin(\theta + 90°)) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$. For $\theta = 30°$ this gives $\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{3}/2 & -1/2 \\ 1/2 & \sqrt{3}/2 \end{pmatrix}$.- Projection. With the unit vector $u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ along the line, the projection of $x$ is $(u \cdot x)\,u = (u u^T) x$, and $uu^T$ is the matrix in the table. It satisfies $P^2 = P$: projecting twice is the same as projecting once.- Reflection. Reflection = $2 \times$ projection $-$ identity (go to the line, then the same distance again). Using $2\cos^2\theta - 1 = \cos 2\theta$ and $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta$ gives the table entry. It satisfies $F^2 = I$. Composition does not commute. Let $R$ be rotation by $90°$ and $F$ reflection in the $x$-axis: $$R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad F = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad FR = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad RF = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$ "Rotate, then reflect" ($FR$) is the reflection in the line $y = -x$; "reflect, then rotate" ($RF$) is the reflection in $y = x$. Two different maps. A projection is diagonal in the right basis. The projection onto the line $y = 2x$ has standard matrix $\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. In the basis $(1, 2), (-2, 1)$ — one vector along the line, one perpendicular to it — its matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: keep the first coordinate, kill the second. Same map, much clearer matrix. Chapter 6 — The determinant Terminology - Multilinear (in the rows) — linear in each row separately when the other rows are held fixed.- Alternating — the value is $0$ whenever two rows are equal.- Permutation of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ — a bijection $\sigma$ of the set to itself; $Sn$ is the set of all $n!$ of them.- Sign $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ — $+1$ if $\sigma$ is a product of an even number of swaps, $-1$ if odd. Equivalently $(-1)^{\text{number of inversions}}$, where an inversion is a pair $i < j$ with $\sigma(i) > \sigma(j)$.- Minor $M{ij}$ — the determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix left after deleting row $i$ and column $j$. Cofactor $C{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M{ij}$. The three axioms For $2 \times 2$ matrices you know $\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc$. The clean way to generalise is not to guess a formula but to list the properties we want and prove that exactly one function has them. A function $D$ from $n \times n$ matrices to $\mathbb{R}$ is a determinant function if: 1. (Multilinear) $D$ is linear in each row: $D(\dots, u + cv, \dots) = D(\dots, u, \dots) + c\,D(\dots, v, \dots)$, other rows fixed.2. (Alternating) $D(A) = 0$ if two rows of $A$ are equal.3. (Normalised) $D(I) = 1$. Theorem 8 (existence and uniqueness). There is exactly one determinant function, written $\det$. Moreover, any function $D$ satisfying axioms 1 and 2 (but not necessarily 3) equals $D(I) \cdot \det$. Existence is shown by checking that the Leibniz formula below satisfies the three axioms; uniqueness follows because the axioms alone determine the value on every matrix, as the row-operation rules show. A full proof is in Axler (Chapter 9) or Hoffman and Kunze (Chapter 5); here we state it and use it. What the axioms force: row operations From the axioms alone: - Swapping two rows changes the sign. Put $u + v$ in both rows $i$ and $j$. By axiom 2 the value is 0; expanding with axiom 1 gives $D(u,u) + D(u,v) + D(v,u) + D(v,v) = 0$ (other rows omitted), and the first and last terms are 0, so $D(u,v) = -D(v,u)$.- Scaling one row by $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$ (axiom 1).- Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged: $D(\dots, u + cv, \dots, v, \dots) = D(\dots, u, \dots, v, \dots) + c\,D(\dots, v, \dots, v, \dots)$, and the last term is 0.- A zero row gives determinant 0 (scale that row by 0).- A triangular matrix has determinant = product of its diagonal entries. If all diagonal entries are nonzero, row replacements clear the off-diagonal entries without changing $D$, leaving a diagonal matrix, and axiom 1 plus axiom 3 give the product. If a diagonal entry is zero, elimination produces a zero row, so both sides are 0. These rules are also the fastest way to compute: row-reduce to triangular form, keeping track of swaps and scalings. Leibniz formula and cofactor expansion Leibniz formula. $$\det A = \sum{\sigma \in Sn} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a{1\sigma(1)}\, a{2\sigma(2)} \cdots a{n\sigma(n)}.$$ Each term picks one entry from every row and every column. For $n = 2$ there are two terms, $a{11}a{22} - a{12}a{21}$. For $n = 3$ there are six: $$\det A = a{11}a{22}a{33} + a{12}a{23}a{31} + a{13}a{21}a{32} - a{13}a{22}a{31} - a{11}a{23}a{32} - a{12}a{21}a{33}.$$ Cofactor (Laplace) expansion along row $i$ or column $j$: $$\det A = \sum{j=1}^{n} a{ij} C{ij} \quad \text{(row } i\text{)}, \qquad \det A = \sum{i=1}^{n} a{ij} C{ij} \quad \text{(column } j\text{)}.$$ The signs $(-1)^{i+j}$ follow a checkerboard starting with $+$ in the top-left corner. Expand along the row or column with the most zeros. Worked example — three ways. Let $$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Cofactor expansion along row 1: $$\det M = 1(5 \cdot 10 - 6 \cdot 8) - 2(4 \cdot 10 - 6 \cdot 7) + 3(4 \cdot 8 - 5 \cdot 7) = 1(2) - 2(-2) + 3(-3) = -3.$$ Leibniz, six terms: $50 + 84 + 96 - 105 - 48 - 80 = 230 - 233 = -3$. Elimination: $R2 - 4R1$ and $R3 - 7R1$ give rows $(0, -3, -6)$ and $(0, -6, -11)$; then $R3 - 2R2$ gives $(0, 0, 1)$. Only row replacements were used, so the determinant is unchanged: $\det M = 1 \cdot (-3) \cdot 1 = -3$. > Watch out: The "diagonals" trick (rule of Sarrus) works for $3 \times 3$ matrices only. For $4 \times 4$ it gives 8 terms, but the determinant has $4! = 24$. The product rule and other properties Theorem 9. $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ for all $n \times n$ matrices $A, B$. Proof. Fix $B$ and define $D(A) = \det(AB)$. Row $i$ of $AB$ is (row $i$ of $A$) $\times B$, which is linear in row $i$ of $A$, so $D$ is multilinear in the rows of $A$. If $A$ has two equal rows, so does $AB$, so $D(A) = 0$. By the second part of Theorem 8, $D(A) = D(I)\det A = \det(B)\det(A)$. ∎ Theorem 10. $\det A^T = \det A$. Reason. In the Leibniz formula, the term for $\sigma$ in $\det A^T$ is the term for $\sigma^{-1}$ in $\det A$, and $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$. As $\sigma$ runs over $Sn$ so does $\sigma^{-1}$. ∎ So every statement about rows also holds for columns: column swaps flip the sign, and so on. Theorem 11 (the invertibility test). A square matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$. In that case $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$. Proof. Row-reduce: $Ek \cdots E1 A = R$ with $R$ in reduced row echelon form. Each elementary operation multiplies the determinant by a nonzero number ($-1$, $c \neq 0$, or $1$), so $\det A \neq 0 \iff \det R \neq 0$. A square RREF is either $I$ (determinant 1) or has a zero row (determinant 0), and $R = I$ exactly when $A$ is invertible (Linear Algebra 01). For the formula, $\det A \det A^{-1} = \det I = 1$. ∎ Further consequences: - $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$ for an $n \times n$ matrix (each of the $n$ rows is scaled). For the matrix $M$ above, $\det(2M) = 8 \cdot (-3) = -24$.- Similar matrices have equal determinants, so $\det T$ is well defined for a linear operator $T$: use its matrix in any basis.- For block upper triangular matrices, $\det \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix} = \det A \det D$ (with $A$, $D$ square). > Common pitfall: $\det(A + B) \neq \det A + \det B$ in general. Try $A = B = I2$: the left side is $\det(2I) = 4$, the right side is 2. The determinant is multiplicative, not additive. Determinant as signed volume For $n \times n$ $A$ with columns $a1, \dots, an$, the number $\lvert\det A\rvert$ is the volume (area when $n = 2$) of the parallelepiped $\{t1 a1 + \dots + tn an : 0 \le ti \le 1\}$. Why this is plausible: signed volume, as a function of the edge vectors, satisfies the axioms. Scaling one edge by $c$ scales the volume by $c$. Sliding one edge along another (a shear) does not change the volume — Cavalieri's principle, the same reason a slanted stack of coins has the same volume as a straight one. Two equal edges give a flat box of volume 0. The unit cube has volume 1. By uniqueness, signed volume is the determinant. Since $A$ sends the unit cube (volume 1) to this parallelepiped, and the map is linear, $A$ multiplies the volume of every region by $\lvert\det A\rvert$. This is the factor $\lvert\det J\rvert$ in the change-of-variables formula for multiple integrals. The sign records orientation: $\det A > 0$ keeps the order of the edges (counter-clockwise stays counter-clockwise in the plane), $\det A < 0$ reverses it, as a mirror does. $\det A = 0$ means the space is flattened into a lower dimension — and that information cannot be undone, which is Theorem 11 seen geometrically. Example in $\mathbb{R}^3$: the columns $(1,0,0)$, $(1,2,0)$, $(1,1,3)$ form an upper triangular matrix with determinant $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 6$, so the box they span has volume 6. If $\det A \neq 0$, the unique solution of $Ax = b$ is $$xi = \frac{\det Ai(b)}{\det A}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,$$ where $Ai(b)$ is $A$ with column $i$ replaced by $b$. Proof. Let $Ii(x)$ be the identity matrix with column $i$ replaced by $x$. Then $A\,Ii(x) = Ai(b)$: its columns are $Ae1, \dots, Ax, \dots, Aen$, which are the columns of $A$ with $b = Ax$ in position $i$. Expanding $\det Ii(x)$ along row $i$ gives $xi$. The product rule then gives $\det A \cdot xi = \det Ai(b)$. ∎ Worked example. $2x + y = 5$, $x + 3y = 10$. Here $\det A = 6 - 1 = 5$, and $$x = \frac{\det\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 10 & 3 \end{pmatrix}}{5} = \frac{5}{5} = 1, \qquad y = \frac{\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 10 \end{pmatrix}}{5} = \frac{15}{5} = 3.$$ Check: $2 + 3 = 5$ and $1 + 9 = 10$. The adjugate. Applying Cramer's rule to each column of $AX = I$ gives an explicit inverse: $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A), \qquad \operatorname{adj}(A){ij} = C{ji}.$$ Note the transpose: entry $(i,j)$ of the adjugate is the cofactor at $(j,i)$. For $2 \times 2$ this is the familiar $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$. For $M$ above, $$\operatorname{adj}(M) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 & -3 \\ 2 & -11 & 6 \\ -3 & 6 & -3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad M^{-1} = -\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{adj}(M) = \begin{pmatrix} -2/3 & -4/3 & 1 \\ -2/3 & 11/3 & -2 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Why not Cramer for large systems Cramer's rule is valuable for theory: it shows that each $xi$ is a rational function of the entries of $A$ and $b$, so the solution depends continuously (indeed smoothly) on the data wherever $\det A \neq 0$. Closeness to singularity is measured by the condition number (Linear Algebra 05 and the planned numerical post), not by the size of $\det A$. > Trick: In practice, a determinant is computed from an LU factorisation with partial pivoting, $PA = LU$: $\det A = \pm \prod u{ii}$, the sign coming from the number of row swaps. Linear Algebra — 02 · Vector Spaces, Subspaces, Basis and Dimension
Created2025-05-26|Mathematics Linear Algebra
Linear Algebra 00 and 01 worked with columns of numbers. This post asks what those columns have in common with polynomials, functions, matrices and the solutions of a differential equation — and answers with one definition: a vector space. From that definition come the ideas that organise the rest of the subject: subspaces, span, linear independence, basis and dimension. The central theorem is that dimension is well defined — every basis of a space has the same size — and it is proved here in full through the Steinitz exchange lemma. The abstract ideas are then brought back to matrices through Strang's four fundamental subspaces, with a complete worked example, a proof that row rank equals column rank, and the rank–nullity theorem in matrix form. The post ends with sums and direct sums of subspaces and the formula $\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W)$. > Series map: 00 Vectors & Linear Systems → 01 Matrix Algebra, Inverses & LU → 02 Vector Spaces, Basis & Dimension (this post) → 03 Linear Maps, Change of Basis & Determinants → 04 Eigenvalues & Diagonalization → 05 Inner Products, Spectral Theorem & SVD. Learning Outcome - State the axioms of a vector space over a field, and check them for $F^n$, polynomials, functions and matrices.- Prove simple consequences of the axioms, such as $0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ and $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$.- Use the subspace test, and recognise common subspaces and non-subspaces.- Work with span and linear independence in any vector space, including the linear dependence lemma.- Prove the Steinitz exchange lemma and deduce that dimension is well defined.- Extend an independent list to a basis, shrink a spanning list to a basis, and compute coordinates.- Find bases and dimensions of the four fundamental subspaces of a matrix from its RREF.- Prove that row rank equals column rank and that $\operatorname{rank} A + \dim N(A) = n$.- Work with sums and direct sums, and prove the dimension formula for $U + W$. Chapter 1 — Fields and vector spaces Terminology - Field — a set $F$ with an addition and a multiplication that satisfy the usual rules of arithmetic: both operations are associative and commutative, multiplication distributes over addition, there are elements $0 \neq 1$ with $a + 0 = a$ and $1a = a$, every $a$ has a negative $-a$, and every nonzero $a$ has an inverse $a^{-1}$. Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{F}2 = \{0, 1\}$ with arithmetic mod 2. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are not a field ($2$ has no integer inverse).- Scalars — the elements of the field. In this series $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ unless stated. Everything in Linear Algebra 00 and 01 — elimination, pivots, inverses, LU — used only these field rules, so it works word for word over any field. The definition A vector space over $F$ is a set $V$ with an addition $V \times V \to V$ and a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ such that, for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and $a, b \in F$: 1. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$ (commutativity).2. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$ (associativity).3. There is an element $\mathbf{0} \in V$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$ for all $\mathbf{v}$ (additive identity).4. For each $\mathbf{v}$ there is $\mathbf{w}$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ (additive inverse).5. $a(b\mathbf{v}) = (ab)\mathbf{v}$.6. $1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$.7. $a(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u} + a\mathbf{v}$.8. $(a + b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{v}$. The elements of $V$ are called vectors, whatever they actually are. First consequences Proposition. In any vector space: (a) the zero vector is unique; (b) each $\mathbf{v}$ has exactly one additive inverse, written $-\mathbf{v}$; (c) $0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$; (d) $a\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$; (e) $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$. Proof. (a) If $\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{0}'$ are both identities, $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}' = \mathbf{0}'$. (b) If $\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}'$ are both inverses of $\mathbf{v}$, then $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}') = (\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w}' = \mathbf{w}'$. (c) $0\mathbf{v} = (0 + 0)\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} + 0\mathbf{v}$; add $-(0\mathbf{v})$ to both sides. (d) The same with $a\mathbf{0} = a(\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0})$. (e) $\mathbf{v} + (-1)\mathbf{v} = (1 + (-1))\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, so $(-1)\mathbf{v}$ is the inverse of $\mathbf{v}$, by (b). $\blacksquare$ Examples | Space | Vectors | Operations ||---|---|---|| $F^n$ | columns $(x1, \dots, xn)$ | entry by entry || $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ | polynomials of degree at most $n$ | add coefficients; scale coefficients || $\mathcal{P}(F)$ | all polynomials | same || $F^S$ | all functions $f : S \to F$ on a set $S$ | $(f + g)(s) = f(s) + g(s)$, $(af)(s) = a f(s)$ || $C[a, b]$ | continuous real functions on $[a, b]$ | pointwise || $M{m \times n}(F)$ | $m \times n$ matrices | entry by entry | $F^n$ is really the special case $F^S$ with $S = \{1, \dots, n\}$: a column is a function from positions to numbers. Non-examples. The first quadrant $\{(x, y) : x, y \ge 0\}$ fails axiom 4 — $(1, 0)$ has no inverse inside it. The line $\{(x, y) : y = x + 1\}$ has no zero vector. The polynomials of degree exactly 2 are not closed under addition: $(t^2 + t) + (-t^2) = t$. Chapter 2 — Subspaces Terminology - Subspace — a subset $U \subseteq V$ that is itself a vector space with the same addition and scalar multiplication. Theorem (subspace test). A subset $U$ of a vector space $V$ is a subspace if and only if 1. $\mathbf{0} \in U$;2. $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in U \Rightarrow \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} \in U$ (closed under addition);3. $a \in F$, $\mathbf{u} \in U \Rightarrow a\mathbf{u} \in U$ (closed under scalar multiplication). Proof. A subspace clearly satisfies 2 and 3, and it contains its own zero vector $\mathbf{z}$ with $\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}$; adding $-\mathbf{z}$ in $V$ shows $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, so 1 holds. Conversely, suppose 1–3 hold. Conditions 2 and 3 make addition and scalar multiplication land in $U$. Axioms 1, 2, 5–8 are identities that hold for all vectors of $V$, so in particular for those of $U$. Axiom 3 holds by condition 1. For axiom 4: if $\mathbf{u} \in U$ then $-\mathbf{u} = (-1)\mathbf{u} \in U$ by condition 3 and part (e) of the proposition. $\blacksquare$ > Trick: conditions 2 and 3 together are the same as "closed under linear combinations": $a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w} \in U$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in U$ and scalars $a, b$. Check $\mathbf{0}$ first — it is the fastest way to reject a non-subspace. Examples and non-examples - Null space $N(A) = \{\mathbf{x} \in F^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\}$ is a subspace of $F^n$: $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, and $A(a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w}) = aA\mathbf{u} + bA\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$.- Column space $C(A) = \{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in F^n\}$, the span of the columns, is a subspace of $F^m$.- $\{p \in \mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R}) : p(1) = 0\}$ is a subspace; $\{p : p(1) = 5\}$ is not (no zero polynomial).- The symmetric $n \times n$ matrices form a subspace of $M{n \times n}$.- The solutions $y$ of $y'' + y = 0$ form a subspace of the twice-differentiable functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.- The solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ are not a subspace (Linear Algebra 00, Chapter 7): $\mathbf{0}$ is not a solution.- The union of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a subspace: $(1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1)$ is in neither. Intersections are subspaces. If $U$ and $W$ are subspaces, so is $U \cap W$: it contains $\mathbf{0}$, and a linear combination of vectors in both lies in both. Unions usually are not, as the axes show; Chapter 8 replaces the union by the sum $U + W$. Chapter 3 — Span and linear independence in general The definitions from Linear Algebra 00 carry over unchanged. For a finite list $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k$ in $V$: - $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k)$ is the set of all linear combinations $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{v}k$. By convention the span of the empty list is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$.- The list spans $V$ if its span is all of $V$.- The list is linearly independent if $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{v}k = \mathbf{0}$ forces $a1 = \dots = ak = 0$; otherwise it is dependent. The empty list is independent. Proposition. $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k)$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ containing every $\mathbf{v}i$. Proof. It contains $\mathbf{0}$, and a combination of combinations is a combination, so it is a subspace; it contains each $\mathbf{v}i$. Any subspace containing all $\mathbf{v}i$ is closed under linear combinations, so it contains the span. $\blacksquare$ The linear dependence lemma Lemma. If $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}m$ is linearly dependent, then there is an index $j$ such that 1. $\mathbf{v}j \in \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}{j-1})$, and2. removing $\mathbf{v}j$ from the list does not change its span. Proof. Take a relation $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + am\mathbf{v}m = \mathbf{0}$ with not all $ai = 0$, and let $j$ be the largest index with $aj \neq 0$. Then $$\mathbf{v}j = -\frac{a1}{aj}\mathbf{v}1 - \dots - \frac{a{j-1}}{aj}\mathbf{v}{j-1},$$ which proves 1. (If $j = 1$ this says $\mathbf{v}1 = \mathbf{0}$, the span of the empty list.) For 2, in any combination of the full list, replace $\mathbf{v}j$ by the right-hand side above; the result is a combination of the list without $\mathbf{v}j$. $\blacksquare$ Independence outside Fⁿ - In $\mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R})$, the list $1, t, t^2$ is independent: if $a + bt + ct^2$ is the zero polynomial, all its coefficients are 0.- In the functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $\sin$ and $\cos$ are independent: if $a\sin x + b\cos x = 0$ for all $x$, then $x = 0$ gives $b = 0$ and $x = \pi/2$ gives $a = 0$. > Common pitfall: "$a\sin x + b\cos x = 0$" means equality as functions, for every $x$. Finding one $x$ where a combination is zero proves nothing; you must show the combination is zero everywhere only when all weights are zero. Chapter 4 — Basis, the exchange lemma and dimension Terminology - Basis of $V$ — a list of vectors in $V$ that is linearly independent and spans $V$.- Finite-dimensional — $V$ is spanned by some finite list. Otherwise infinite-dimensional.- Standard basis of $F^n$ — $\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n$. Of $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ — $1, t, \dots, t^n$. Theorem (unique representation). A list $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}n$ is a basis of $V$ if and only if every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be written as $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + an\mathbf{v}n$ in exactly one way. Proof. Spanning is "at least one way". If a basis gave two ways, subtracting them gives $\sum (ai - bi)\mathbf{v}i = \mathbf{0}$, so every $ai = bi$ by independence. Conversely, uniqueness for $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (which is $0\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + 0\mathbf{v}n$) is exactly independence. $\blacksquare$ The Steinitz exchange lemma Lemma (Steinitz exchange). Let $V$ be a vector space. Suppose $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}m$ is linearly independent in $V$ and $\mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$. Then $m \le n$. Moreover, after renumbering the $\mathbf{w}$'s, the list $$\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}m, \mathbf{w}{m+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$$ also spans $V$. Proof. We show by induction on $k = 0, 1, \dots, m$ that $k \le n$ and that, after renumbering, $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k, \mathbf{w}{k+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$. For $k = 0$ this is the hypothesis. Assume it holds for some $k < m$. Since the list $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k, \mathbf{w}{k+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$, we can write $$\mathbf{u}{k+1} = a1\mathbf{u}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{u}k + b{k+1}\mathbf{w}{k+1} + \dots + bn\mathbf{w}n.$$ If every $bj$ were 0 (in particular, if no $\mathbf{w}$'s were left, $k = n$), then $\mathbf{u}{k+1}$ would be a combination of $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k$, contradicting independence of the $\mathbf{u}$'s. So some $bj \neq 0$; hence $k + 1 \le n$. Renumber so that $b{k+1} \neq 0$ and solve for $\mathbf{w}{k+1}$: $$\mathbf{w}{k+1} = \frac{1}{b{k+1}}\Bigl(\mathbf{u}{k+1} - \sum{i=1}^{k} ai\mathbf{u}i - \sum{j=k+2}^{n} bj\mathbf{w}j\Bigr).$$ So $\mathbf{w}{k+1}$ lies in the span of $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}{k+1}, \mathbf{w}{k+2}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$. That span therefore contains the whole old spanning list, so it is $V$. This completes the step. At $k = m$ we get $m \le n$ and the stated spanning list. $\blacksquare$ Small example. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ take the independent $\mathbf{u}1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $\mathbf{u}2 = (0, 1, 1)$ and the spanning $\mathbf{e}1, \mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3$. Since $\mathbf{u}1 = \mathbf{e}1 + \mathbf{e}2$ has a nonzero coefficient on $\mathbf{e}1$, swap it out: $\mathbf{u}1, \mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3$ spans. Then $\mathbf{u}2 = 0\,\mathbf{u}1 + \mathbf{e}2 + \mathbf{e}3$; swap out $\mathbf{e}2$: $\mathbf{u}1, \mathbf{u}2, \mathbf{e}3$ spans $\mathbb{R}^3$. (Three vectors that span $\mathbb{R}^3$ are automatically a basis — see the theorem at the end of this chapter.) Dimension is well defined Theorem. In a finite-dimensional vector space, any two bases have the same length. Proof. Let $B1$ and $B2$ be bases, of lengths $m$ and $n$. $B1$ is independent and $B2$ spans, so $m \le n$ by Steinitz. Swap roles: $n \le m$. $\blacksquare$ This common length is the dimension $\dim V$. | Space | Dimension | A basis ||---|---|---|| $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ | 0 | the empty list || $F^n$ | $n$ | $\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n$ || $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ | $n + 1$ | $1, t, \dots, t^n$ || $M{m \times n}(F)$ | $mn$ | matrices with one entry 1, the rest 0 || symmetric $2 \times 2$ | 3 | one basis matrix each for the (1,1), (2,2) and paired off-diagonal entries || $\mathcal{P}(F)$ | infinite | $1, t, t^2, \dots$ (no finite list spans) | > Watch out: $\mathbb{C}^n$ has dimension $n$ over $\mathbb{C}$ but $2n$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (a basis over $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathbf{e}1, i\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n, i\mathbf{e}n$). Dimension depends on the field of scalars. Growing and shrinking to a basis Theorem. Let $V$ be finite-dimensional with $\dim V = n$. 1. Every spanning list can be shrunk to a basis by removing vectors.2. Every independent list can be extended to a basis by adding vectors.3. Any $n$ independent vectors form a basis; any $n$ spanning vectors form a basis.4. Every subspace $U$ is finite-dimensional with $\dim U \le n$, and $\dim U = n$ only if $U = V$. Proof. (1) While the list is dependent, the dependence lemma lets you remove one vector without changing the span. The process stops at an independent spanning list. (2) Apply Steinitz with a basis as the spanning list: the independent list plus the remaining $n - m$ basis vectors spans $V$; it has length $n$, and by (1) some sublist is a basis, which must have length $n$, so it is the whole list. (3) An independent list of length $n$ extends to a basis of length $n$, so nothing is added; a spanning list of length $n$ shrinks to a basis of length $n$, so nothing is removed. (4) Any independent list in $U$ is independent in $V$, so it has length at most $n$ by Steinitz. Keep adding vectors of $U$ not yet in the span of an independent list (independence is kept, by Exercise 7); this stops after at most $n$ steps with a basis of $U$. If $\dim U = n$, that basis is $n$ independent vectors of $V$, so by (3) it spans $V$, hence $U = V$. $\blacksquare$ Chapter 5 — Coordinates If $B = (\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}n)$ is a basis of $V$, each $\mathbf{v}$ has unique weights with $\mathbf{v} = a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + an\mathbf{v}n$. The column $[\mathbf{v}]B = (a1, \dots, an) \in F^n$ is the coordinate vector of $\mathbf{v}$ relative to $B$. The map $\mathbf{v} \mapsto [\mathbf{v}]B$ is one-to-one, onto $F^n$, and respects linear combinations: $[a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w}]B = a[\mathbf{u}]B + b[\mathbf{w}]B$. So every $n$-dimensional space over $F$ behaves exactly like $F^n$, and computations in it can be done with columns and matrices. Linear Algebra 03 makes this precise (isomorphism) and studies how coordinates change when the basis changes. Example in $\mathbb{R}^2$. With $B = ((1, 1), (1, -1))$ and $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1)$: solve $a(1,1) + b(1,-1) = (3, 1)$, so $a + b = 3$, $a - b = 1$, giving $a = 2$, $b = 1$. So $[\mathbf{x}]B = (2, 1)$, while the standard coordinates are $(3, 1)$ — same vector, different description. Example in $\mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R})$. With $B = (1,\ 1 + t,\ (1 + t)^2)$, find $[t^2]B$. Put $s = 1 + t$; then $t = s - 1$ and $t^2 = s^2 - 2s + 1$. So $$t^2 = 1 \cdot (1 + t)^2 - 2(1 + t) + 1 \cdot 1, \qquad [t^2]B = (1, -2, 1)$$ (the weights on $1$, $1 + t$ and $(1 + t)^2$, in that order). Check: $(1 + 2t + t^2) - 2 - 2t + 1 = t^2$. Chapter 6 — The four fundamental subspaces Terminology For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ (the number of pivots): | Subspace | Symbol | Lives in | Definition ||---|---|---|---|| Column space | $C(A)$ | $F^m$ | all $A\mathbf{x}$; span of the columns || Null space | $N(A)$ | $F^n$ | all $\mathbf{x}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ || Row space | $C(A^T)$ | $F^n$ | span of the rows || Left null space | $N(A^T)$ | $F^m$ | all $\mathbf{y}$ with $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$, that is $\mathbf{y}^TA = \mathbf{0}^T$ | Two facts about row operations Let $EA = R$ with $E$ invertible (a product of elementary matrices) and $R$ the RREF. Fact 1 — the row space does not change. Every row of $R$ is a combination of rows of $A$, and since $A = E^{-1}R$, every row of $A$ is a combination of rows of $R$. So $C(A^T) = C(R^T)$. Fact 2 — linear relations among columns do not change. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff EA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff R\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, because $E$ is invertible. So a combination of columns of $A$ is zero exactly when the same combination of columns of $R$ is zero, and $N(A) = N(R)$. (The column spaces of $A$ and $R$ usually differ.) Bases from the RREF - $C(A)$ — the pivot columns of $A$ (not of $R$). In $R$ the pivot columns are distinct standard basis vectors, so they are independent, and every other column is a combination of them. By Fact 2 the same holds for the corresponding columns of $A$.- $C(A^T)$ — the nonzero rows of $R$. They span the row space by Fact 1, and they are independent because each has a leading 1 in a column where the other rows have 0.- $N(A)$ — the $n - r$ special solutions (one free variable 1, the others 0), as in Linear Algebra 00.- $N(A^T)$ — the last $m - r$ rows of $E$: those rows multiply $A$ to give the zero rows of $R$. Worked example Use the matrix from Linear Algebra 00, Chapter 6: $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadR = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad m = 3,\ n = 4,\ r = 2.$$ Pivot columns 1 and 3; free variables $x2, x4$. - Column space (dimension $r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^3$): basis $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(2, 6, 8)$ — columns 1 and 3 of $A$. The other columns satisfy the same relations as in $R$: column 2 $= 2 \times$ column 1, and column 4 $= -2 \times$ column 1 $+ 2 \times$ column 3. Check: $-2(1,2,3) + 2(2,6,8) = (2, 8, 10)$.- Row space (dimension $r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^4$): basis $(1, 2, 0, -2)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$.- Null space (dimension $n - r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^4$): basis $\mathbf{s}1 = (-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $\mathbf{s}2 = (2, 0, -2, 1)$.- Left null space (dimension $m - r = 1$, in $\mathbb{R}^3$): the elimination steps $R2 - 2R1$, $R3 - 3R1$, $R3 - R2$ make row 3 into $-(\text{row } 1) - (\text{row } 2) + (\text{row } 3) = \mathbf{0}$. So $\mathbf{y} = (-1, -1, 1)$ is a basis: $\mathbf{y}^TA = \mathbf{0}^T$. Orthogonality — a preview Each row of $A$ dotted with a null-space vector gives 0 — that is what $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ says, one row at a time. So every vector of $N(A)$ is orthogonal to every vector of $C(A^T)$. In the example, $(1, 2, 0, -2)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$, $(1, 2, 0, -2)\cdot(2, 0, -2, 1) = 0$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(2, 0, -2, 1) = 0$. In the same way $N(A^T)$ is orthogonal to $C(A)$: $(-1, -1, 1)\cdot(1, 2, 3) = 0$ and $(-1, -1, 1)\cdot(2, 6, 8) = 0$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the dimensions add up ($2 + 2 = 4$, $2 + 1 = 3$), which makes each pair orthogonal complements — proved in Linear Algebra 05. Row rank equals column rank Theorem. For every matrix, $\dim C(A) = \dim C(A^T) = r$, the number of pivots. Proof. The pivot columns of $A$ are a basis of $C(A)$, and there are $r$ of them. The nonzero rows of $R$ are a basis of $C(A^T)$, and there are $r$ of them (one per pivot). $\blacksquare$ This is not obvious: for a $3 \times 100$ matrix it says that the 100 columns span a space of the same dimension as the 3 rows. It also shows $\operatorname{rank} A = \operatorname{rank} A^T$, and that $r \le \min(m, n)$. Rank-one matrices. A matrix has rank 1 exactly when it is a nonzero column times a nonzero row, $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$: every column is a multiple of $\mathbf{u}$, every row a multiple of $\mathbf{v}^T$. The outer-product view of Linear Algebra 01 writes any $AB$ as a sum of such pieces. Chapter 7 — Rank–nullity (matrix form) Theorem (rank–nullity). For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, $$\dim C(A) + \dim N(A) = n.$$ Proof. Let $r$ be the number of pivots. $\dim C(A) = r$ by Chapter 6. It remains to show that the $n - r$ special solutions $\mathbf{s}1, \dots, \mathbf{s}{n-r}$ are a basis of $N(A)$. Independent: look only at the free-variable entries. In $\mathbf{s}k$ the $k$-th free variable is 1 and the others are 0. So in $\sum ck\mathbf{s}k$ the $k$-th free entry is $ck$; if the sum is $\mathbf{0}$, every $ck = 0$. Spanning: let $\mathbf{x} \in N(A)$ with free-variable values $f1, \dots, f{n-r}$. Then $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x} - \sum fk\mathbf{s}k$ is in $N(A)$ and has every free variable equal to 0. The RREF expresses each basic variable in terms of the free ones, so all basic variables of $\mathbf{z}$ are 0 too; thus $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \sum fk\mathbf{s}k$. So $\dim N(A) = n - r$, and $r + (n - r) = n$. $\blacksquare$ In words: each of the $n$ columns either contributes a pivot (a new direction in the column space) or a free variable (a new direction in the null space). For the example, $2 + 2 = 4$. Applied to $A^T$ (which has $m$ columns and the same rank), it gives $\dim N(A^T) = m - r$, here $3 - 2 = 1$. The general version — for a linear map $T : V \to W$, $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{im} T + \dim \ker T$ — is proved in Linear Algebra 03. Consequences | Condition on the rank | Meaning for $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ ||---|---|| $r = m$ (full row rank) | solvable for every $\mathbf{b}$; $C(A) = F^m$ || $r = n$ (full column rank) | at most one solution; $N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ || $r = m = n$ | exactly one solution; $A$ invertible || $r < m$ and $r < n$ | either no solution or infinitely many | For a square matrix, "rank $n$", "$N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$" and "$C(A) = F^n$" join the invertible matrix theorem from Linear Algebra 01. > Trick: rank–nullity is a quick sanity check. A $5 \times 7$ matrix has rank at most 5, so its null space has dimension at least $7 - 5 = 2$ — there are nonzero solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ before any computation. Chapter 8 — Sums and direct sums Terminology - Sum of subspaces $U, W \subseteq V$: $U + W = \{\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} : \mathbf{u} \in U, \mathbf{w} \in W\}$. It is the smallest subspace containing both (a subspace containing $U$ and $W$ contains all such sums).- Direct sum — the sum is direct, written $U \oplus W$, if every vector of $U + W$ can be written as $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}$ in only one way. Proposition. $U + W$ is direct if and only if $U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$. Proof. Suppose the sum is direct and $\mathbf{v} \in U \cap W$. Then $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v})$ with $\mathbf{v} \in U$, $-\mathbf{v} \in W$, and also $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}$; uniqueness gives $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. Conversely, if $U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u}' + \mathbf{w}'$, then $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}' = \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w}$ lies in both $U$ and $W$, so it is $\mathbf{0}$. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: for three or more subspaces, pairwise zero intersections are not enough. Three distinct lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ meet pairwise only at $\mathbf{0}$, but their sum is not direct (three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are dependent). The dimension formula Theorem. If $U$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional subspaces of $V$, then $$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W).$$ Proof. Let $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k$ be a basis of $U \cap W$. Extend it to a basis $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}p$ of $U$ and to a basis $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}q$ of $W$ (Chapter 4). The combined list $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}p, \mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}q$ clearly spans $U + W$. For independence, suppose $$\sum ai\mathbf{z}i + \sum bj\mathbf{u}j + \sum cl\mathbf{w}l = \mathbf{0}.$$ Then $\sum cl\mathbf{w}l = -\sum ai\mathbf{z}i - \sum bj\mathbf{u}j$ lies in $W$ and in $U$, hence in $U \cap W$, so $\sum cl\mathbf{w}l = \sum di\mathbf{z}i$ for some $di$. The $\mathbf{z}$'s and $\mathbf{w}$'s together are a basis of $W$, so all $cl = 0$ (and all $di = 0$). Now $\sum ai\mathbf{z}i + \sum bj\mathbf{u}j = \mathbf{0}$ is a relation in a basis of $U$, so all $ai, bj = 0$. Hence the combined list is a basis, and $$\dim(U + W) = k + p + q = (k + p) + (k + q) - k. \qquad \blacksquare$$ This is the same shape as $\lvert A \cup B\rvert = \lvert A\rvert + \lvert B\rvert - \lvert A \cap B\rvert$ for finite sets. Examples - In $\mathbb{R}^3$, let $U = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}1, \mathbf{e}2)$ (the $xy$-plane) and $W = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3)$ (the $yz$-plane). Then $U \cap W = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}2)$ and $\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$, so $U + W = \mathbb{R}^3$, but the sum is not direct.- The plane $P = \{\mathbf{x} : x1 + x2 + x3 = 0\}$ and the line $L = \operatorname{span}(1, 1, 1)$ satisfy $P \cap L = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (a point $(c, c, c)$ with $3c = 0$), so $\dim(P + L) = 2 + 1 = 3$ and $\mathbb{R}^3 = P \oplus L$. For example $(1, 2, 3) = (-1, 0, 1) + (2, 2, 2)$.- Every square matrix splits uniquely into symmetric plus skew-symmetric parts, $M = \tfrac12(M + M^T) + \tfrac12(M - M^T)$, so over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ (any field where $2 \neq 0$), $M{n \times n} = \text{Sym} \oplus \text{Skew}$. For $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ the parts are $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.- For a real $m \times n$ matrix, $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$. The intersection is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, because a vector in both is orthogonal to itself, and $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x} = 0$ forces $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$; the dimensions add to $r + (n - r) = n$. So every $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ splits uniquely as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}r + \mathbf{x}n$, a row-space part plus a null-space part — the left half of the four-subspace picture. Since $A\mathbf{x}n = \mathbf{0}$, $A\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{x}r$: every $\mathbf{b}$ in $C(A)$ is $A\mathbf{x}r$ for a row-space vector $\mathbf{x}r$, which is the arrow from row space to column space in the figure of Chapter 6.
