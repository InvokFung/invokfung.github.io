Piano Notes — 01 · Grade 1 Theory & Repertoire
Created2026-05-16|Music
ABRSM Grade 1 covers the foundations every musician needs: clefs, note values, simple time signatures, the first few keys, basic intervals, and the very first scales. This post pairs the syllabus with practice pieces graded at the same level, plus the memory tricks I wish someone had handed me on day one. > Series map: 00 Beginner Basics → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 to follow. Learning Outcome - Read fluently in treble and bass clef on the five staff lines, four spaces, and one ledger line above or below.- Identify all note values from semibreve to quaver, and matching rests.- Use 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 time signatures correctly.- Name notes in C, G, F majors and their key signatures.- Build major scales for C, G, F (one octave, hands separate).- Identify intervals between any two notes of the C major scale (number only).- Recognise the tonic, dominant, subdominant of those three keys.- Play 3 to 5 short pieces hands together at Grade 1 standard. Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap Terminology - Pitch (音高) — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.- Accidental — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.- Sharp (♯) — raise the pitch by one semitone.- Flat (♭) — lower the pitch by one semitone.- Natural (♮) — cancel a previous sharp or flat. An accidental affects every occurrence of that note in the same octave for the rest of the bar — then the barline resets it. A key signature is the only "global" accidental. Ledger lines (加線) Grade 1 requires one ledger above and one below in each clef. - Treble: one line above the staff = A5, one line below the staff = Middle C (C4).- Bass: one line above the staff = Middle C (C4) as well (the same note), one line below the staff = E2. > Trick: Middle C is the connector. It sits one ledger below the treble staff AND one ledger above the bass staff. Same key on the piano, two ways to write it. Drill this until it's automatic — Grade 2 and beyond use it constantly. Chapter 2 — Note Values (音符的時值) English / 中文 / symbol table | EN | 中文 | UK name | Beats in 4/4 ||----|------|---------|--------------|| Whole note | 全音符 | Semibreve | 4 || Half note | 二分音符 | Minim | 2 || Quarter note | 四分音符 | Crotchet | 1 || Eighth note | 八分音符 | Quaver | ½ | ABRSM uses the UK terms (semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver). American sheet music uses whole/half/quarter/eighth. Same notes, different names. Get used to both. Rests Every note value has a matching rest. The same beat counts apply — a crotchet rest is 1 beat of silence in 4/4. > Trick: I confused the whole-note rest (hangs down from a line) and the half-note rest (sits on top of a line). Memory aid: "the whole rest is a hole — it falls down. The half is half full — it sits up." Silly, never forgot it again. Dots and ties - A dot after a note adds half its value. Dotted minim = 2 + 1 = 3 beats.- A tie joins two notes of the same pitch — sum their durations.- A slur joins two notes of different pitches — phrasing, not duration. Chapter 3 — Time Signatures (拍號) Grade 1 covers 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 only. The top number = beats per bar. The bottom number = which note value gets one beat (4 = crotchet). | Time signature | Beats per bar | Feel ||----------------|---------------|------|| 2/4 | 2 | March (left-right-left-right) || 3/4 | 3 | Waltz (ONE-two-three) || 4/4 | 4 | Pop, hymn, default (ONE-two-three-four) | The first beat of every bar is the strongest. Place natural emphasis there; the bar lines exist precisely to tell you where to push slightly. Bar (小節) maths The notes in any bar must add up to the time signature's beat count. Silent counting is for performance, not learning. Chapter 4 — Keys and Key Signatures Grade 1 syllabus: C major, G major, F major. | Key | Sharps/Flats | Memory anchor ||-----|--------------|---------------|| C major | none | Easy default || G major | 1 sharp: F♯ | "Goes up" → sharp || F major | 1 flat: B♭ | "Falls down" → flat | Scale construction reminder Major scale pattern in semitones: W W H W W W H (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half). Apply from any tonic and you get a major scale. From C: all whites. From G: hits F♯ as the 7th. From F: hits B♭ as the 4th. > Trick: the major-scale pattern doesn't change — only the starting note does. If you can derive C major from scratch, you can derive every major scale by counting W W H W W W H with your finger on the keyboard. Don't memorise 12 scales; memorise one pattern and 12 starting points. Tonic, Dominant, Subdominant For any major scale: - Tonic (I) — the 1st degree. Home note. C in C major.- Subdominant (IV) — the 4th degree. F in C major.- Dominant (V) — the 5th degree. G in C major. These three notes form the primary triads of the key, the most important chords in any major piece. Half of pop and folk music is built from just I, IV, V. > Where I got stuck: I assumed "dominant" meant "loudest" or "main". It actually means the 5th, always. The label is positional, not musical. Once I stopped overthinking it, every scale fell into place. Chapter 5 — Intervals (Number Only) At Grade 1 you only need to identify the number of an interval (2nd, 3rd, etc.), not its quality (major / minor / perfect). Quality starts at Grade 2. Counting Count the lower note as 1, then count up the staff lines and spaces to the upper note. The count includes both notes. | Interval | Staff positions ||----------|-----------------|| Unison | Same line/space || 2nd | Adjacent line + space || 3rd | Line to next line, or space to next space || 4th | Line to space-after-the-next-line || 5th | Line to second line above || 6th | Space to third line above, or line to third space above || 7th | Line to third line above, or space to third space above (one short of an octave) || Octave (8th) | Same letter, seven steps up (line to space, or space to line) | > Trick: 3rds and 5ths "jump white" — they go line-to-line or space-to-space, looking visually stacked. 2nds and 4ths "step black" — one note sits on a line, the other in a space. Once your eye reads these shapes, you stop counting notes one-by-one. Chapter 6 — Hands-Together Coordination You played hands together in Post 00 already with "Twinkle". Grade 1 expands this. The technique: 1. Each hand alone, played until boring. Genuinely boring — no mistakes, no thinking.2. Hands together at half speed. Eyes on the page, not the hands.3. Pulse first, accuracy second. If one hand stumbles, don't stop the pulse. Drop the wrong-hand note rather than break the beat.4. Slow with metronome. Crotchet = 60. Once smooth, raise by 5 BPM increments. > Where I got stuck: my left hand was 6 weeks behind my right. The fix: I practised only the left hand for one full week. No right hand at all. Result: by the next week my left could play its own line cleanly, and hands-together suddenly worked. Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 1 ABRSM practical includes an aural test. The skills: - Clap back a rhythm — listen to a 2- or 3-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4; clap it back.- Sing back a short phrase — three or four notes, played twice.- Identify time signature — listen to a tune; say whether it's in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4. Practising aural without a teacher - Phone metronome + clap for rhythms. Tap a 4-bar pattern on the table, then clap it back without looking.- Sing scale degrees — call out "1, 2, 3" up the major scale while playing it. Builds the do-re-mi reflex.- Listen to any piece and tap your foot. If your tapping settles into pairs, it's 2/4 or 4/4. If into threes, it's 3/4. Public music is your unlimited test bank. > Trick: whistling is sneaky-good for aural training. If you can whistle the tune you've been practising, you've internalised the pitch shape. If you can only "sort of" whistle it, you haven't. The whistle is a free pitch test. AI Prompting (Foundations & Practice)
Created2026-05-15|AI
Prompting is the practice of writing inputs that steer a Large Language Model (LLM) toward useful outputs. It is part interface design, part rhetoric, and part empirical science. This note covers the foundations and the techniques that have been validated by published research, with worked examples you can copy. Learning Outcome - Explain what a prompt is and why model behaviour is sensitive to its phrasing.- Decompose a prompt into its functional parts (instruction, context, examples, format spec).- Apply core techniques: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role conditioning, decomposition, and self-consistency.- Recognise common anti-patterns and explain why they fail.- Design an evaluation harness with a golden set + rubric + A/B framework.- Read a research paper on prompting and judge whether its claims will transfer to your task. Chapter 1 - Foundations Terminology - Prompt — the full token sequence the model conditions on before generating the next token.- Completion — the model's generated response.- Token — sub-word unit (4 characters of English on average). All limits (context window, throughput, billing) are measured in tokens.- Context window — maximum prompt+completion length the model can attend to.- In-context learning (ICL) — model adapts its behaviour from examples in the prompt without weight updates. Reported in Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, Brown et al. (2020).- Temperature — sampling parameter; $0$ is greedy, higher values increase diversity.- Top-p / Top-k — nucleus / top-k sampling, alternatives to temperature.- System / developer message — privileged prompt segment used to set persistent behaviour. Mechanism formalised by The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Why prompts matter An LLM is a next-token predictor over a learned distribution $P(tn \mid t1, \dots, t{n-1})$. The prompt is the conditional sequence $t1, \dots, t{n-1}$. Two prompts that read as semantically equivalent to a human can produce very different distributions because the model is sensitive to token-level cues it has learned to associate with answer quality (formality, code-block fences, "Let's think step by step", etc.). This sensitivity has been measured empirically. Lu et al. (2022) showed that simply reordering few-shot examples can swing accuracy on classification benchmarks by tens of percentage points. Sclar et al. (2024) showed that benign formatting changes (newline vs. colon as separator) shift task accuracy by a comparable margin. The practical consequence: prompts are programs. They deserve version control, testing, and review. How the model "reads" a prompt A typical chat-tuned model receives a structured sequence — for instance OpenAI's ChatML or Anthropic's / turn markers — where each turn is tagged with a role. The model has been fine-tuned (instruction-tuned + RLHF or DPO) to: 1. Treat the system / developer message as a high-priority constraint.2. Treat user messages as questions or requests.3. Treat its own prior responses as commitments to maintain consistency with. The training procedure was first detailed in InstructGPT, Ouyang et al. (2022) and the Anthropic RLHF paper, Bai et al. (2022). The hierarchy of trust between roles was later sharpened in The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Chapter 2 - Anatomy of a Prompt A well-formed prompt contains, in some combination, six functional parts. Naming them helps you debug what is missing. | Part | Purpose | Example || --- | --- | --- || Role / persona | Set a frame the model uses to choose vocabulary, depth, formality | "You are a senior database administrator." || Instruction | The actual verb of the request | "Explain why this query is slow." || Context | Domain facts, retrieved documents, prior turns | The SQL query + output || Examples (shots) | Demonstrations of input → output | A prior worked optimisation || Format spec | Constraint on output shape | "Return JSON matching schema X" || Stop / guardrails | Negative constraints, refusal triggers | "If the query is fine, say only." | A minimal template The order matters less than the presence of each part. The OpenAI prompt-engineering guide (OpenAI 2024) and Anthropic's prompt library both converge on the same six-part skeleton. Chapter 3 - Core Techniques Zero-shot Ask the question directly with no examples. Works when the task is well-represented in the training distribution (translation, summarisation, common QA). Few-shot (in-context learning) Provide $k$ input/output pairs, then the new input. Brown et al. (2020) showed few-shot prompting closes a large fraction of the gap to fine-tuning on many tasks without any gradient updates. Tips: - Order examples easy → hard (Lu et al., 2022).- Make examples cover the failure modes you observe in zero-shot, not the easy cases.- Keep formatting identical across shots; a stray newline can degrade accuracy (Sclar et al., 2024). Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting Ask the model to show its work before committing to an answer. Reported in Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, Wei et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning on large models. Two variants worth knowing: - Zero-shot CoT: append "Let's think step by step." Kojima et al. (2022). Cheap and surprisingly effective.- Self-consistency: sample $n$ chains at temperature $>0$, take the majority vote on the final answer. Wang et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic accuracy further at $n \times$ cost. Role / persona conditioning Setting an expert persona biases vocabulary, depth, and inferred audience. The effect is real but smaller than CoT or few-shot; treat it as fine-tuning of style, not capability. Decomposition Break a hard problem into ordered sub-problems and prompt each separately, chaining outputs. Equivalent to Khot et al. (2023) "Decomposed Prompting" or least-to-most prompting by Zhou et al. (2023). The chain is easier to debug than a single monolithic prompt; you can swap or rerun any step. ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) Interleave reasoning steps with tool calls. Yao et al. (2023). The model alternates between (a reasoning step), (a tool call), and (tool result), until ready to emit an . ReAct is the foundation pattern under most "agent" frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex, modern function-calling SDKs). Chapter 4 - Structural Patterns Delimiters Use unambiguous markers around user-supplied or retrieved content. Three formats all work, in roughly equal measure: ... XML tags have the advantage of being self-documenting and survive whitespace normalisation. Anthropic explicitly recommends XML in their prompt-engineering documentation. Markdown structure Models trained on heavy Markdown corpora respond well to headings (), lists, and tables. Use the same conventions in your prompt that you want to see in the output. Numbered constraints Numbered constraints are easier for the model to enumerate against than a paragraph of "and also, make sure to..." Format specifications Three escalating strengths: | Strength | Mechanism | Reliability || --- | --- | --- || Soft | Natural-language description ("return JSON") | 60-80% || Schema | Provide explicit schema (TypeScript / JSON Schema / Pydantic) | 90% || Hard | Use the provider's structured-output / JSON-mode API | 100% (provider-enforced) | If you need a guaranteed shape, use the provider's structured output. Soft format is convenient but will fail on tail cases. Chapter 5 - Reasoning Boosters Self-consistency Already mentioned. Formally: sample $n$ chains $c1, \dots, cn$ at $T > 0$, extract the final answer $ai$ from each, return $\arg\maxa \sumi \mathbb{1}[ai = a]$. The probability that the majority answer is correct converges to the per-sample accuracy raised to a Condorcet-style power as $n$ grows, when individual samples are conditionally independent given the question. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) Yao et al. (2023). Generalises CoT from a chain to a search tree. The model proposes multiple next-thoughts at each step, a value model scores each, and a search algorithm (BFS/DFS) explores. Helps on combinatorial problems (Game of 24, crosswords) where a single chain commits too early. Reflexion Shinn et al. (2023). After a wrong attempt, the model writes a critique of its own trace; that critique is appended to context for the next attempt. The agent improves across attempts within a single session. Step-Back prompting Zheng et al. (2024). Before answering a specific question, ask the model to first derive the general principle that governs the question. The principle then conditions the specific answer. Improves abstract / multi-step reasoning. Chapter 6 - Common Anti-patterns Vague verbs , , , are unmeasurable. The model has to guess a direction. Replace with a measurable verb: , , . Conflicting constraints The model resolves contradictions arbitrarily. Pick one side of each axis and state it. Leading questions The framing forces the model to defend a premise instead of evaluating it. Prefer: Long irrelevant context Liu et al. (2023) Lost in the Middle showed models degrade on retrieval tasks when relevant information sits in the middle of a long context. Prune aggressively. If you must include long retrieved documents, put the most relevant snippets at the start or the end. Negation-only instructions is weaker than Models follow positive constraints more reliably than negative ones. "Polite filler" adds tokens without affecting behaviour. Strip it. Chapter 7 - Evaluation A prompt is a piece of software, so evaluate it like software. Golden set A small, curated set of representative inputs with hand-graded expected outputs. 30–100 items is enough to detect a 5pp accuracy shift. Rubric Decompose "is the output good?" into yes/no axes. For a summarisation task you might score: - Factual (no hallucinated entities)- Complete (all named main points present)- Concise (within length limit)- Format (parses as JSON)- Refusal (correctly refused when no claim present) Each axis scored 0/1 by a human grader or a separate "judge" model. LLM-as-judge Zheng et al. (2023). A stronger model grades a weaker model's output against a rubric. Cheap, scales, but biased toward outputs that look like the judge's style. Use a different family of model as the judge where possible, and spot-check 10% with a human. A/B framework Track $\Delta$ per axis, not just overall. A change that improves factuality but hurts conciseness is still a regression on the conciseness axis. Statistical sanity With $n=100$ binary outcomes, the 95% confidence interval on accuracy is roughly $\pm 10$pp around the observed rate. Differences smaller than that are noise. Increase $n$ or repeat the run to tighten. Chapter 8 - Tool Use and Structured Output Modern model providers expose two APIs that should replace most prompt-engineering hacks: Function calling / tool use You declare a JSON schema for one or more tools; the model picks one and emits arguments matching the schema. The runtime executes the tool and returns the result, which you append to the conversation and prompt again. The model is fine-tuned to know when to call a tool versus answer directly. This is more reliable than ReAct in raw text because the model emits parsed JSON via the API. Structured output (JSON / Pydantic mode) You supply the full output schema; the provider's decoder constrains generation so the response is guaranteed valid against the schema. Eliminates "the model added a friendly preamble that broke my parser" failures. When NOT to use tools If the task is pure transformation of text the model already has the knowledge for (summarise, translate, classify), tools add latency and cost. Use tools when you need fresh data, deterministic computation, or side effects. Chapter 9 - Putting It Together A production prompt for "extract action items from a meeting transcript" — combining roles, delimiters, few-shot, format, and a guardrail: This prompt is testable (you have a golden set), debuggable (each rule is a single axis you can score), and safe (the negative examples teach refusal on vague language). Piano Notes — 00 · Beginner Basics & Your First Songs
Created2026-05-15|Music
The first post in a self-study piano series. Goal: by the end of this one you can sit at any keyboard, find any note in under five seconds, and play three short pieces from memory. Skip the marketing fluff and go straight to the muscle memory. > Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 will follow. Learning Outcome - Sit comfortably at the keyboard with healthy posture.- Name every white key on sight — both directions, both clefs.- Find Middle C on any size piano without thinking.- Play three short pieces with both hands.- Recognise the difference between a piano, an upright, a digital, and a synth — and pick the right one for practice. Chapter 1 — Why piano is a good first instrument The keyboard is the clearest visual map of Western music. Every other instrument hides pitch behind a fingering chart; the piano lays the entire pitch space out in front of you, left-to-right, low-to-high. If you understand "how a scale looks" on the piano, you understand it on a guitar, a violin, or in a software synth — you just don't see it as plainly. A second practical reason: the piano is monophonically simple but polyphonically rich. Beginners can produce a clean tone from day one (press → sound), while advanced players spend a lifetime on the same instrument. Few instruments span that range. > Where I started: I wasted my first two weeks googling "best digital piano under $500" instead of practising. The truth is any 88-key weighted keyboard from a major brand (Yamaha, Roland, Casio, Kawai) is fine for the first year. The piano you have and play every day beats the piano you research and never own. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Acoustic (real) piano - Upright (直立式) — most homes. Violin Notes — 08 · Grade 8 Virtuoso Bowing, All Double Stops, Performance Polish
Created2026-05-09|Music
Grade 8 ABRSM violin is the diploma threshold. All major and minor scales in three or four octaves, double stops in 3rds / 6ths / 8ves / 10ths, the full vocabulary of bow techniques (martelé, ricochet, sautillé, flying staccato, col legno, jeté), and concerto / sonata repertoire approaching professional standard. Past Grade 8 lies DipABRSM and onward. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8 (this post — final in this series). Learning Outcome - Play all major and minor three-octave scales + arpeggios fluently.- Play scales in 3rds, 6ths, octaves, and 10ths in all common keys.- Use every bow technique in repertoire.- Apply expressive vibrato (variable speed + width) for musical effect.- Perform a complete concerto movement of significant length (8–15 minutes) from memory.- Read and interpret 20th-century notation (extended techniques, irregular metres).- Demonstrate stylistic understanding: Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern. Chapter 1 — The full scale + double-stop arsenal Three-octave scales: every key By Grade 8 you must own all 12 major and all 12 minor (× 2 = harmonic + melodic) scales — three octaves each. Practice rotation (4-week cycle) | Week | Major keys | Minor keys ||------|------------|------------|| 1 | C, G, D, A, E | A, E, B, F♯, C♯ || 2 | B, F♯, C♯, D♭, A♭ | G♯, D♯, A♯, B♭, F || 3 | E♭, B♭, F | C, G, D || 4 | Review weakest 4 keys | Review weakest 4 keys | 15 minutes scales per day, rotating focus across the cycle. Double stops at Grade 8 | Interval | Difficulty | Key example ||----------|------------|--------------|| 3rds | Moderate (Grade 6+) | G, D, A major || 6ths | Moderate | G, D, A major || Octaves | Hard | G, D major || 10ths | Very hard | Selected keys, single octave || Fingered octaves | Very hard | Heifetz scale system | 10ths require an enormous left-hand stretch (1st + 4th finger on adjacent strings, an octave and a third apart). Most adult learners take 1–2 years to play 10ths reliably. > Trick: don't force 10ths into pieces if they're not in the syllabus. Practise as a stretch exercise (open-palm yoga for the violin hand). When the syllabus demands them, you'll already have the reach. Chapter 2 — Virtuoso bowing toolkit By Grade 8 every technique is in active use. Quick reference: | Bowing | Description | Typical use ||--------|-------------|--------------|| Détaché | Smooth separate strokes | Most Classical/Romantic passages || Legato / slur | Connected, one bow | Lyrical lines || Staccato | Sharp short, on string | Articulated passages || Slurred staccato | Multiple stops, one direction | Long lines with articulation || Spiccato | Bow lifts between notes | Light, dancing passages || Sautillé | Very fast spiccato | Fast 16th-note passages || Ricochet | Multiple bounces per drop | Showpieces, cadenzas || Martelé | Hammered, percussive | Accents, dramatic openings || Flying staccato | Lifts each note, one direction | Showpieces || Col legno battuto | Tap with the bow stick | Modern repertoire, Mars (Holst) || Col legno tratto | Draw with the wood | Eerie effects, rare || Sul ponticello | Near the bridge | Glassy colour || Sul tasto | Over the fingerboard | Soft, dreamy colour || Pizzicato | Plucked | Common; Bartók pizz snaps string against fingerboard || Jeté | "Thrown" — multiple notes from a single bounce-then-draw | Showpieces || Tremolo | Rapid bow oscillation on one note | Suspense, orchestral effect | A piece using nearly all of them Sarasate Zigeunerweisen uses détaché, legato, spiccato, sautillé, ricochet, harmonics, pizzicato, and flying staccato in one 9-minute work. Reference repertoire for technique synthesis. Chapter 3 — Vibrato as expression By Grade 8, vibrato is not "on" or "off" — it's a continuous parameter. The four vibrato variables 1. Width — narrow (subtle warmth) vs wide (passionate Romantic).2. Speed — slow (sustained nobility) vs fast (intense, virtuoso fire).3. Continuous vs delayed — start the vibrato instantly, or let the note begin straight then add vibrato.4. Pulse — strict steady vibrato vs phrased vibrato (slows and speeds with the phrase). Vibrato style per period | Period | Vibrato preference ||--------|---------------------|| Baroque | Sparse, narrow, often as ornament (varies by historical performance practice) || Classical | Selective; on long notes mostly || Romantic | Continuous, wide, warm || 20th-c / Modern | Variable; sometimes deliberately senza vibrato || Folk / world | Narrow, fast, intermittent | > Trick: listen to one violinist play the same piece from different periods (e.g., Hilary Hahn plays both Bach and Tchaikovsky). Notice how her vibrato choice changes. Internalise the period-appropriate sound by daily listening. Chapter 4 — 20th-century notation Grade 8 introduces: Irregular metres 5/4, 7/8, 11/16. Count subdivisions: Listen: Holst Mars (5/4), Stravinsky Rite of Spring (constantly shifting metres). Extended techniques (notation symbols) | Symbol | Technique ||--------|-----------|| Diamond note | Natural or artificial harmonic || Open circle above note | Natural harmonic || "+" above note | Left-hand pizzicato (pluck with left finger) || "Bartók pizz" symbol | Snap pluck (string against fingerboard) || Wavy line above note | Trill || ↑ ↓ arrows | Glissando direction || Box around staff | Aleatoric (player chooses) | Atonal pitch language Some Grade 8 repertoire abandons traditional key signatures. Read by intervals, not by key. Practise by sight-reading short atonal exercises (Bartók Mikrokosmos arranged for violin, etc.). Chapter 5 — Suggested Performance Repertoire Concerto choices (Grade 8 level — pick one to focus 6 months) | Concerto | Composer | Style ||----------|----------|-------|| Mozart Concerto No. 3, 4, or 5 | Mozart | Classical balance || Bruch Concerto No. 1 in G minor | Bruch | Romantic favourite || Saint-Saëns Concerto No. 3 in B minor | Saint-Saëns | French Romantic || Mendelssohn Concerto in E minor | Mendelssohn | The "standard" Romantic || Lalo Symphonie Espagnole | Lalo | Spanish-flavoured concerto | Sonata choices | Sonata | Composer | Style ||--------|----------|-------|| Bach Sonatas Nos. 1–3 (unaccompanied) | Bach | Baroque solo || Beethoven Spring Sonata Op. 24 | Beethoven | Classical/early Romantic || Brahms Sonatas 1, 2, 3 | Brahms | German Romantic || Franck Sonata in A | Franck | Late Romantic French | Showpieces (encore-level virtuoso) | Piece | Composer | What it tests ||-------|----------|---------------|| Zigeunerweisen | Sarasate | Every bowing technique || Tzigane | Ravel | Harmonics, modal language || Praeludium and Allegro | Kreisler | Pseudo-Baroque style || Caprices (selected) | Paganini | Each caprice = one virtuoso problem || Méditation from Thaïs | Massenet | Lyrical depth + vibrato | Études at Grade 8 - Rode 24 Caprices.- Dont Op. 35 — major reference.- Paganini 24 Caprices — start Nos. 5, 9, 13.- Wieniawski L'École Moderne Op. 10. Chapter 6 — Performance polish Grade 8 exams test interpretation, not just technique. Programme planning (typical Grade 8 recital) | Slot | Choice | Length ||------|--------|--------|| 1 | Baroque (Bach, Handel) | 5–8 min || 2 | Classical / early Romantic (Mozart, Beethoven) | 8–12 min || 3 | Late Romantic / Modern (Brahms, Ravel, Bartók) | 5–10 min || 4 | Virtuoso showpiece | 4–8 min | Variety across periods is graded. Stage presentation - Tuning ritual: walk onstage, tune visibly, take a breath, begin.- Eye contact with pianist (if accompanied) before nodding to start.- Bow direction visible to audience.- Recovery: if you make a mistake, don't react. Continue. Audience usually misses small errors. Stage anxiety management - Daily mock performance for 30 days before exam. Play full programme top-to-bottom every day.- Performance-day routine: same warm-up, same meal timing, same clothing dress rehearsal.- Breathing: 4 seconds in, 6 seconds out, before walking onstage. Lowers heart rate.- Visualisation: mentally walk through the recital from arrival to bow, daily for 2 weeks before exam. > Where I got stuck: I prepared technically but didn't rehearse the experience of performing. First exam: hands shook on the bow. Fix: 4 weeks of daily mock recitals — in front of family, friends, even an empty room with a recording running. By exam day, the room was less intimidating because I'd "performed" 30 times already. Chapter 7 — Stylistic understanding Grade 8 exams ask: Why is this Baroque ornament played this way? You should know: Baroque (1600–1750) - Trills start on upper note, on the beat.- Less vibrato; vibrato as occasional ornament.- Strong terraced dynamics (sudden f to p, not gradual).- Rubato sparingly; mostly steady tempo.- Détaché with subtle weight changes for phrase shape. Classical (1750–1820) - Balanced phrases, clear structure.- Vibrato on long notes only.- Dynamic gradients (cresc, dim) more frequent.- Détaché with elegance; spiccato light. Romantic (1820–1900) - Vibrato continuous.- Wide dynamic range; rubato substantial.- Long lyrical lines.- Full use of register; expressive shifts welcomed. Modern (1900+) - Technique-heavy.- May use extended techniques.- Senza vibrato on certain passages.- Irregular metres + atonal pitch language possible. How to internalise style Listen to one piece per period weekly, with score in hand. Mark performance choices (vibrato, rubato, bowing). Compare across recordings. Over a year, style sensitivity becomes second nature. Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 8 aural is extensive: - Sing/play 8-bar phrases with modulations and chromatic notes after 1–2 hearings.- Identify period + style at sight/sound.- Identify formal structure (sonata-allegro, rondo, theme + variations).- Identify modulations to distant + enharmonic keys.- Identify orchestral textures (homophonic, polyphonic, monophonic).- Sight-sing complex 8-bar phrases with modulation + chromaticism. Daily preparation 15 minutes ear training daily for 3 months before exam. Use Tonal Energy, Functional Ear Trainer, or Teoria.com drills. Mix:- Intervals (target: 1 sec recognition)- Chord qualities (target: 2 sec recognition)- Modulation type (target: 5 sec recognition)- Period style (target: 10 sec from short clip) Chapter 9 — Beyond Grade 8: the diploma path After Grade 8, the ABRSM diploma path: | Diploma | Level | Requirements ||---------|-------|--------------|| ARSM | Performance diploma | 30-min recital, professional standard || DipABRSM | Performance / Teaching | 35-min recital + viva / pedagogy paper || LRSM | Licentiate | 50-min recital + extensive theory || FRSM | Fellowship | Concert-artist level | Most adult learners stop at Grade 8 or ARSM. The leap to DipABRSM and beyond is substantial — concerto level reaches Tchaikovsky / Brahms / Sibelius standards, with full memorisation and stylistic mastery. Closing — the journey from Grade 1 Looking back across this series: - Grades 1–2: getting the hands to work at all.- Grades 3–4: first shift, first concerto, first real expression.- Grade 5: the bottleneck (vibrato + theory paper).- Grades 6–7: double stops, advanced bowings, full repertoire.- Grade 8: synthesis — every technique in service of musical interpretation. Many adult learners find Grade 8 is where the violin finally feels like a voice rather than a project. > Final trick: keep listening. Daily, lifelong. Every great violinist I admire spends more time listening than playing. Hearing shapes the hand. Hearing shapes the bow. Hearing makes you a musician — not just a violinist.
