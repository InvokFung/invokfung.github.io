Abstract Algebra — 00 · Integers, Relations and Modular Arithmetic
Created2025-11-05|Mathematics Abstract Algebra
Abstract algebra studies structures: a set together with one or more operations that obey a short list of rules. Instead of asking "what is $7 \cdot 13$?", it asks "which facts about multiplication follow from the rules alone, so that they hold for integers, for clock arithmetic, for matrices, for symmetries of a molecule, all at once?". That shift pays off in many places: symmetry (crystallography, chemistry, physics), cryptography (RSA and elliptic-curve systems are group theory in disguise), coding theory (error-correcting codes on every phone and disk are built over finite fields), and the classical question of solving polynomial equations, which Galois settled with groups. This series follows the standard senior undergraduate path of Dummit & Foote, Gallian, Artin and Fraleigh: groups, then rings and fields, then Galois theory. Post 00 lays the ground: the proof methods used throughout, sets and functions, equivalence relations, and the arithmetic of the integers up to modular arithmetic, the Chinese remainder theorem and a small but complete RSA example. Every later post uses $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as its first examples. > Series map: 00 Integers, Relations & Modular Arithmetic (this post) → 01 Groups & Subgroups → 02 Cosets, Lagrange & Quotient Groups → 03 Homomorphisms, Group Actions & Sylow → 04 Rings, Ideals & Fields → 05 Field Extensions & Galois Theory. Learning Outcome - Read and write the four basic proof patterns: direct, contrapositive, contradiction, induction.- Decide whether a function is injective, surjective or bijective, and use the fact that bijections are exactly the invertible functions.- Prove that an equivalence relation on a set is the same thing as a partition of that set.- State and prove the division algorithm from the well-ordering principle.- Run the Euclidean algorithm and the extended Euclidean algorithm by hand, and prove Bézout's identity and Euclid's lemma.- Prove the fundamental theorem of arithmetic (existence and uniqueness).- Compute in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, find inverses, and decide which classes are units.- Use Euler's $\varphi$ function, Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem to reduce huge powers.- Solve simultaneous congruences with the Chinese remainder theorem.- Carry out a toy RSA key generation, encryption and decryption, and explain why decryption works. Chapter 1 — The proof toolkit Abstract algebra is a proof-based subject. Almost every statement is an implication "if $P$ then $Q$", and there are four standard ways to prove one. Terminology - Proposition / theorem / lemma / corollary — a proved statement; a lemma is a helper, a corollary follows quickly from a theorem.- Definition — fixes the meaning of a word. Definitions are "if and only if" even when written with "if".- Contrapositive of "$P \Rightarrow Q$" is "not $Q \Rightarrow$ not $P$". It is logically equivalent to the original.- Converse of "$P \Rightarrow Q$" is "$Q \Rightarrow P$". It is a different statement and may be false.- Counterexample — one concrete case that shows a universal statement is false. Direct proof Assume $P$, reason forward, reach $Q$. Claim. The sum of two odd integers is even. Proof. Odd integers can be written $2a+1$ and $2b+1$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$. Their sum is $2a + 2b + 2 = 2(a+b+1)$, which is even. $\blacksquare$ Proof by contrapositive Prove "not $Q \Rightarrow$ not $P$" instead. Claim. If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even. Proof. Contrapositive: if $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd. Write $n = 2k+1$; then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2+2k) + 1$, which is odd. $\blacksquare$ Proof by contradiction Assume the statement is false and derive something impossible. Claim. $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. Proof. Suppose $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ with integers $p, q$, $q \neq 0$, and the fraction in lowest terms. Then $p^2 = 2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, so $p$ is even by the previous claim. Write $p = 2m$: then $4m^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2m^2$ is even and $q$ is even. Both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting lowest terms. $\blacksquare$ Proof by induction To prove a statement $S(n)$ for all integers $n \geq n0$: prove $S(n0)$ (the base case), then prove $S(k) \Rightarrow S(k+1)$ for every $k \geq n0$ (the inductive step). In strong induction the inductive step may assume $S(n0), \dots, S(k)$ all at once. Claim. For every $n \geq 1$, $1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n-1) = n^2$. Proof. Base case $n=1$: $1 = 1^2$. Inductive step: if the sum of the first $k$ odd numbers is $k^2$, adding the next one gives $k^2 + (2k+1) = (k+1)^2$. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: proving the converse is not proving the statement. "If $n$ is even then $n^2$ is even" is true, but it does not prove "if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even"; that needed its own proof above. Chapter 2 — Sets, functions and equivalence relations Terminology - $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ (some books start at 1; this series includes 0), $\mathbb{Z}$ the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ the rationals, $\mathbb{R}$ the reals, $\mathbb{C}$ the complex numbers.- $A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A, b \in B\}$ is the Cartesian product.- A function $f : A \to B$ assigns to each $a \in A$ exactly one $f(a) \in B$. $A$ is the domain, $B$ the codomain, $f(A)$ the image.- $f$ is injective (one-to-one) if $f(a1) = f(a2)$ implies $a1 = a2$.- $f$ is surjective (onto) if every $b \in B$ equals $f(a)$ for some $a$.- $f$ is bijective if it is both. A bijection from a set to itself is a permutation of the set.- A relation on $A$ is a subset $R \subseteq A \times A$; write $a \sim b$ when $(a, b) \in R$. Functions and inverses Examples on $\mathbb{Z}$: $f(x) = 2x$ is injective but not surjective (odd numbers are missed). $g(x) = \lfloor x/2 \rfloor$ is surjective but not injective ($g(0) = g(1) = 0$). $h(x) = x + 5$ is bijective with inverse $x \mapsto x - 5$. Proposition 2.1. A function $f : A \to B$ has an inverse function $g : B \to A$ (with $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}A$ and $f \circ g = \mathrm{id}B$) if and only if $f$ is bijective. Proof. If $g$ exists: $f(a1) = f(a2)$ gives $a1 = g(f(a1)) = g(f(a2)) = a2$, so $f$ is injective; and any $b$ equals $f(g(b))$, so $f$ is surjective. Conversely, if $f$ is bijective, each $b$ has exactly one preimage; define $g(b)$ to be it. Then both compositions are identities. $\blacksquare$ Proposition 2.2 (pigeonhole). If $A$ is a finite set and $f : A \to A$ is injective, then $f$ is bijective. Proof. An injective $f$ sends the $\lvert A\rvert$ elements of $A$ to $\lvert A\rvert$ distinct elements of $A$, so the image is all of $A$. $\blacksquare$ This small fact drives the proofs of Fermat's little theorem below and of Cayley's theorem later in the series. It is false for infinite sets: $x \mapsto 2x$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is injective but not surjective. Equivalence relations A relation $\sim$ on $A$ is an equivalence relation if it is - reflexive: $a \sim a$ for all $a$;- symmetric: $a \sim b$ implies $b \sim a$;- transitive: $a \sim b$ and $b \sim c$ imply $a \sim c$. The equivalence class of $a$ is $[a] = \{x \in A : x \sim a\}$. Examples: equality on any set; "same birthday" on people; "$a - b$ is divisible by $n$" on $\mathbb{Z}$ (Chapter 5); "same determinant" on $2 \times 2$ matrices. Non-examples: $\leq$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is reflexive and transitive but not symmetric; "$\lvert a - b\rvert \leq 1$" on $\mathbb{R}$ is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive ($0 \sim 1$, $1 \sim 2$, but $0 \not\sim 2$). A partition of $A$ is a collection of non-empty subsets of $A$ such that every element of $A$ lies in exactly one of them. Theorem 2.3 (equivalence relations = partitions). If $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on $A$, its equivalence classes form a partition of $A$. Conversely, every partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $A$ comes from exactly one equivalence relation: $a \sim b$ if and only if $a$ and $b$ lie in the same block of $\mathcal{P}$. Proof. Each class is non-empty and every $a$ lies in a class, because $a \in [a]$ by reflexivity. It remains to show that two classes are either equal or disjoint. Suppose $c \in [a] \cap [b]$, so $c \sim a$ and $c \sim b$. Take any $x \in [a]$: then $x \sim a$, $a \sim c$ (symmetry), $c \sim b$, so $x \sim b$ by transitivity twice, and $x \in [b]$. Hence $[a] \subseteq [b]$, and by the same argument $[b] \subseteq [a]$. So overlapping classes are equal. Conversely, given a partition, "same block" is reflexive (every element is in a block), symmetric (obvious), and transitive (if $a, b$ share block $P$ and $b, c$ share block $Q$, then $b \in P \cap Q$, so $P = Q$ because blocks are disjoint). Its classes are exactly the blocks. $\blacksquare$ > Trick: to show two classes are equal it is enough to find one common element. This "one overlap means equal" step is used again for cosets in post 02, where it gives Lagrange's theorem almost for free. Chapter 3 — Divisibility, gcd and the Euclidean algorithm Terminology - $a$ divides $b$, written $a \mid b$, if $b = ak$ for some integer $k$. Every integer divides $0$; $0$ divides only $0$.- A prime is an integer $p \geq 2$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. An integer $n \geq 2$ that is not prime is composite.- The greatest common divisor $\gcd(a, b)$ of integers not both zero is the largest integer dividing both. Set $\gcd(0, 0) = 0$.- $a$ and $b$ are coprime (relatively prime) if $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.- Well-ordering principle: every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a least element. It is equivalent to induction and is taken as an axiom about $\mathbb{N}$. The division algorithm Theorem 3.1 (division algorithm). For integers $a$ and $b$ with $b > 0$ there are unique integers $q, r$ with $$a = qb + r, \qquad 0 \leq r < b.$$ Proof. Existence: let $S = \{a - kb : k \in \mathbb{Z}, \ a - kb \geq 0\}$. $S$ is non-empty (take $k = -\lvert a\rvert$; then $a - kb = a + \lvert a\rvert b \geq a + \lvert a\rvert \geq 0$). By well-ordering $S$ has a least element $r = a - qb \geq 0$. If $r \geq b$, then $r - b = a - (q+1)b$ is a smaller element of $S$, a contradiction. So $0 \leq r < b$. Uniqueness: if $qb + r = q'b + r'$ with both remainders in $[0, b)$, then $b(q - q') = r' - r$ and $\lvert r' - r\rvert < b$. The only multiple of $b$ strictly between $-b$ and $b$ is $0$, so $r = r'$ and $q = q'$. $\blacksquare$ For negative $a$ the remainder is still non-negative: $-17 = (-4) \cdot 5 + 3$, not $(-3) \cdot 5 - 2$. > Common pitfall: in many programming languages returns (C, Java, JavaScript) while Python returns . Mathematics always uses $0 \leq r < b$. The Euclidean algorithm Lemma 3.2. If $a = qb + r$, then $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)$. Proof. Any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $r = a - qb$; any common divisor of $b$ and $r$ divides $a = qb + r$. The two pairs have the same common divisors, so the same greatest one. $\blacksquare$ Repeating the division algorithm, the remainders strictly decrease and so reach $0$; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd. For $\gcd(240, 46)$: $$\begin{aligned}240 &= 5 \cdot 46 + 10 \\46 &= 4 \cdot 10 + 6 \\10 &= 1 \cdot 6 + 4 \\6 &= 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \\4 &= 2 \cdot 2 + 0\end{aligned}$$ So $\gcd(240, 46) = 2$. The number of steps is small: it grows like the number of digits (Lamé's theorem bounds it by five times the number of decimal digits of the smaller number). Bézout's identity and the extended algorithm Theorem 3.3 (Bézout). For integers $a, b$ not both zero, there are integers $x, y$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. Moreover $\gcd(a, b)$ is the smallest positive integer of the form $ax + by$. Proof. Let $S = \{ax + by : x, y \in \mathbb{Z}\} \cap \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. It is non-empty (it contains $a^2 + b^2 > 0$), so it has a least element $d = ax0 + by0$. Divide: $a = qd + r$ with $0 \leq r < d$. Then $r = a - q(ax0 + by0) = a(1 - qx0) + b(-qy0)$ has the same form, so by minimality $r = 0$ and $d \mid a$. Likewise $d \mid b$. Any common divisor $c$ of $a, b$ divides $ax0 + by0 = d$, so $c \leq d$. Hence $d = \gcd(a, b)$. $\blacksquare$ The proof shows $x, y$ exist but does not find them. The extended Euclidean algorithm does: keep, next to every remainder $ri$, integers $si, ti$ with $ri = 240 si + 46 ti$. Start with $r0 = 240 = 240 \cdot 1 + 46 \cdot 0$ and $r1 = 46 = 240 \cdot 0 + 46 \cdot 1$; each new row is "row two above minus $q$ times row above". | $i$ | $ri$ | $qi$ | $si$ | $ti$ | check $240 si + 46 ti$ ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| 0 | 240 | — | 1 | 0 | 240 || 1 | 46 | — | 0 | 1 | 46 || 2 | 10 | 5 | 1 | −5 | 240 − 230 = 10 || 3 | 6 | 4 | −4 | 21 | −960 + 966 = 6 || 4 | 4 | 1 | 5 | −26 | 1200 − 1196 = 4 || 5 | 2 | 1 | −9 | 47 | −2160 + 2162 = 2 || 6 | 0 | 2 | — | — | stop | So $2 = 240 \cdot (-9) + 46 \cdot 47$. The same answer comes from back-substitution, reading the divisions from the bottom up: $2 = 6 - 4 = 6 - (10 - 6) = 2 \cdot 6 - 10 = 2(46 - 4 \cdot 10) - 10 = 2 \cdot 46 - 9 \cdot 10 = 2 \cdot 46 - 9(240 - 5 \cdot 46) = 47 \cdot 46 - 9 \cdot 240$. > Trick: the $x, y$ are not unique: $(x + 23k, \ y - 120k)$ works for every integer $k$, because $240 \cdot 23 = 46 \cdot 120$. Add a check column like the one above; one wrong sign early ruins every later row. Euclid's lemma Theorem 3.4 (Euclid's lemma). If $a \mid bc$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then $a \mid c$. In particular, if a prime $p$ divides $bc$, then $p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$. Proof. By Bézout, $ax + by = 1$ for some $x, y$. Multiply by $c$: $c = acx + bcy$. The number $a$ divides $acx$ and divides $bcy$ (because $a \mid bc$), so $a \mid c$. For the prime case: if $p \nmid b$ then $\gcd(p, b) = 1$ (the only positive divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$), so $p \mid c$. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: the prime hypothesis matters. $6 \mid 4 \cdot 9 = 36$ but $6 \nmid 4$ and $6 \nmid 9$. Chapter 4 — The fundamental theorem of arithmetic Theorem 4.1 (fundamental theorem of arithmetic). Every integer $n \geq 2$ can be written as a product of primes $n = p1 p2 \cdots pk$, and this factorisation is unique up to the order of the factors. Proof. Existence, by strong induction on $n$. If $n$ is prime, it is its own factorisation. Otherwise $n = ab$ with $2 \leq a, b < n$; by the induction hypothesis both $a$ and $b$ are products of primes, so $n$ is too. Uniqueness, by induction on $n$. Suppose $n = p1 \cdots pk = q1 \cdots ql$ with all $pi, qj$ prime. Then $p1$ divides $q1 \cdots ql$, so by Euclid's lemma (applied repeatedly) $p1 \mid qj$ for some $j$. Since $qj$ is prime and $p1 \geq 2$, $p1 = qj$. Reorder so that $j = 1$ and cancel: $p2 \cdots pk = q2 \cdots ql$ is a smaller number (or both sides are empty products equal to $1$), and by induction the remaining primes agree up to order. $\blacksquare$ Grouping equal primes gives the canonical form $n = p1^{e1} \cdots pr^{er}$ with $p1 < \dots < pr$. Examples: $360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$, $1001 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$, $2026 = 2 \cdot 1013$. Corollary 4.2 (Euclid). There are infinitely many primes. Proof. Given primes $p1, \dots, pk$, the number $N = p1 \cdots pk + 1$ has a prime factor $p$ by Theorem 4.1. If $p$ were some $pi$, then $p$ would divide $N - p1 \cdots pk = 1$, which is impossible. So no finite list contains every prime. $\blacksquare$ Uniqueness is not automatic. In the set $\{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ gives two genuinely different factorisations into elements that cannot be split further. Explaining when unique factorisation holds is one of the main themes of ring theory (post 04). Chapter 5 — Congruences and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ Terminology - Fix an integer $n \geq 1$, the modulus. $a$ is congruent to $b$ modulo $n$, written $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$, if $n \mid a - b$.- The class of $a$ is $[a]n = \{a + kn : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, also written $\bar{a}$ or just $a$ when the modulus is clear.- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (also written $\mathbb{Z}n$) is the set of the $n$ classes $[0], [1], \dots, [n-1]$.- A class $[a]$ is a unit if there is $[b]$ with $[a][b] = [1]$. The units form $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, also written $U(n)$.- A non-zero class $[a]$ is a zero divisor if $[a][b] = [0]$ for some non-zero $[b]$. Congruence mod $n$ is an equivalence relation: $n \mid 0$ (reflexive); $n \mid a - b$ implies $n \mid b - a$ (symmetric); $n \mid a - b$ and $n \mid b - c$ imply $n \mid a - c$ (transitive). By Theorem 2.3 its classes partition $\mathbb{Z}$, and by the division algorithm each class contains exactly one of $0, 1, \dots, n-1$, so there are exactly $n$ classes. Arithmetic on classes Define $[a] + [b] = [a + b]$ and $[a] \cdot [b] = [ab]$. Proposition 5.1. These operations are well defined: the result does not depend on which representatives are chosen. Proof. Suppose $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b'$, say $a' = a + kn$ and $b' = b + ln$. Then $a' + b' = a + b + (k + l)n \equiv a + b$ and $a'b' = ab + (al + bk + kln)n \equiv ab$. $\blacksquare$ "Well defined" is the central worry whenever an operation is defined on classes through representatives. The same check, done for cosets, is what forces the definition of a normal subgroup in post 02. In practice: reduce early and often. For example $3^{100} \bmod 7$: $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ (checked below), and $100 = 6 \cdot 16 + 4$, so $3^{100} = (3^6)^{16} \cdot 3^4 \equiv 3^4 = 81 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$. Units and zero divisors In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$: $[3][4] = [12] = [0]$, so $[3]$ and $[4]$ are zero divisors. On the other hand $[5][5] = [25] = [1]$, $[7][7] = [49] = [1]$ and $[11][11] = [121] = [1]$. The units are exactly $[1], [5], [7], [11]$, the classes coprime to $12$, and each of them is its own inverse. Theorem 5.2. $[a]$ is a unit in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if $\gcd(a, n) = 1$. If $\gcd(a, n) = d > 1$ and $n \nmid a$, then $[a]$ is a zero divisor. Proof. If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, Bézout gives $ax + ny = 1$, so $ax \equiv 1$ and $[x]$ is the inverse. Conversely if $ab \equiv 1$, then $ab - 1 = kn$, so $ab - kn = 1$ and every common divisor of $a$ and $n$ divides $1$. For the last claim: $a \cdot (n/d) = (a/d) \cdot n \equiv 0$, and $[n/d] \neq [0]$ because $0 < n/d < n$. $\blacksquare$ Corollary 5.3. $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has every non-zero class invertible if and only if $n$ is prime. Theorem 5.2 is also an algorithm: the inverse is the Bézout coefficient. To invert $17$ modulo $43$: $43 = 2 \cdot 17 + 9$, $17 = 1 \cdot 9 + 8$, $9 = 1 \cdot 8 + 1$. Back-substitute: $1 = 9 - 8 = 9 - (17 - 9) = 2 \cdot 9 - 17 = 2(43 - 2 \cdot 17) - 17 = 2 \cdot 43 - 5 \cdot 17$. So $17^{-1} \equiv -5 \equiv 38 \pmod{43}$. Check: $17 \cdot 38 = 646 = 15 \cdot 43 + 1$. Linear congruences Theorem 5.4. The congruence $ax \equiv b \pmod n$ has a solution if and only if $d = \gcd(a, n)$ divides $b$. In that case it has exactly $d$ solutions modulo $n$. Proof sketch. A solution means $ax - ny = b$ for some $y$, and by Bézout the values of $ax - ny$ are exactly the multiples of $d$. If $d \mid b$, divide through: $(a/d)x \equiv b/d \pmod{n/d}$, and $a/d$ is a unit mod $n/d$, so $x$ is unique mod $n/d$. That single class mod $n/d$ splits into $d$ classes mod $n$. $\blacksquare$ Example: $14x \equiv 6 \pmod{20}$. Here $\gcd(14, 20) = 2$ divides $6$. Divide by 2: $7x \equiv 3 \pmod{10}$. Since $7 \cdot 3 = 21 \equiv 1$, $7^{-1} \equiv 3 \pmod{10}$, so $x \equiv 9 \pmod{10}$, giving $x \equiv 9$ or $19 \pmod{20}$. Check: $14 \cdot 9 = 126 = 6 \cdot 20 + 6$ and $14 \cdot 19 = 266 = 13 \cdot 20 + 6$. > Common pitfall: you may not "divide both sides" of a congruence by a number that is not a unit. $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 9 \pmod{12}$, but $3 \not\equiv 9 \pmod{12}$. Division by $a$ modulo $n$ is multiplication by $a^{-1}$, which exists only when $\gcd(a, n) = 1$; otherwise the modulus must be divided too, as above. Chapter 6 — Euler's phi, Fermat and Euler Euler's phi function $\varphi(n)$ is the number of integers in $\{1, \dots, n\}$ coprime to $n$; by Theorem 5.2 it is the number of units, $\varphi(n) = \lvert(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times\rvert$. - For a prime $p$: $\varphi(p) = p - 1$.- For a prime power: $\varphi(p^k) = p^k - p^{k-1}$, because the numbers in $\{1, \dots, p^k\}$ not coprime to $p^k$ are exactly the $p^{k-1}$ multiples of $p$.- Multiplicative: if $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ then $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$. This follows from the Chinese remainder theorem (Chapter 7): $a$ is coprime to $mn$ exactly when it is coprime to both $m$ and $n$, and the CRT bijection matches such $a$ with pairs of units. Together these give, for $n = p1^{e1} \cdots pr^{er}$, $$\varphi(n) = n \prod{p \mid n} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p}\right).$$ Examples: $\varphi(12) = 12 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = 4$ (the units $1, 5, 7, 11$); $\varphi(36) = 36 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = 12$; $\varphi(100) = 100 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac45 = 40$; $\varphi(35) = \varphi(5)\varphi(7) = 4 \cdot 6 = 24$. Fermat's little theorem Theorem 6.1 (Fermat's little theorem). If $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. Consequently $a^p \equiv a \pmod p$ for every integer $a$. Proof. Consider the map $x \mapsto ax$ on the set $R = \{[1], [2], \dots, [p-1]\}$ of non-zero classes mod $p$. It lands in $R$ (a product of two units is a unit, so never $[0]$) and is injective, because $ax \equiv ay$ implies $x \equiv y$ after multiplying by $a^{-1}$. By the pigeonhole Proposition 2.2 it is a bijection, so it just reorders $R$. Multiply all elements of $R$ both ways: $$(a \cdot 1)(a \cdot 2) \cdots (a \cdot (p-1)) \equiv 1 \cdot 2 \cdots (p-1) \pmod p,$$ that is $a^{p-1} (p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod p$. Every factor of $(p-1)!$ is a unit, so $(p-1)!$ is a unit and can be cancelled: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$. For the second statement multiply by $a$; if $p \mid a$ both sides are $0$. $\blacksquare$ Check with $p = 7$, $a = 3$: multiplying $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ by 3 gives $3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4$ modulo 7, the same six classes in a new order, and $3^6 = 729 = 104 \cdot 7 + 1$. Euler's theorem Theorem 6.2 (Euler). If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Proof. Repeat the proof above with the set $U$ of the $\varphi(n)$ units mod $n$ instead of $R$: $x \mapsto ax$ permutes $U$, so $a^{\varphi(n)} \prod{u \in U} u \equiv \prod{u \in U} u$, and the product of units is a unit, so it cancels. $\blacksquare$ Fermat is the case $n = p$. Post 02 re-proves both in one line from Lagrange's theorem, because $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is a group of order $\varphi(n)$ and the order of an element divides the order of the group. Example: the last digit of $7^{222}$. $\varphi(10) = 4$ and $\gcd(7, 10) = 1$, so $7^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$. Since $222 = 4 \cdot 55 + 2$, $7^{222} \equiv 7^2 = 49 \equiv 9$. The last digit is 9. > Watch out: Euler's theorem needs $\gcd(a, n) = 1$. $2^{\varphi(4)} = 2^2 = 4 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$, not 1. Also, $\varphi(n)$ is an exponent that always works, not always the smallest one: modulo 12, every unit already satisfies $u^2 \equiv 1$ although $\varphi(12) = 4$. Chapter 7 — The Chinese remainder theorem Theorem 7.1 (Chinese remainder theorem). Let $n1, \dots, nk$ be pairwise coprime positive integers and $N = n1 \cdots nk$. For any integers $a1, \dots, ak$ the system $$x \equiv a1 \pmod{n1}, \quad \dots, \quad x \equiv ak \pmod{nk}$$ has a solution, and any two solutions are congruent modulo $N$. Proof. Existence: put $Ni = N / ni$. Since $ni$ is coprime to every other $nj$, it is coprime to $Ni$, so there is $Mi$ with $Ni Mi \equiv 1 \pmod{ni}$. Let $x = \sumi ai Ni Mi$. Modulo $nj$, every term with $i \neq j$ vanishes (because $nj \mid Ni$), and the $j$-th term is $aj \cdot 1$. Uniqueness: if $x, x'$ both work, each $ni$ divides $x - x'$; the $ni$ are pairwise coprime, so (by Euclid's lemma, repeatedly) their product $N$ divides $x - x'$. $\blacksquare$ Worked example (Sunzi's classic puzzle). Find $x$ with $x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, $x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$, $x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$. Schema design is the decision with the longest half-life in any system. Indexes can be added, queries can be rewritten, hardware can be replaced — but a wrong key choice or a wrong normalization decision propagates into every query, every migration, and every downstream consumer for years. This post covers the formal part (normal forms, keys, constraints), the pragmatic part (when denormalization is correct, how to model hierarchies and temporal data), the PostgreSQL-specific part (JSONB and when it is the right answer), and the operational part (how to change a schema without locking production out of its own database). > Series map: 00 Foundations & Setup → 01 SQL Fundamentals & Data Types → 02 Joins, Aggregation & Window Functions → 03 Indexes & the Query Planner → 04 Query Optimization & Statistics → 05 Transactions, MVCC, Isolation & Locking → 06 Schema Design, Normalization & JSONB (this post) → 07 Operations, Backup, Replication & Security. Chapter 3 — Keys: natural, surrogate, and index locality Terminology - Natural key — a key drawn from the data itself: ISBN, ISO country code, email address.- Surrogate key — a meaningless generated identifier: a sequence or a UUID.- Index locality — how close together in the btree consecutive inserts land. PostgreSQL Course — 03 · Indexes and the Query Planner
Created2025-10-05|Database PostgreSQL
An index is a redundant, automatically maintained data structure that lets the server find rows without reading the whole table. Adding the right one turns a forty-second query into a two-millisecond one; adding six wrong ones slows every write, wastes gigabytes, and leaves the slow query exactly as slow. The skill worth acquiring is not "add an index" — it is reading well enough to know which index, and to recognise the cases where the planner's sequential scan is genuinely the right answer. This post covers B-tree mechanics, all six index access methods, the leading-column rule, index-only scans, and the operational side: concurrent builds, bloat, and finding indexes nobody uses. Structure A PostgreSQL B-tree is a Lehman–Yao B-link tree, built from 8 KB pages: Numbers worth internalising: - Each page holds on the order of a few hundred entries for typical key widths, so fanout is a few hundred.- Three levels therefore index roughly a hundred million rows; four levels index far more. Chapter 2 — The six index types B-tree The default and the right answer for perhaps 90% of indexes: any type with a total order, equality and range predicates, sorting, uniqueness. Hash Equality only. WAL-logged and crash-safe since PostgreSQL 10 (before that they were not replicated and had to be rebuilt after a crash, which is why old advice says "never use hash indexes"). Narrower than a B-tree for long keys, since it stores a 32-bit hash rather than the value. In exchange it cannot do ranges, cannot sort, cannot be unique, and cannot be multicolumn. The niche is genuinely narrow: very long keys, equality-only access, no ordering requirement. Benchmark against a B-tree on your own data before committing. GIN — Generalized Inverted Index For values that contain multiple searchable items: array elements, keys and values, full-text lexemes, trigrams. GIN reads fast and writes slowly, because one row update can touch many index entries. The mechanism buffers changes in a pending list and flushes them in batches — good for write throughput, but it means a query may have to scan the unflushed pending list, producing occasional latency spikes. Tune with or set for read-latency-sensitive workloads. GiST — Generalized Search Tree A framework for indexing things without a natural total order: geometry, ranges, nearest-neighbour search, and the exclusion constraints from Post 01. GiST is lossy: it returns candidate rows that the executor must recheck. That is why a GiST plan shows a . SP-GiST — Space-Partitioned GiST For naturally hierarchical, non-balanced partitionings: quadtrees, k-d trees, radix trees. In practice: / containment, points, and text prefix search where a radix tree beats a B-tree on space. BRIN — Block Range INdex Stores a summary (by default min/max) for each range of heap pages — 128 pages by default. Consequently it is minuscule: an index over a billion-row table can be a few megabytes. BRIN only works when the physical row order correlates with the indexed value — append-only time-series data, log tables, tables loaded in key order. If rows are scattered, every block range spans the whole value domain and the index eliminates nothing. Check before building: PostgreSQL 14 added opclasses (tolerant of a few outliers that would otherwise ruin a range's summary) and opclasses (equality on uncorrelated columns). Chapter 1 — Inner products and norms Terminology - Inner product on a real vector space $V$ — a function $\langle u, v\rangle \in \mathbb{R}$ that is symmetric, linear in each argument, and positive definite (axioms below).- Inner product space — a vector space together with an inner product.- Norm $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$ — the length of $v$. A unit vector has norm 1.- Orthogonal — $\langle u, v\rangle = 0$, written $u \perp v$.- Orthonormal list — pairwise orthogonal unit vectors.- Orthogonal complement $W^\perp = \{v : \langle v, w\rangle = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\}$.- Orthogonal matrix — a square real matrix $Q$ with $Q^TQ = I$. Axioms and examples An inner product on a real vector space satisfies, for all $u, v, w$ and scalars $c$: 1. Symmetry: $\langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle$.2. Linearity: $\langle u + cv, w\rangle = \langle u, w\rangle + c\langle v, w\rangle$ (and so, by symmetry, linear in the second slot too).3. Positive definiteness: $\langle v, v\rangle \ge 0$, with equality only for $v = 0$. Examples: - Dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$: $\langle x, y\rangle = x^Ty = \sum xi yi$. This is the default in this post.- Weighted dot product: $\langle x, y\rangle = \sum wi xi yi$ with all $wi > 0$. More generally $\langle x, y\rangle = x^TMy$ for any symmetric positive definite $M$ (Chapter 6).- Functions: on $C[0,1]$, $\langle f, g\rangle = \int0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt$. For example $\langle 1, t - \tfrac12\rangle = \int0^1 (t - \tfrac12)\,dt = 0$: the constant 1 and $t - \frac12$ are orthogonal, and $\lVert t - \tfrac12\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{12}$.- Matrices: $\langle A, B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(A^TB) = \sum{i,j} a{ij}b{ij}$ (the Frobenius inner product). Over $\mathbb{C}$ the symmetry axiom becomes $\langle u, v\rangle = \overline{\langle v, u\rangle}$ and the standard product is $\sum xi \overline{yi}$; everything below has a complex version with $A^T$ replaced by the conjugate transpose $A^$. Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality Theorem 1 (Cauchy–Schwarz). $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert$, with equality if and only if $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent. Proof. If $v = 0$ both sides are 0. Otherwise, for every real $t$, $$0 \le \lVert u - tv\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 - 2t\langle u, v\rangle + t^2\lVert v\rVert^2.$$ A quadratic in $t$ with positive leading coefficient that is never negative has at most one real root, so its discriminant is $\le 0$: $4\langle u, v\rangle^2 - 4\lVert u\rVert^2\lVert v\rVert^2 \le 0$. Equality means the discriminant is 0, so for some $t$ the quadratic is 0, that is $u = tv$; conversely $u = tv$ gives equality directly. ∎ Cauchy–Schwarz makes angles possible: define $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ by $\cos\theta = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert}$, a number in $[-1, 1]$. Example: $u = (1, 2, 2)$, $v = (3, 0, 4)$ have $u \cdot v = 11$, $\lVert u\rVert = 3$, $\lVert v\rVert = 5$, so $\cos\theta = 11/15$ and $\theta \approx 42.8°$. Theorem 2 (triangle inequality). $\lVert u + v\rVert \le \lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert$. Proof. $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \le \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert + \lVert v\rVert^2 = (\lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert)^2$, using Cauchy–Schwarz in the middle. Take square roots. ∎ The same expansion gives Pythagoras: if $u \perp v$ then $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2$. Chapter 2 — Orthogonality and projection Orthonormal bases Proposition 3. An orthogonal list of nonzero vectors is linearly independent. Proof. If $\sum ci vi = 0$, take the inner product with $vj$: every term except $cj\langle vj, vj\rangle$ vanishes, so $cj\lVert vj\rVert^2 = 0$ and $cj = 0$. ∎ The same computation shows why orthonormal bases are so convenient. If $q1, \dots, qn$ is an orthonormal basis, then for every $v$: $$v = \sum{i=1}^n \langle v, qi\rangle\, qi, \qquad \lVert v\rVert^2 = \sum{i=1}^n \langle v, qi\rangle^2.$$ Coordinates are just inner products — no linear system to solve. The second formula is Parseval's identity. Orthogonal projection onto a subspace Let $W$ be a subspace with orthonormal basis $q1, \dots, qk$. The orthogonal projection of $v$ onto $W$ is $$PW v = \sum{i=1}^{k} \langle v, qi\rangle\, qi.$$ The residual $v - PW v$ is orthogonal to every $qj$ (take the inner product and use orthonormality), hence to all of $W$. So every $v$ splits uniquely as $v = PW v + (v - PW v)$ with the first part in $W$ and the second in $W^\perp$, and $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = \dim V$. Theorem 4 (best approximation). $PW v$ is the unique closest point of $W$ to $v$: for every $w \in W$ with $w \neq PW v$, $$\lVert v - w\rVert > \lVert v - PW v\rVert.$$ Proof. Write $v - w = (v - PW v) + (PW v - w)$. The first part is in $W^\perp$, the second in $W$, so by Pythagoras $\lVert v - w\rVert^2 = \lVert v - PW v\rVert^2 + \lVert PW v - w\rVert^2$, and the last term is positive when $w \neq PW v$. ∎ In matrix form. If $Q$ is the $n \times k$ matrix with orthonormal columns $qi$, then $Q^TQ = Ik$ and the projection matrix is $P = QQ^T$. If the columns of $A$ are any basis of $W$ (not orthonormal), then $$P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T.$$ Every projection matrix satisfies $P^2 = P$ and $P^T = P$. Projection onto a single line through $a$ is $\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}$; for example the projection of $b = (3, 0, 3)$ onto the line through $a = (1, 2, 2)$ is $\frac{a \cdot b}{a \cdot a}a = \frac{9}{9}a = (1, 2, 2)$, with residual $(2, -2, 1) \perp a$. > Common pitfall: $\sum \langle v, qi\rangle qi$ is the projection only when the $qi$ are orthonormal. With a non-orthogonal basis, adding up the one-dimensional projections double-counts, and the result is not the closest point. Use $A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$, or orthonormalise first. Chapter 3 — Gram–Schmidt and QR The algorithm Given independent vectors $v1, \dots, vn$, Gram–Schmidt produces an orthonormal list $q1, \dots, qn$ with the same span at every stage: $\operatorname{span}(q1, \dots, qj) = \operatorname{span}(v1, \dots, vj)$ for each $j$. At step $j$, subtract from $vj$ its projection onto the span of the earlier $q$'s, then normalise: $$wj = vj - \sum{i < j} \langle vj, qi\rangle\, qi, \qquad qj = \frac{wj}{\lVert wj\rVert}.$$ $wj \neq 0$ because $vj$ is not in the span of $v1, \dots, v{j-1}$. So every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. Worked example. $v1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $v2 = (1, 0, 1)$, $v3 = (0, 1, 1)$. - $q1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)$.- $\langle v2, q1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so $w2 = (1, 0, 1) - \frac12(1, 1, 0) = (\frac12, -\frac12, 1)$, with $\lVert w2\rVert = \sqrt{3/2}$. Then $q2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, -1, 2)$.- $\langle v3, q1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\langle v3, q2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}$, so $w3 = (0, 1, 1) - \frac12(1, 1, 0) - \frac16(1, -1, 2) = (-\frac23, \frac23, \frac23)$, and $q3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}(-1, 1, 1)$. Check: $q1 \cdot q2 = \frac{1 - 1 + 0}{\sqrt{12}} = 0$, $q1 \cdot q3 = \frac{-1 + 1 + 0}{\sqrt6} = 0$, $q2 \cdot q3 = \frac{-1 - 1 + 2}{\sqrt{18}} = 0$. QR factorisation Gram–Schmidt, written as matrices, is a factorisation. Each $vj$ is a combination of $q1, \dots, qj$ only, with coefficients $r{ij} = \langle vj, qi\rangle$ ($i < j$) and $r{jj} = \lVert wj\rVert$. So $$A = QR,$$ with $Q$ having orthonormal columns and $R$ upper triangular with positive diagonal. For the example, $R = Q^TA$: $$R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} & 1/\sqrt6 \\ 0 & 0 & 2/\sqrt3 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1.4142 & 0.7071 & 0.7071 \\ 0 & 1.2247 & 0.4082 \\ 0 & 0 & 1.1547 \end{pmatrix}.$$ > Watch out: Classical Gram–Schmidt loses orthogonality in floating point when the columns are nearly dependent. Libraries compute QR with Householder reflections (or use modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts each projection from the updated vector). The factorisation is the same; the arithmetic is more stable. Chapter 4 — Least squares The problem An overdetermined system $Ax = b$ ($A$ is $m \times n$ with $m > n$) usually has no solution: $b$ is not in the column space $C(A)$. The least-squares solution $\hat x$ minimises $\lVert b - Ax\rVert$. By Theorem 4, $A\hat x$ must be the projection of $b$ onto $C(A)$, so the residual $e = b - A\hat x$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$: $A^Te = 0$. That gives the normal equations $$A^TA\,\hat x = A^Tb.$$ If the columns of $A$ are independent, $A^TA$ is invertible (if $A^TAx = 0$ then $x^TA^TAx = \lVert Ax\rVert^2 = 0$, so $Ax = 0$, so $x = 0$), and $\hat x = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb$ is unique. Worked example — fitting a line Fit $y = c0 + c1 x$ to the points $(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2), (4, 5)$. Each point gives one equation $c0 + c1 xi = yi$: $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^Tb = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix}.$$ $\det(A^TA) = 120 - 100 = 20$, so $$\hat c = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} 30 & -10 \\ -10 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 31 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} -10 \\ 24 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 \\ 1.2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ The best line is $y = -0.5 + 1.2x$. The fitted values are $(0.7, 1.9, 3.1, 4.3)$ and the residuals $e = (0.3, 0.1, -1.1, 0.7)$. Checks: $A^Te = 0$, that is, the residuals sum to 0 and $\sum xi ei = 0.3 + 0.2 - 3.3 + 2.8 = 0$. The minimum sum of squared errors is $0.09 + 0.01 + 1.21 + 0.49 = 1.8$; no other line does better. Least squares with QR If $A = QR$ (reduced: $Q$ is $m \times n$), the normal equations $R^TQ^TQR\hat x = R^TQ^Tb$ simplify to $$R\hat x = Q^Tb,$$ solved by back substitution. This is the method used in practice. Forming $A^TA$ squares the condition number (Chapter 7), which can destroy half the significant digits; QR avoids it. Chapter 5 — Orthogonal matrices and the spectral theorem Orthogonal matrices A square $Q$ with $Q^TQ = I$ has orthonormal columns and $Q^{-1} = Q^T$. It preserves inner products, lengths and angles: $(Qx)\cdot(Qy) = x^TQ^TQy = x \cdot y$. Taking determinants, $\det(Q)^2 = 1$, so $\det Q = \pm 1$. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ the orthogonal matrices are exactly the rotations ($\det = 1$) and the reflections ($\det = -1$) of Linear Algebra 03. Products and inverses of orthogonal matrices are orthogonal. A change of basis to an orthonormal basis uses an orthogonal $P$, so $P^{-1}AP = P^TAP$: no inverse to compute. The spectral theorem Theorem 5 (spectral theorem, real symmetric case). If $A$ is a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix ($A^T = A$), then: 1. all eigenvalues of $A$ are real;2. eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal;3. $A$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $Q$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal. Conversely, any $Q\Lambda Q^T$ is symmetric, so a real matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable if and only if it is symmetric. Proof sketch. The key identity is $\langle Ax, y\rangle = x^TA^Ty = x^TAy = \langle x, Ay\rangle$. (1) Let $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \in \mathbb{C}^n$ nonzero. The number $\bar v^TAv$ equals its own complex conjugate (conjugate it, use that $A$ is real, then transpose the $1 \times 1$ result and use $A^T = A$), so it is real. It also equals $\lambda\,\bar v^Tv = \lambda\sum\lvert vi\rvert^2$, and $\sum\lvert vi\rvert^2 > 0$ is real. So $\lambda$ is real. (2) If $Av1 = \lambda1 v1$ and $Av2 = \lambda2 v2$, then $\lambda1\langle v1, v2\rangle = \langle Av1, v2\rangle = \langle v1, Av2\rangle = \lambda2\langle v1, v2\rangle$, so $(\lambda1 - \lambda2)\langle v1, v2\rangle = 0$. (3) Induction on $n$. By (1) there is a real eigenvalue $\lambda1$ with a unit eigenvector $q1$. The complement $W = q1^\perp$ is mapped into itself: if $w \perp q1$ then $\langle Aw, q1\rangle = \langle w, Aq1\rangle = \lambda1\langle w, q1\rangle = 0$. On the $(n-1)$-dimensional space $W$, $A$ still satisfies $\langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle$, so by induction $W$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $q2, \dots, qn$. Together with $q1$ this is an orthonormal eigenbasis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. ∎ Worked example. $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ from Linear Algebra 04 has eigenvalues $4, 1, 1$. The eigenvector $(1,1,1)$ for 4 is automatically orthogonal to the eigenplane $x + y + z = 0$ for 1. Inside that plane, Linear Algebra 04 used $(-1, 1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, 1)$, which are not orthogonal to each other — Gram–Schmidt fixes that. An orthonormal choice is $$q1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1), \quad q2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1, 0), \quad q3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, 1, -2), \qquad Q^TBQ = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1, 1).$$ The spectral decomposition writes $A$ as a sum of rank-one projections: $$A = \lambda1 q1q1^T + \lambda2 q2q2^T + \dots + \lambdan qnqn^T.$$ For $B$: $B = 4q1q1^T + q2q2^T + q3q3^T$. Since $q2q2^T + q3q3^T = I - q1q1^T$, this is $B = I + 3q1q1^T$. > Trick: For symmetric matrices, eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are orthogonal for free. You only need Gram–Schmidt inside an eigenspace of dimension 2 or more. Chapter 6 — Positive definite matrices and quadratic forms Four equivalent tests A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite if $x^TAx > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$, and positive semidefinite if $x^TAx \ge 0$ for every $x$. Theorem 6. For a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent: 1. $A$ is positive definite;2. every eigenvalue of $A$ is positive;3. every leading principal minor (the determinant of the top-left $k \times k$ block, $k = 1, \dots, n$) is positive (Sylvester's criterion);4. elimination without row swaps succeeds with every pivot positive;5. $A = R^TR$ for some invertible $R$ — for example the Cholesky factor, upper triangular with positive diagonal. Proof of 1 ⇔ 2. Write $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ and put $y = Q^Tx$ (a change to the eigenvector basis; $y \neq 0$ exactly when $x \neq 0$). Then $$x^TAx = y^T\Lambda y = \lambda1 y1^2 + \dots + \lambdan yn^2,$$ which is positive for all $y \neq 0$ exactly when every $\lambdai > 0$. For 5 ⇒ 1: $x^TR^TRx = \lVert Rx\rVert^2 > 0$ when $x \neq 0$ because $R$ is invertible. The links to 3 and 4 go through the LU factorisation of Linear Algebra 01: the $k$-th pivot equals the ratio of the $k$-th and $(k-1)$-th leading minors. ∎ Worked example. $$K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ - Leading minors: $2$, $4 - 1 = 3$, $\det K = 4$. All positive.- Pivots: $2$, $\frac32$, $\frac43$ — the ratios $\frac21, \frac32, \frac43$ of consecutive minors.- Eigenvalues: $2 - \sqrt2 \approx 0.586$, $2$, $2 + \sqrt2 \approx 3.414$.- The quadratic form is a sum of squares: $x^TKx = 2x^2 - 2xy + 2y^2 - 2yz + 2z^2 = x^2 + (x - y)^2 + (y - z)^2 + z^2$, which is $0$ only when $x = y = z = 0$. All four tests agree: $K$ is positive definite. (This matrix is the second-difference matrix that appears when a differential equation $-u'' = f$ is discretised.) > Common pitfall: Positive entries do not make a matrix positive definite, and negative entries do not prevent it. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ has all entries positive but determinant $-1$, so its eigenvalues are $2 \pm \sqrt5$, one of them negative; $K$ above has negative entries and is positive definite. Quadratic forms and principal axes A quadratic form is $q(x) = x^TAx$ with $A$ symmetric (any cross term $bxy$ is split evenly between the two off-diagonal entries). The spectral theorem rotates the axes so the cross terms disappear. Worked example. $q(x, y) = 5x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2$ has $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with trace 7 and determinant 6, so eigenvalues $6$ and $1$. Unit eigenvectors: $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)$ for 6 and $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1, 2)$ for 1. In the rotated coordinates $u = \frac{2x + y}{\sqrt5}$, $v = \frac{-x + 2y}{\sqrt5}$, $$q = 6u^2 + v^2.$$ (Check at $(x, y) = (1, 0)$: $u = 2/\sqrt5$, $v = -1/\sqrt5$, and $6 \cdot \frac45 + \frac15 = 5 = q(1, 0)$.) The level curve $q = 6$ is the ellipse $u^2 + v^2/6 = 1$: semi-axis $1$ along $(2, 1)$ and $\sqrt6$ along $(-1, 2)$. The principal axes are the eigenvectors, and the semi-axes are $\sqrt{c/\lambdai}$ for the level $q = c$. In calculus the same idea classifies critical points: a positive definite Hessian means a local minimum. Chapter 7 — The singular value decomposition The theorem Diagonalization needs a square matrix, and even then it can fail or need complex numbers. The SVD always works. Theorem 7 (SVD). Every real $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ can be written as $$A = U\Sigma V^T,$$ where $U$ ($m \times m$) and $V$ ($n \times n$) are orthogonal and $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ with diagonal entries $\sigma1 \ge \sigma2 \ge \dots \ge \sigmar > 0$ and zeros elsewhere. The $\sigmai$ are the singular values (uniquely determined by $A$); the columns $vi$ of $V$ and $ui$ of $U$ are the right and left singular vectors, and $Avi = \sigmai ui$. Proof. $A^TA$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite ($x^TA^TAx = \lVert Ax\rVert^2 \ge 0$), so by the spectral theorem it has an orthonormal eigenbasis $v1, \dots, vn$ with eigenvalues $\lambda1 \ge \dots \ge \lambdan \ge 0$. Exactly $r$ of them are positive, because $A^TA$ has the same null space as $A$ (shown in Chapter 4), hence rank $r$. Put $\sigmai = \sqrt{\lambdai}$ and, for $i \le r$, $ui = Avi/\sigmai$. These are orthonormal: $$ui \cdot uj = \frac{vi^TA^TAvj}{\sigmai\sigmaj} = \frac{\lambdaj\, vi \cdot vj}{\sigmai\sigmaj} = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \neq j. \end{cases}$$ Extend $u1, \dots, ur$ to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$. Then $Avi = \sigmai ui$ for $i \le r$ and $Avi = 0$ for $i > r$, which is $AV = U\Sigma$, that is $A = U\Sigma V^T$. ∎ Dropping the zero parts gives the compact form $A = \sum{i=1}^{r}\sigmai ui vi^T$, a sum of $r$ rank-one matrices. The SVD also hands you orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces of Linear Algebra 02: $v1, \dots, vr$ for the row space, $v{r+1}, \dots, vn$ for the null space, $u1, \dots, ur$ for the column space, and $u{r+1}, \dots, um$ for the left null space. Worked example $$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}.$$ $A^TA$ has trace 50 and determinant $625 - 400 = 225$, so eigenvalues $45$ and $5$, with unit eigenvectors $v1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $v2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)$. The singular values are $\sigma1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.708$ and $\sigma2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$. Then $$u1 = \frac{Av1}{\sigma1} = \frac{(3, 9)/\sqrt2}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3), \qquad u2 = \frac{Av2}{\sigma2} = \frac{(-3, 1)/\sqrt2}{\sqrt5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(-3, 1),$$ $$A = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}{U} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 3\sqrt5 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt5 \end{pmatrix}}{\Sigma} \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}{V^T}.$$ Checks: $\sigma1\sigma2 = 15 = \det A$ (here $\det U = \det V = 1$), and $\sigma1^2 + \sigma2^2 = 50 = 9 + 0 + 16 + 25$, the sum of the squared entries. > Watch out: The singular values of $A$ are not its eigenvalues. This $A$ is triangular with eigenvalues 3 and 5, but singular values $6.708$ and $2.236$. For a symmetric matrix the singular values are the absolute values of the eigenvalues (more generally this holds for normal matrices, those with $A^TA = AA^T$), and for a positive semidefinite matrix they are exactly the eigenvalues. Geometry: rotate, stretch, rotate Read $A = U\Sigma V^T$ from right to left as three steps applied to the unit circle: 1. $V^T$ is orthogonal: it rotates (or reflects) the plane, carrying $v1, v2$ to $e1, e2$. Here $V^T$ is rotation by $-45°$. The circle stays a circle.2. $\Sigma$ stretches along the axes by $\sigma1$ and $\sigma2$. The circle becomes an axis-aligned ellipse with semi-axes $6.708$ and $2.236$.3. $U$ rotates again, carrying $e1, e2$ to $u1, u2$. In general $U$ and $V$ may include a reflection; both can be chosen as rotations exactly when $\det A \ge 0$ (for square $A$). Norms and conditioning - Spectral norm: $\lVert A\rVert2 = \max{\lVert x\rVert = 1}\lVert Ax\rVert = \sigma1$ — the longest semi-axis.- Frobenius norm: $\lVert A\rVertF = \sqrt{\sum a{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sigma1^2 + \dots + \sigmar^2}$ (orthogonal matrices do not change it).- Condition number of an invertible $A$: $\kappa(A) = \sigma1/\sigman$, the ratio of the longest to the shortest semi-axis. For the example $\kappa = 3\sqrt5/\sqrt5 = 3$. Solving $Ax = b$ can amplify relative errors in $b$ by up to $\kappa$; since $\sigmai(A^TA) = \sigmai^2$, forming the normal equations squares it. This, and not the size of $\det A$, measures closeness to singularity (Linear Algebra 03, Chapter 7). Chapter 8 — Using the SVD Low-rank approximation Theorem 8 (Eckart–Young, statement). Let $A = \sum{i=1}^r \sigmai ui vi^T$ and, for $k < r$, $Ak = \sum{i=1}^k \sigmai ui vi^T$. Among all matrices $B$ of rank at most $k$, $$\min{\operatorname{rank} B \le k}\lVert A - B\rVert2 = \lVert A - Ak\rVert2 = \sigma{k+1}, \qquad \min{\operatorname{rank} B \le k}\lVert A - B\rVertF = \lVert A - Ak\rVertF = \sqrt{\sigma{k+1}^2 + \dots + \sigmar^2}.$$ Keep the $k$ largest singular triples and drop the rest; nothing of rank $k$ does better. For the example, $$A1 = 3\sqrt5\, u1v1^T = \frac{3}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 1.5 \\ 4.5 & 4.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A - A1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix},$$ and $\lVert A - A1\rVertF^2 = 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5 = \sigma2^2$, as the theorem says. Storage: a rank-$k$ approximation of an $m \times n$ matrix needs $k(m + n + 1)$ numbers instead of $mn$. For a $1000 \times 1000$ greyscale image and $k = 50$, that is $100\,050$ numbers instead of $1\,000\,000$, about 10%. Whether the result looks acceptable depends on how fast the singular values decay. The pseudoinverse The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of $A = U\Sigma V^T$ is $$A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^T,$$ where $\Sigma^+$ is $n \times m$ with $1/\sigmai$ in place of each nonzero $\sigmai$. It inverts $A$ on the row space and sends the left null space to 0. $x^+ = A^+b$ is the least-squares solution of $Ax = b$ with the smallest norm — well defined even when $A$ is rank-deficient and the normal equations have infinitely many solutions. If $A$ has independent columns, $A^+ = (A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ and $A^+b$ is the $\hat x$ of Chapter 4; for the line fit, $A^+b = (-0.5, 1.2)$. If $A$ is invertible, $A^+ = A^{-1}$. Example. $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2\,uu^T$ with $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, so $A^+ = \frac12 uu^T = \frac14\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. For $b = (1, 3)$ (not in the column space), $x^+ = A^+b = (1, 1)$. Every $x$ with $x1 + x2 = 2$ gives the same smallest residual (because $Ax = (2, 2)$, the projection of $b$), and $(1, 1)$ is the shortest of them. Principal component analysis Put $N$ data points in $\mathbb{R}^p$ as the rows of a matrix and subtract the mean of each column, giving a centred $N \times p$ matrix $X$. The sample covariance matrix is $C = \frac{1}{N - 1}X^TX$. If $X = U\Sigma V^T$ then $C = V\frac{\Sigma^T\Sigma}{N - 1}V^T$, so: - the principal directions are the right singular vectors $vi$ (eigenvectors of $C$);- the variance along $vi$ is $\sigmai^2/(N - 1)$;- projecting the data onto $v1, \dots, vk$ is the best $k$-dimensional summary in the least-squares sense (Eckart–Young applied to $X$). Worked example. The four points $(3, 3), (-3, -3), (1, -1), (-1, 1)$ already have mean zero. $X^TX = \begin{pmatrix} 20 & 16 \\ 16 & 20\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 36 and 4 with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so the singular values of $X$ are 6 and 2. The first principal direction is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ with variance $36/3 = 12$; the second has variance $4/3$. The first component explains $36/(36 + 4) = 90\%$ of the total variance. > Common pitfall: Forgetting to centre the data. Without subtracting the mean, the first "principal component" mostly points at the mean itself. Also scale the columns first when they are in different units, or the largest-unit variable dominates. Practice Exercises 1. Show that $\langle f, g\rangle = \int0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt$ satisfies the positive-definiteness axiom on $C[0,1]$. Then find the projection of $f(t) = t$ onto the constants. <details><summary>Solution</summary> $\langle f, f\rangle = \int0^1 f^2 \ge 0$. If $f$ is continuous and $f(t0) \neq 0$, then $f^2 > \frac12 f(t0)^2$ on some interval around $t0$, so the integral is positive; hence $\langle f, f\rangle = 0$ forces $f = 0$. (Continuity matters here.) Projection onto $\operatorname{span}(1)$: $\frac{\langle t, 1\rangle}{\langle 1, 1\rangle}\cdot 1 = \frac{1/2}{1} = \frac12$. The residual $t - \frac12$ is orthogonal to 1, as computed in Chapter 1. </details> 2. Apply Gram–Schmidt to $a1 = (1, 1, 1, 1)$, $a2 = (0, 1, 2, 3)$. <details><summary>Solution</summary> $q1 = \frac12(1, 1, 1, 1)$. $\langle a2, a1\rangle / \langle a1, a1\rangle = 6/4 = \frac32$, so $w2 = (0, 1, 2, 3) - \frac32(1, 1, 1, 1) = (-\frac32, -\frac12, \frac12, \frac32)$ with $\lVert w2\rVert = \sqrt{\frac94 + \frac14 + \frac14 + \frac94} = \sqrt5$. So $q2 = \frac{1}{2\sqrt5}(-3, -1, 1, 3)$. (This is the "centred $x$" that makes line fitting at $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ decouple.) </details> 3. For the line fit in Chapter 4, compute the projection matrix $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$ and check $Pb$. <details><summary>Solution</summary> $(A^TA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{pmatrix} 30 & -10 \\ -10 & 4\end{pmatrix}$, and $$P = \frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 4 & 1 & -2 \\ 4 & 3 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\ -2 & 1 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$ $Pb = \frac{1}{10}(7 + 8 + 2 - 10,\ 4 + 6 + 4 + 5,\ 1 + 4 + 6 + 20,\ -2 + 2 + 8 + 35) = (0.7, 1.9, 3.1, 4.3)$, the fitted values. Also $P^2 = P$ and $P^T = P$. </details> 4. Is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ positive definite? Use two different tests. <details><summary>Solution</summary> Leading minors: $4 > 0$ and $12 - 4 = 8 > 0$: yes. Eigenvalues: trace 7, determinant 8, so $\lambda = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{49 - 32}}{2} = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{17}}{2}$, both positive because $\sqrt{17} < 7$. Or complete the square: $4x^2 + 4xy + 3y^2 = (2x + y)^2 + 2y^2$. </details> 5. Find the singular values of $E = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ and its condition number. <details><summary>Solution</summary> $E^TE = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 5\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 8 and 2, so $\sigma1 = 2\sqrt2$, $\sigma2 = \sqrt2$, and $\kappa = 2$. Check: $\sigma1\sigma2 = 4 = \lvert\det E\rvert = \lvert -2 - 2\rvert$. </details> 6. Prove that if $Q$ is orthogonal then every real eigenvalue of $Q$ is $\pm1$, and every singular value of $Q$ is 1. <details><summary>Solution</summary> If $Qv = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$, then $\lVert v\rVert = \lVert Qv\rVert = \lvert\lambda\rvert\,\lVert v\rVert$, so $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$. Singular values are square roots of the eigenvalues of $Q^TQ = I$, all equal to 1: an orthogonal map sends the unit sphere to itself. </details> 7.
