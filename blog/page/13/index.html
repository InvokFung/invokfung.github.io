Violin Notes — 07 · Grade 7 All Positions, Harmonics, Advanced Bowing
Created2026-05-08|Music
Grade 7 ABRSM violin is conservatory-entry territory. Every position from half to 8th is required, three-octave scales in all major and minor keys, harmonics (natural and artificial), more double-stop intervals (4ths, octaves), and the bow technique vocabulary expands to martelé, ricochet, sautillé. Repertoire is full Romantic concertos and Bach solo suite movements. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 (this post) → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play all major and minor scales (three-octave + harmonic & melodic minor).- Use all positions half through 8th in repertoire.- Play natural harmonics and introduce artificial harmonics.- Use martelé ("hammered"), ricochet ("thrown bow"), sautillé (fast spiccato).- Play octaves as double stops in selected keys.- Identify modulations to remote keys; understand modal music.- Perform a complete Romantic concerto movement. Chapter 1 — Harmonics: the bell-like tones A harmonic is a high, pure, bell-like tone produced by lightly touching the string at specific nodes (rather than pressing down). Natural harmonics Found at division points of the open string: | Touch point on string | Resulting harmonic | Pitch ||-----------------------|--------------------|-------|| 1/2 (octave node) | 1st overtone | Open string + octave || 1/3 node | 2nd overtone | Octave + 5th || 1/4 node | 3rd overtone | Two octaves above open || 1/5 node | 4th overtone | Two octaves + maj 3rd | The 1/2 node on the A string is found by lightly touching with your 4th finger at the position where the 4th finger would normally play A5 (one octave above open A). The sound: a soft, ringing high A — an octave higher than expected. > Trick: harmonics need almost no finger pressure — just a feather touch. If you press, you get a muted scratchy note. If you brush, you get the pure overtone. Practise the touch on open A: bow long stroke, feather-touch where the 3rd finger sits in 1st position. The ringing high A (two octaves up) appears almost magically. Artificial harmonics Press one finger firmly (the "stopping finger") and touch with another finger a 4th higher (the "harmonic finger"). The pitch produced is two octaves above the stopped pitch. Notation: diamond-shape note head for the touched note, plus regular note for the stopped note. Used famously in: Tchaikovsky Violin Concerto, Paganini Caprices, Ravel Tzigane. Chapter 2 — All positions: fluency target By Grade 7, you've learned half, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th positions. Now: 8th position and fluency across all of them. 8th position On the A string: 1=B5, 2=C♯6, 3=D6, 4=E6 (right at the top of the violin's standard range for most pieces). The left hand pivots: thumb shifts to a different angle, hand approaches the bridge. The fluency goal You should be able to:1. Shift to any position without looking.2. Find the correct first finger pitch within a quarter-tone, on the first try.3. Choose appropriate position for expression (tone colour) — not just to reach a note. > Trick: practise a single piece entirely in 1st position, then again entirely in 3rd, then entirely in 5th (skipping notes you can't reach). The same melody in different positions sounds different — different string vibrations, different timbre. Pick the position that suits the music. Position landmarks (physical) | Position | Hand landmark ||----------|---------------|| 1st | Index touches the neck near the nut || 3rd | Thumb at the curve of the neck || 5th | Hand at the neck-body joint || 7th | Hand over the body curve || 8th+ | Thumb leaves the neck, sits on the shoulder of the violin | Chapter 3 — Advanced bowings Martelé ("hammered") A short, percussive stroke with a strong attack and a dead stop. Bow pressure increased before the stroke begins, released through the stroke, stops abruptly. Used for: marching feel, accented passages, opening of dramatic phrases. Ricochet ("rebound, thrown") Multiple notes from a single thrown bow. The bow is dropped from above, bounces 2–4 times naturally, lifted before it stops. Used in: Mendelssohn Concerto last movement, Paganini Caprices, Sarasate showpieces. Sautillé ("springing") Very fast spiccato — so fast that the bow no longer fully lifts off; the wood "springs" rapidly. Played in the middle of the bow with a loose grip. Used in: Vivaldi Four Seasons (Summer storm), Beethoven Concerto 1st mvt, Mendelssohn Concerto 1st mvt. > Where I got stuck: I couldn't transition from spiccato to sautillé. The hand wanted to "control" each bounce. Fix: extreme tempo experiment. Start a tremolo (very fast back-and-forth on one note). Gradually add finger changes. The wrist learns the bouncing speed first, then the fingers add pitches. Chapter 4 — Octave double stops Two notes an octave apart, played simultaneously. Fingering: 1st finger on lower string + 4th finger on the next string up (e.g., 1 on G + 4 on D for an octave). Octaves require:- 1st and 4th fingers to be exactly in tune with each other.- 3rd and 4th fingers stretched apart maximally. Practice Slow tuner work. Tune 1st finger first; freeze it. Add 4th finger; adjust until the octave rings (no beats). Then bow the double stop. Practise on every string pair: G+D, D+A, A+E. > Trick: octaves are the second-hardest interval to tune (after major 7ths). Beats are very audible; an octave that's even slightly off sounds painful. Practise daily for 5 minutes. Octave fluency takes 6 months minimum. Chapter 5 — Modulations to remote keys Grade 7 introduces modulation analysis to: - Distant keys (e.g., C major → E major; 4 sharps' difference)- Modal music (Dorian, Phrygian, Mixolydian, Lydian) Modal recognition | Mode | Built on (C major scale) | Sound ||------|--------------------------|-------|| Ionian | C — C | Major (familiar) || Dorian | D — D | Minor with raised 6 || Phrygian | E — E | Minor with lowered 2 (Spanish/Flamenco) || Lydian | F — F | Major with raised 4 (dreamy) || Mixolydian | G — G | Major with lowered 7 (folk/rock) || Aeolian | A — A | Natural minor || Locrian | B — B | Diminished, rare | > Trick: listen to Bartók Romanian Folk Dances, Bach modal cantatas, Debussy. Modal music sounds "neither major nor minor". Train the ear to spot the characteristic interval that defines each mode (Dorian's raised 6, Phrygian's lowered 2, etc.). Chapter 7 — Memorisation strategy By Grade 7, you should perform from memory. Strategy: 1. Analyse the structure first. Break the piece into sections (A-B-A, exposition-development-recap, theme + variations, etc.). Label each section.2. Memorise section by section — not start-to-finish.3. Memorise the hardest section first. Mental energy is highest at the start of practice; spend it on the toughest bars.4. Practise from any starting point. Open the score randomly; you should be able to start there from memory. This builds resilience for performance anxiety.5. Mental practice. Without the violin, mentally walk through the piece — every shift, every bow direction, every fingering. 10 minutes a day. Increases memory reliability dramatically.6. Slow performance practice. Once memorised, play the whole piece at 60% tempo from memory. Catches weak spots. > Where I got stuck: I memorised by repetition only — fingers learned the piece, but my brain didn't. Under stress (an audition), my fingers blanked. Fix: 10 minutes of mental practice daily. It feels useless at first, but after a month, memory failure rate dropped 80%. Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 7 aural: - Sing/play 8-bar phrases with modulations after 2 hearings.- Identify modes (Dorian, Phrygian, etc.).- Identify chord progressions with non-diatonic chords.- Sight-sing 8-bar with modulation.- Identify period (Baroque / Classical / Romantic / 20th-c) by style. Violin Notes — 06 · Grade 6 Double Stops, Higher Positions, Chromatic Scales
Created2026-05-07|Music
Grade 6 ABRSM violin is where the instrument doubles itself. Double stops — two strings sounded together — become a syllabus requirement. The right hand learns flying staccato, the left hand uses all positions through 5th regularly, and chromatic scales add 12-note fluency. The repertoire is now full Baroque sonatas, mid-Romantic showpieces, and the first complete concerto movements expected at concert level. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 (this post) → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play 3rds and 6ths double stops through one octave on selected keys.- Play chromatic scale (one or two octaves) starting on G or A.- Use 1st–5th positions fluently in a single piece.- Play three-octave scales in G, A, B♭ major (and some minor).- Use staccato volante ("flying staccato") in showpieces.- Identify ornaments + execute trills, mordents, turns idiomatically.- Perform a complete sonata movement + a Romantic showpiece. Chapter 1 — Double stops: the violin singing in two voices A double stop is two strings bowed simultaneously, each producing a different pitch. The bow must contact both strings at once. Bow angle for double stops The bow normally tilts to one side to contact one string. For double stops, the bow lies flat on both strings at the same angle. Bow arm height adjusts to suit. Easy double stops: open string + stopped string Examples:- Open E + 1st finger B on A = B4 + E5 (a 4th)- Open A + 1st finger F♯ on E = A4 + F♯5 (a 6th) These are the entry-level double stops; you've been hearing them in pieces since Grade 3 (as the drone effect). Stopped double stops: 3rds Two fingers on adjacent strings, both stopped. For a 3rd in 1st position: To get a 3rd, you need two fingers, one per string, giving notes 3 letters apart. In 1st position on A + E: The Grade 6 syllabus typically asks for a one-octave scale in 3rds in keys like G major. 6ths Two-finger spread; the two fingers are usually on different strings, one slightly behind the other. > Where I got stuck: my fingers couldn't independently stop adjacent strings at different positions. Fix: very slow practice with a tuner on each note separately, then together. 5 minutes per day, 6 weeks, before any scale in 3rds was reliable. Chapter 2 — Chromatic scale 12 semitones in one octave, played either with traditional fingering or sliding-finger chromatic. Traditional fingering (G chromatic ascending, one octave on G + D string) The same finger plays two consecutive semitones by sliding (1 plays G♯ then A; 2 plays A♯ then B). The finger slides up about ½ a finger-width. Galamian's chromatic fingering Uses a different finger pattern that avoids slides. Faster but harder to learn. > Trick: the slide-finger chromatic is more idiomatic and what most pieces expect. Practise it very slowly with metronome. The "click" of the finger sliding between two semitones should be inaudible — the bow disguises it. Chapter 3 — Flying staccato (staccato volante) A series of bouncing staccato notes all on one bow direction, the bow lifting between each. Distinct from slurred staccato (which stays on the string). Flying staccato lifts. How to learn it 1. Solid up-bow staccato first (easier direction). 4 notes per bow at slow tempo.2. Each note: bow stops and lifts a millimetre, then drops for the next note.3. Build to 8 notes per bow.4. Reverse to down-bow staccato. Used famously in: Sarasate Zigeunerweisen, Wieniawski concertos, encore showpieces. > Trick: the wrist + fingers produce the "stop+lift" motion; the arm stays steady. If your arm is jerking, you're forcing it. Relax, slow down. Chapter 4 — Higher positions: 6th, 7th, half position Half position The hand sits below 1st position — 1st finger plays just one semitone above the open string (B♭ on the A string). Used for low chromatic passages. 6th and 7th positions 6th pos on A: 1=G♯5.7th pos on A: 1=A5 (an octave above open A). In 7th position, the hand sits over the body of the violin. Thumb adjusts to a different position; left hand has limited mobility. Used mostly for very high passages in concertos and showpieces. > Where I got stuck: my left wrist collapsed in 7th position — the angle felt wrong. Fix: explicitly lift the left elbow forward (more underneath the violin) when shifting above 5th. The hand pivots more from underneath. Chapter 5 — Ornaments executed idiomatically Grade 6 demands playing, not just identifying, ornaments. Trill (tr or ⟵⟶) Rapid alternation between the written note and the note a step above. In Baroque: starts on the upper note. In Classical/Romantic: starts on the written note. The 2nd finger trills (lift-drop) while bow sustains. Trill speed: 6–10 oscillations per second. Practise slowly first, build speed. Mordent (𝆚) Quick three-note ornament: written note → step below (or above) → written note. Turn (∾) Four-note ornament: above-note → written → below-note → written. Appoggiatura (small note before main note) Takes half the value of the following note in Classical music. Acciaccatura (small note with slash through stem) A "crush" note — played as fast as possible, then immediately to the main note. Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 6 aural expands: - Sing/play back 4-bar phrases with modulations.- Identify modulations to dominant, subdominant, relative minor.- Identify chord progressions including II-V-I, secondary dominants.- Identify formal sections (binary, ternary, rondo).- Sight-sing 8-bar phrase with chromatic notes. Modulation identification Listen for the new key's tonic as the resolution point. The dominant of the new key (V/V if modulating to dominant) often precedes it. > Trick: practise modulation identification using piano hymnals or simple chorales. Sing the new tonic when it arrives. Build the habit of locating "the new home". Violin Notes — 05 · Grade 5 Vibrato, Shifting Across Positions, Three-Octave Scales
Created2026-05-06|Music
Grade 5 ABRSM violin is the milestone exam. Pass it, and you've earned the right to attempt Grades 6–8 (which require Grade 5 theory as a prerequisite — read your local rules). Technically, Grade 5 demands vibrato, shifts to 4th and 5th position, the first three-octave scales for some keys, and a far wider repertoire including full sonata movements. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 (this post) → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Apply continuous vibrato to sustained notes.- Shift fluently between 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th positions.- Play three-octave G and A major scales (some syllabi).- Play two-octave scales in all major keys + most minor keys (harmonic + melodic).- Use sul G (play on G string only) and sul ponticello / sul tasto colour techniques.- Identify all triads (major, minor, diminished, augmented) at sight.- Play a complete Baroque sonata movement + one concerto movement. Chapter 1 — Vibrato: the expression Vibrato is the small, rapid pitch oscillation that adds warmth and life to sustained notes. Without it, classical violin tone sounds naked. Three vibrato types | Type | Source | Speed | When ||------|--------|-------|------|| Wrist vibrato | Wrist rocks back and forth | Medium | Most repertoire || Arm vibrato | Whole forearm rocks | Slow + wide | Romantic, big tone || Finger vibrato | Finger joint flexes | Fast + narrow | Rare; modern works | Wrist is the standard first vibrato taught at Grade 5. Learning wrist vibrato — step by step 1. Air vibrato — without violin. Hold left arm up as if holding violin. Wave hand at the wrist, palm-up to palm-down motion, for 30 sec. This is the motion.2. Vibrato on knee. Sit. Place finger on knee. Rock wrist gently, finger stays in contact, pad rolls. Build motor pattern.3. Vibrato on violin neck (no string). Finger on the neck, not string. Rock wrist.4. Vibrato on string with finger anchored. 3rd finger on A (= D5). Bow a long D on the A string. Rock wrist; finger should roll (not slide). Pitch should wobble below the pitch and back to it.5. Slow metronome vibrato. 4 wobbles per beat at 60 bpm = 4 Hz. Build to 6 Hz over 6 months.6. Apply to scales. Vibrate every sustained note in a slow scale. > Where I got stuck: my wrist locked. Vibrato felt impossible for 3 months. Fix: spend 5 minutes per day on the "knee drill" — no violin, no string, just the wrist motion. The brain learns motion separately from instrument. Once the wrist could rock freely, transferring to the violin took one week. Pitch direction Vibrato should wobble below the in-tune pitch back up to it. Never above. The brain hears the upper limit of the oscillation as "the note". Where to start using it Don't vibrate every note. Start with:- Long notes (half-notes, whole-notes).- Phrase peak notes.- Final note of a phrase. By Grade 6, you'll vibrate almost every note that's longer than a quaver. At Grade 5, just the obvious ones. Chapter 2 — Shifting to 4th and 5th positions 4th position On the A string: 1=E5, 2=F♯5, 3=G♯5, 4=A5. Note: 1 in 4th position = E5 = the same note as the open E next door. Useful for staying on one string. 5th position 1=F♯5, 2=G♯5, 3=A5, 4=B5. 5th position is where the violin's neck meets the body. The thumb position changes — you'll start using the "shoulder" of the violin as a reference. The shift map (3-octave G major) To play G major across 3 octaves, you traverse:- G string: 1st position G-A-B-C- D string: 1st pos D-E-F♯-G- A string: 1st pos A-B-C-D- E string: 1st pos E-F♯-G- Shift to 3rd pos on E: A-B-C- Shift to 5th pos on E: D-E-F♯ (fingers 2-3-4)- Shift to 7th pos on E for the top G (3rd finger) — or go to 6th position for D-E-F♯-G (1-2-3-4); depends on edition > Trick: learn the shift route as a fixed sequence. Mark "III" or "V" above the music. Practise just the route (no rhythm) until the shifts are automatic; then add timing. Silent shift drill (essential at Grade 5+) 1. Bow open A.2. Slide 1st finger up to D5 (3rd pos).3. Continue sliding to F♯5 (5th pos).4. Back down to D5, then to 1st position B.5. Repeat 10×, very slowly. No glissando audible. Daily. The shifts smoothen over weeks. Chapter 3 — Three-octave scales The standard 3-octave scale layout (G major shown): Exact fingering varies by teacher. Pick one fingering and stick to it for 6 months minimum. Switching fingerings mid-learning resets your muscle memory. Recommended fingering source - Carl Flesch, Scale System — the bible of violin scales. Used by every conservatory student.- Galamian Contemporary Violin Technique — slightly different fingerings.- Whichever your teacher prefers. Chapter 4 — Triads: all four qualities Grade 5 theory requires identification of: | Triad | Intervals (from root) | Sound ||-------|------------------------|-------|| Major | Maj 3rd + Min 3rd | Bright || Minor | Min 3rd + Maj 3rd | Sad || Diminished | Min 3rd + Min 3rd | Tense, unstable || Augmented | Maj 3rd + Maj 3rd | Suspenseful, dreamy | Building each from C - C major: C–E–G (M3 + m3)- C minor: C–E♭–G (m3 + M3)- C diminished: C–E♭–G♭ (m3 + m3)- C augmented: C–E–G♯ (M3 + M3) Inversions A triad has 3 notes; any of them can be in the bass: | Position | Bass note | Example (C major) ||----------|-----------|--------------------|| Root | Root | C–E–G || First inversion | 3rd | E–G–C || Second inversion | 5th | G–C–E | Notation: figured bass uses ⁶ (1st inv) and ⁶⁄₄ (2nd inv). At Grade 5 you must identify these from a score. Chapter 5 — Tone colours Grade 5 introduces colour markings in scores. Common ones: | Marking | Italian | Effect ||---------|---------|--------|| sul G | "on the G string" | Forces phrase onto G string for rich, dark tone || sul D / A / E | "on the D/A/E string" | Same idea, different colour || sul ponticello | "near the bridge" | Glassy, eerie, metallic || sul tasto | "over the fingerboard" | Soft, flute-like, dreamy || pizzicato | "plucked" | Plucked string || col legno | "with the wood" | Tap with the bow stick | Practise: D major scale sul ponticello, then sul tasto. The difference is dramatic. Composers exploit this. Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 5 aural is a separate exam paper in some syllabi or a section of the practical. It includes: - Sing or play back a 4–8 bar phrase after one hearing.- Identify modulations to dominant or relative minor.- Identify cadences in context.- Identify chord progressions (basic I-IV-V-I).- Sight-sing a longer phrase with leaps. > Trick: daily aural drill. 5 minutes, every day. Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine 3rd position — the workhorse 3rd position is the second most used position after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd. Finding 3rd position On the A string:1. Play open A.2. Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).3. Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5. Other strings analogous:- G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.- D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.- E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D. The shift motion Three components, in this order:1. Release finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).2. Thumb and hand travel together up the neck.3. Press the new finger at the target. The finger that leads the shift is called the guide finger. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1. > Trick: practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a "scrape", the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide. 2nd position Less common than 3rd. The hand sits halfway between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F. 2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits just above 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the "sweet spot" (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note. Spiccato practice progression 1. Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves) B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering: Wait — that's a lot of "low" fingers across the scale: every B♭ and E♭, plus F and G on the E string. Chapter 3 — Dotted rhythms A dot after a note adds half its value: The Grade 2 staple is the dotted-quaver + semiquaver pair: Count subdivisions: > Trick: I subdivided in semiquavers aloud for every dotted pair for 2 weeks: "ONE-e-and-AH, ONE-e-and-AH". Violin Notes — 01 · Grade 1 First Steps & Repertoire
Created2026-05-02|Music
Grade 1 ABRSM violin is where fingers meet strings. The syllabus is small but the techniques inside are foundational: 1st position fingering, three major keys (G, D, A), one-octave scales, basic bowing (détaché and slur), and a handful of short pieces that you'll remember decades later. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Place fingers 1, 2, 3, 4 in 1st position on all four strings.- Play one-octave scales of G, D, A majors (separate bows).- Play one-octave arpeggios for the same keys.- Read fluently in treble clef across the first 1.5 octaves above Middle C.- Use détaché (separate) bowing cleanly.- Begin to feel "in tune" — recognise when a finger placement is sharp or flat.- Play 3–5 Grade 1 / Initial Grade pieces with hands together. Chapter 1 — 1st Position fingering What "1st position" means The position number tells you where the first finger sits on the fingerboard. In 1st position, the index finger sits just below the nut — about a whole tone above the open string. From there, fingers 2, 3, 4 extend up the fingerboard, each roughly a whole tone apart (with one half-step adjustment per scale). The diagram shows the Grade 1 pattern: whole steps between the fingers, except a half-step between fingers 2 and 3. In other keys, the half-step location shifts (Chapter 3). Finger numbering | Finger | Number ||--------|--------|| Index | 1 || Middle | 2 || Ring | 3 || Little (pinky) | 4 || (Thumb supports underneath, no number) | — | This is opposite to piano — there, the thumb is 1. On the violin, the thumb has no number because it never stops a string; its job is to brace the neck. > Trick: coming from piano, I kept calling my thumb "finger 1" and reaching with my index expecting it to be "2". The fix: every Grade 1 piece marks "1" above the index. Trust the printed number, not your old habit. Placing fingers cleanly 1. Fingertip down, not the pad — the smaller contact area produces a cleaner stop.2. Curved finger — knuckles bent.3. Just enough pressure to stop the string vibrating above the finger. Too much pressure = sharp pitch + finger fatigue.4. Drop and lift — don't slide. Vertical motion only. > Where I got stuck: I pressed every finger too hard for two months. My left hand cramped after 10 minutes. The fix: deliberately under-press until the note buzzes; then add the minimum extra pressure to stop the buzz. That's the right amount. Most students press 2-3× more than needed. Chapter 2 — Tuning revisited (the violinist's ear) You learnt to tune in Post 00. Grade 1 adds double-stop tuning — bow two adjacent open strings together and listen for the perfect 5th. How a perfect 5th sounds When two strings tuned a perfect 5th apart sound together, the combined tone is smooth and ringing. When slightly off, you hear beats — a wobbling, rough pulse like an out-of-sync washing machine. To tune by 5ths:1. Tune A to A=440 with a tuner.2. Bow A and D together gently. Adjust D's fine tuner until beats vanish.3. Bow D and G together. Adjust G.4. Bow A and E together. Adjust E. > Trick: sit in a quiet room. Beats are easier to hear without background noise. After 6 months of tuning by 5ths, you'll do it automatically — and you'll have built a perfect-5th template in your ear that helps every interval recognition exercise. Chapter 3 — The three Grade 1 scales G major (one octave, G string + D string) Open G → 1st finger A → 2nd finger B → 3rd finger C → open D → 1st finger E → 2nd finger F♯ → 3rd finger G. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 on each string. (B→C and F♯→G are half-steps.) D major (one octave, D string + A string) Open D → 1 E → 2 F♯ → 3 G → open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D. Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 again. A major (one octave, A string + E string) Open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D → open E → 1 F♯ → 2 G♯ → 3 A. Same half-step pattern. > Trick: G, D, A majors all use the same finger pattern in 1st position: 0-1-2-3 with the half-step between 2 and 3 on each string. Memorise one hand shape; transfer to all three keys. This is the "violin equivalent" of the piano's W-W-H-W-W-W-H — but expressed as finger placement. Arpeggios (Grade 1) The tonic triad spelled out: root, third, fifth, octave. Practise scales and arpeggios in pairs: G major scale → G major arpeggio → G again. Builds the key-feel. Chapter 4 — Bowing: détaché and slur Détaché "Detached" — each note gets its own bow stroke (down or up). The bow doesn't lift between notes; it changes direction on the string. In Grade 1 you play almost everything détaché. Goal: smooth, even tone with no scratching at the bow direction change. The wrist makes a tiny "give" at each change, like a pendulum bottom. Slur A curved line over two or more notes means play them in one bow stroke (one direction, no change). Same hair, multiple finger changes. > Where I got stuck: I scratched every bow change for the first 2 months. Fix: practise the bow change motion itself without a string — just air bow with the empty bow, smoothly reversing direction. The wrist learns to soften the change. Then add the string back. Bow distribution On a slur of 4 notes, you have to "ration" the bow length so you don't run out. Roughly: divide the bow into 4 equal parts; one part per note. Practice: slur 2 notes per bow, then 4, then 8. By Grade 3 you'll be slurring entire scales in one bow. Chapter 5 — Reading music for violin Violin reads in treble clef only (unlike the piano, which uses both). Range covers from open G (below the staff, under two ledger lines) up to many ledger lines above. By Grade 1 you read up to about A or B above the staff (3rd finger on E string). Violin Notes — 00 · Beginner Basics & Your First Sound
Created2026-05-01|Music
The first post in a self-study violin series. Goal: by the end you can hold the violin without straining, name every open string, draw a straight bow that makes one sustained tone, and play three short open-string pieces. The violin is harder than the piano at the start — there's no "pressed key = right pitch" guarantee — but the payoff is the most expressive single-line instrument we have. > Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Hold the violin with relaxed posture, no shoulder hunching.- Hold the bow with the correct grip and produce a straight stroke.- Name the four open strings (G, D, A, E) low to high and find them on the staff.- Tune with a clip-on tuner or app.- Play three open-string exercises that produce a clear, ringing tone.- Understand the difference between violin, viola, cello, and double bass — pick the right one. Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now) The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is unfretted — you place your finger somewhere and the precision of placement decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise. In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match. > Where I started: I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Size matters first Violins come in fractional sizes for children: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 4/4 (full). Adults use 4/4 unless they have unusually short arms. Measure from neck-base to the centre of the palm with arm extended: | Arm length (neck to palm) | Size ||---------------------------|------|| < 36 cm | 1/8 || 36–43 cm | 1/4 || 43–48 cm | 1/2 || 48–55 cm | 3/4 || ≥ 55 cm | 4/4 (full) | What to buy as an adult beginner A "beginner outfit" includes: violin, bow, case, rosin, shoulder rest. Brands in the $200-$500 range that don't disappoint: - Yamaha V3, V5, V7 — reliable workhorse, well-set-up.- Stentor Student II, Conservatoire — UK-popular student line.- Cremona SV-175, SV-500 — entry-level, decent set-up.- Eastman VL80, VL100 — Chinese workshop, often resold by reputable shops. Avoid $50-$100 Amazon "VSO" (Violin-Shaped Object) instruments — they're often un-tuneable, with cheap pegs that slip and bridges that are wrong. Accessories you'll definitely need | Item | Notes ||------|-------|| Rosin | Light or dark; "Pirastro Goldflex" or "Hidersine" are safe starts || Shoulder rest | Kun, Everest, Bonmusica — try in person if possible || Tuner | Clip-on (Snark, KLIQ) or phone app (Tunable, Cleartune) || Music stand | Foldable, cheap. Use it from day one || Metronome | App fine. Essential by week 4 || Spare strings | Dominant or Tonica brand for first replacement || Soft cloth | Wipe rosin dust off strings + body after every session | > Trick: the violin shop visit is worth it even if you order online. Spend 30 minutes at a real shop trying setups in your size range. Your ear immediately tells you which one rings — pay the extra $100 for the one that sings. Cheap-and-dull will discourage you within a month. Chapter 3 — The instrument anatomy Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these): - Scroll, pegs, peg box — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs- Nut — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard- Fingerboard — the long black wooden strip your fingers press- Bridge — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration- F-holes — the two carved openings; let sound out- Soundpost — small wooden dowel inside the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.- Bass bar — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.- Tailpiece — anchors the strings at the bottom- Fine tuners — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)- Chinrest — where your chin rests- Shoulder rest — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years. Standing 1. Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.2. Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).3. Head facing forward, then turn it slightly left to look down the fingerboard.4. Place the violin on your left collarbone, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.5. The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — don't grip with your jaw. The shoulder rest does the work. > Where I got stuck: I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest higher until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is supported, not gripped. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit. Sitting Same upper-body posture. Sit on the front half of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch. Left-hand position - Thumb rests on the side of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.- Fingers curve over the strings.- Wrist straight — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.- A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger. Common mistakes:- Wrist collapsed under the neck (the "frying pan grip") — kills agility.- Thumb wrapped over the top of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.- Fingers flat on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down. Chapter 5 — The bow Anatomy of the bow - Stick — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)- Hair — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings- Frog — black bottom piece where the screw lives- Screw / button — adjusts hair tension- Tip / point — top end- Grip / wrap — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life) 1. Pick the bow up at the frog with your right hand.2. Thumb bent, tip placed in the notch between the frog and the wrap.3. Middle finger opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.4. Ring finger rests on the side of the frog.5. Pinky curved on top of the stick, near the screw.6. Index finger wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight. All fingers curved, never locked straight. The grip is light — too tight and the bow can't move. > Trick: practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — then pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows. Tightening the bow Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a standard pencil fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings. Always loosen after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber. Rosin Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times before every session. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone. Chapter 6 — Tuning The four strings, low to high: G — D — A — E. Each interval is a perfect 5th (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5. With a clip-on tuner or app 1. Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.2. Pluck or bow each string one at a time.3. Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise. Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments) Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing into the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Chapter 8 — Where statistics becomes machine learning Everything above is mostly inference: estimate a parameter, quantify uncertainty, decide whether an effect is real. Machine learning uses the same mathematics to optimise a different target — out-of-sample prediction error. The methods overlap almost entirely; the objectives do not. Terminology - Training error — error on the data used to fit; optimistically biased, always. Generalisation error — expected error on new data from the same distribution; the quantity you actually care about.- Overfitting — the model learned noise specific to the training set (train error low, generalisation error high). Underfitting — too rigid to capture the signal (both high). Hyperparameter — a knob not learned from the training data. Regularisation — a penalty on complexity, trading a little bias for a lot of variance. High bias / low variance is a linear model on curved data: wrong the same way every time. Low bias / high variance is a degree-15 polynomial through 20 points: fits each training set beautifully and differently. The best model is rarely the most flexible; it is the one minimising the sum. Train / validation / test Train fits the parameters, validation chooses hyperparameters and model families, and test is touched exactly once to estimate generalisation error (60/20/20, or 80/20 with cross-validation inside the 80). k-fold cross-validation replaces a single validation split: partition into folds, fit on , score the held-out one, rotate, average. or is standard, uses the data far more efficiently, and gives a variance estimate alongside the mean. > Watch out — leakage. Fit scalers, imputers and feature selectors inside the CV loop, on the training fold only; standardising the full dataset before splitting leaks test information and produces a score that will not survive production. makes the correct thing the easy thing. Two more traps: split time-ordered data by time (), never randomly, or the model sees the future; and split grouped data by group (), or the same user lands on both sides. Regularisation as a statistical idea Ridge and lasso are not ML inventions bolted onto regression — they are from Chapter 5 turned into an algorithm: accept a little bias, cut a lot of variance, lower the total. | | Ridge (L2) | Lasso (L1) ||---|---|---|| Coefficients | shrunk toward 0, never exactly 0 | some set exactly to 0 || Feature selection | no | yes, automatically || Correlated predictors | keeps all, splits the weight | picks one arbitrarily, drops the rest || Bayesian reading | MAP with a Gaussian prior on | MAP with a Laplace prior on || Best when | many small effects; multicollinearity | few predictors matter; | is chosen by cross-validation, never by looking at the test set. Both penalties require standardised predictors — otherwise a variable in millimetres is penalised a thousand times more than the same variable in metres — and the intercept is conventionally left unpenalised. > Common pitfall: the p-values printed for a regularised model are not valid in the usual sense — the penalty biases the coefficients on purpose. Selecting variables with lasso and then reporting ordinary OLS inference on the survivors badly understates the uncertainty. Honest p-values are a job for an inferential model on a separate dataset. A gradient-boosted model with excellent AUC says nothing about whether raising the price causes churn — it will happily exploit any correlate, including consequences of churn. Conversely, a beautifully specified regression with every assumption satisfied may generalise poorly, because unbiasedness is not low prediction error.
