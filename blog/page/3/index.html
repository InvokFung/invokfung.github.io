Violin Notes — 00 · Beginner Basics & Your First Sound
Created2026-05-20|Music
The first post in a self-study violin series. Goal: by the end you can hold the violin without straining, name every open string, draw a straight bow that makes one sustained tone, and play three short open-string pieces. The violin is harder than the piano at the start — there's no "pressed key = right pitch" guarantee — but the payoff is the most expressive single-line instrument we have. > Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Hold the violin with relaxed posture, no shoulder hunching.- Hold the bow with the correct grip and produce a straight stroke.- Name the four open strings (G, D, A, E) low to high and find them on the staff.- Tune with a clip-on tuner or app.- Play three open-string exercises that produce a clear, ringing tone.- Understand the difference between violin, viola, cello, and double bass — pick the right one. Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now) The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is unfretted — you place your finger somewhere and the precision of placement decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise. In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match. > Where I started: I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Size matters first Violins come in fractional sizes for children: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 4/4 (full). Adults use 4/4 unless they have unusually short arms. Measure from neck-base to the centre of the palm with arm extended: | Arm length (neck to palm) | Size ||---------------------------|------|| < 36 cm | 1/8 || 36–43 cm | 1/4 || 43–48 cm | 1/2 || 48–55 cm | 3/4 || ≥ 55 cm | 4/4 (full) | What to buy as an adult beginner A "beginner outfit" includes: violin, bow, case, rosin, shoulder rest. Brands in the $200-$500 range that don't disappoint: - Yamaha V3, V5, V7 — reliable workhorse, well-set-up.- Stentor Student II, Conservatoire — UK-popular student line.- Cremona SV-175, SV-500 — entry-level, decent set-up.- Eastman VL80, VL100 — Chinese workshop, often resold by reputable shops. Avoid $50-$100 Amazon "VSO" (Violin-Shaped Object) instruments — they're often un-tuneable, with cheap pegs that slip and bridges that are wrong. Accessories you'll definitely need | Item | Notes ||------|-------|| Rosin | Light or dark; "Pirastro Goldflex" or "Hidersine" are safe starts || Shoulder rest | Kun, Everest, Bonmusica — try in person if possible || Tuner | Clip-on (Snark, KLIQ) or phone app (Tunable, Cleartune) || Music stand | Foldable, cheap. Use it from day one || Metronome | App fine. Essential by week 4 || Spare strings | Dominant or Tonica brand for first replacement || Soft cloth | Wipe rosin dust off strings + body after every session | > Trick: the violin shop visit is worth it even if you order online. Spend 30 minutes at a real shop trying setups in your size range. Your ear immediately tells you which one rings — pay the extra $100 for the one that sings. Cheap-and-dull will discourage you within a month. Chapter 3 — The instrument anatomy Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these): - Scroll, pegs, peg box — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs- Nut — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard- Fingerboard — the long black wooden strip your fingers press- Bridge — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration- F-holes — the two carved openings; let sound out- Soundpost — small wooden dowel inside the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.- Bass bar — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.- Tailpiece — anchors the strings at the bottom- Fine tuners — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)- Chinrest — where your chin rests- Shoulder rest — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years. Standing 1. Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.2. Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).3. Head facing forward, then turn it slightly left to look down the fingerboard.4. Place the violin on your left collarbone, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.5. The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — don't grip with your jaw. The shoulder rest does the work. > Where I got stuck: I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest higher until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is supported, not gripped. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit. Sitting Same upper-body posture. Sit on the front half of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch. Left-hand position - Thumb rests on the side of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.- Fingers curve over the strings.- Wrist straight — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.- A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger. Common mistakes:- Wrist collapsed under the neck (the "frying pan grip") — kills agility.- Thumb wrapped over the top of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.- Fingers flat on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down. Chapter 5 — The bow Anatomy of the bow - Stick — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)- Hair — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings- Frog — black bottom piece where the screw lives- Screw / button — adjusts hair tension- Tip / point — top end- Grip / wrap — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life) 1. Pick the bow up at the frog with your right hand.2. Thumb bent, tip placed in the notch between the frog and the wrap.3. Middle finger opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.4. Ring finger rests on the side of the frog.5. Pinky curved on top of the stick, near the screw.6. Index finger wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight. All fingers curved, never locked straight. The grip is light — too tight and the bow can't move. > Trick: practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — then pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows. Tightening the bow Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a standard pencil fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings. Always loosen after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber. Rosin Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times before every session. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone. Chapter 6 — Tuning The four strings, low to high: G — D — A — E. Each interval is a perfect 5th (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5. With a clip-on tuner or app 1. Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.2. Pluck or bow each string one at a time.3. Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise. Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments) Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing into the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Push-and-turn together. - Turn away from you (top of peg moves forward) = string winds up = pitch rises.- Turn toward you = pitch falls. Small turns. Pegs are sensitive. Tune the G first, then D, A, E. Fine tuning (small adjustments) The little screws on the tailpiece. Turn clockwise to raise pitch, counter-clockwise to lower. Use these for daily small corrections (< quarter-tone). > Where I got stuck: I terrified myself snapping a string in week one. Fix: tune slowly and small. If a string is way off, raise it gradually, not in one big peg crank. Snapped strings are normal — buy a backup E string ($5) and stop being afraid. Tuning A first (orchestral convention) In orchestral settings, tune A first (because the oboe gives an A). Then tune D, G, E by listening for perfect fifths with the open A. This is your first ear-training exercise — when the two strings are exactly a 5th apart, the combined sound is clean. When slightly off, you hear "beats" — a wobbling pulse. Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing Before fingers go on strings, master pulling a clean tone on each open string. The straight bow The bow must move perpendicular to the strings (parallel to the bridge). If it angles toward the fingerboard or the bridge, the contact point changes and the tone breaks up. Practice exercise:1. Place the bow flat on the A string, about halfway between bridge and fingerboard.2. Slowly pull a downstroke (bow moving from frog toward tip). Count "1-2-3-4".3. Pause. Reset. Upstroke (tip toward frog). Same count.4. Watch the bow in a mirror — is it staying parallel to the bridge? Contact point Three knobs control your sound:- Weight — how much the bow presses down (mostly from the index finger via the stick)- Speed — how fast the bow moves- Contact point — distance from the bridge (closer to bridge = louder + sharper; closer to fingerboard = softer + warmer) Balance these three. A faster bow needs more weight to maintain pressure. A bow close to the bridge needs slower speed to avoid scratching. The "sweet spot" for a clean, warm tone is about 1.5–2 cm from the bridge for a normal mezzo-forte dynamic. > Analogy: think of the bow like painting. Speed is how fast you move the brush. Weight is how hard you press. Contact point is which part of the paper you're stroking. Change any one and the paint stroke changes. Beginners change all three accidentally and produce a mess. Master one knob at a time. Three first exercises Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string Whole bow length, 8 beats per stroke. Down-up-down-up on each string in turn: G, D, A, E. Listen for a clean, sustained tone with no scratches or wobbles. Five minutes daily. Exercise 2 — String crossings Bow A for one beat, then D for one beat. The forearm rotates from elbow; wrist stays neutral. Do A-D-A-D-A-D, then D-A-D-A. Then add G-D and A-E. Tests bow-arm coordination without fingers complicating things. Exercise 3 — "Hot cross buns" (open strings only) The melody fits perfectly on three open strings if you treat it as A-D-A-D-G-D-A. Just bow each string in the rhythm of the tune. You'll feel embarrassed playing nursery rhymes — and then proud, because you sound musical. Chapter 8 — How to practise The single most important rule: slow and small reps beat fast big ones. Five minutes of focused open-string bowing teaches more than thirty minutes of trying to play melodies you can't yet. Daily routine, 25 minutes | Block | Time | What ||-------|------|------|| Tune | 2 min | Tuner first, then check by ear || Bow hold check | 2 min | Hold + release the bow on a pencil 5 times || Open-string long bows | 8 min | All 4 strings, full bow, 8 beats per stroke || String crossings | 5 min | A-D, D-A, G-D, A-E || Exercise piece | 5 min | One short pattern (open strings only this month) || Cool-down | 3 min | Play any open string slowly, listen to the tone, end on a clear sound | Slow practice rules 1. No scratch, no rush. If the tone is bad, slow down.2. One thing at a time. Bow speed or bow weight or contact point — not all three at once.3. Mirror. Use a mirror or phone front-camera for 5 min per session for the first 3 months. The eye catches what the hand doesn't.4. Stop before fatigue. Tired = tense = bad habits. Better to stop at 20 minutes feeling fresh than 45 minutes feeling done. > Trick: after every session, record 30 seconds on your phone. Open string, long bow, full attention. Listen back. Week 1 will sound rough. Week 8 will sound transformed. The recording is your honest progress meter when your ears in the moment lie. Chapter 9 — Common beginner mistakes | Mistake | Fix ||---------|-----|| Crooked bow (drifts toward fingerboard) | Mirror practice. Mark the bridge-side bow lane with tape on your bow if needed || Death-grip on the bow | Lay the bow on your palm, close the fingers gently, lift. Fingers curved, never locked || Pressing the string too hard with left fingers | The fingertip just needs to touch the string firmly enough to stop it vibrating above. Test by pressing with minimum force until the note is clean || Shoulder hunching | Drop the shoulder. Reset every minute. If pain appears, the shoulder rest needs adjusting || Loose bow hair | Tighten before playing, loosen after. Storage in tight position warps the stick || Forgetting to rosin | The hair must be coated. New hair = many strokes; daily = a few || Tuning by guess | Always use a tuner the first 3 months. Ear training comes from playing in tune, not from guessing || Practising past pain | Stop. The violin is the most injury-prone instrument. Pain = bad form | Useful Shortcut Cheat-sheet | What | How ||------|-----|| Open strings (low → high) | G D A E (each a perfect 5th) || Tuning reference | A = 440 Hz (orchestral standard) || Bow hold thumb | In the notch between frog and wrap || Bow hair tension | Pencil fits between hair + stick at midpoint || Straight bow | Parallel to the bridge, perpendicular to strings || Sound sweet spot | 1.5–2 cm from bridge for mf || Practice motto | Slow + clean tone > fast + scratchy || End each session | Loosen the bow, wipe rosin off strings + body | When to move on You're ready for Post 01 — Grade 1 Theory & Repertoire when you can:- Tune all four strings with a tuner unaided.- Produce a clean, sustained tone on each open string for 8 beats.- Cross strings smoothly without scratching.- Play "Hot cross buns" or "Twinkle Twinkle" (open-string version) recognisably.- Hold the violin and bow for 15 minutes without fatigue. If you're not there yet, stay here another 2-3 weeks. Grade 1 adds fingers; without solid open-string tone, fingered notes will sound worse, not better. AWS Course — 07 · Observability & Security Baseline
Created2026-05-19|Cloud AWS
A workload that runs but can't be observed is unmaintainable; a workload without a security baseline is a liability. This post covers the production-ready ops layer every AWS account needs: CloudWatch (metrics, logs, alarms, dashboards), CloudTrail (API audit log), Systems Manager (Parameter Store, Patch Manager, Run Command), KMS (encryption keys), Secrets Manager (credentials), WAF (web firewall), and GuardDuty (threat detection). None of these were strictly required to deploy a workload in the previous posts — they're required to run one safely. > Series map: 00 Foundations → 01 Network First → 02 Compute & Storage → 03 Scale & Survive → 04 Managed Data → 05 Serverless & Events → 06 Workflow Orchestration → 07 Observability & Security (this post) → 08 IaC. Learning Outcome - Read CloudWatch metrics, write logs, create alarms + dashboards.- Use CloudTrail to find "who did what when" in your account.- Store config in Parameter Store, secrets in Secrets Manager.- Use KMS to encrypt at rest with customer-managed keys.- Attach AWS WAF to ALB / API Gateway / CloudFront for L7 protection.- Enable GuardDuty + Security Hub for baseline threat detection.- Set up the minimum production observability checklist. Chapter 1 — CloudWatch metrics Every AWS service publishes metrics to CloudWatch automatically: - EC2 — CPU, network in/out, status checks.- ALB — request count, target response time, 5XX count, healthy host count.- Lambda — invocations, errors, throttles, duration, concurrent executions.- RDS — CPU, free memory, IOPS, replica lag.- DynamoDB — consumed read/write capacity, throttles. Metrics are stored per namespace (e.g., ) with dimensions (e.g., ). Each is a time series of values. Custom metrics For Lambdas, Embedded Metric Format (EMF) is cheaper — write a specific JSON line to logs, CloudWatch extracts it as a metric automatically. Chapter 2 — CloudWatch Logs Every Lambda, ECS task, EC2 (with CloudWatch Agent), API Gateway, RDS (optional), and ALB (optional) writes logs to log groups. - Group: or .- Stream: one per execution / container / day.- Each log event has a timestamp + message. Retention The default retention is forever — costly over time. Set retention on every log group: 7, 14, 30, or 90 days are typical. A chatty Lambda producing 5 MB/min over a month = USD 1.30/GB × 200 GB = USD 260. Demote to for prod, sample noisy lines, and set short retention. Chapter 3 — Alarms A CloudWatch alarm watches a metric and notifies (usually via SNS) when a threshold is breached. Chapter 4 — Dashboards CloudWatch dashboards combine metrics + logs widgets into a single page. Reasonable starter set: - Service KPI tile: request rate, p50 / p95 latency, 5xx count.- Resource utilisation row: CPU, memory, errors per service.- Cost tile: estimated charges + month-over-month.- Dependency row: DB CPU, queue depth, cache hit rate. For multi-account visibility, CloudWatch cross-account observability lets one monitoring account aggregate metrics + logs from many workload accounts. Alternatives Datadog, Grafana / Prometheus, New Relic, Honeycomb — all integrate with CloudWatch. Pick whichever your team uses if it's already paid for. Pure-AWS dashboards are fine to start. Chapter 5 — X-Ray (distributed tracing) X-Ray traces a request as it hops between services (API Gateway → Lambda → DynamoDB → SNS). For each segment: start time, end time, latency, errors. Find bottlenecks in seconds. Enable per service:- Lambda: tick "Active tracing" or .- API Gateway: enable in stage settings.- ECS: add the X-Ray daemon container. In the SDK, instrument client calls: X-Ray is being superseded by the newer AWS Distro for OpenTelemetry (ADOT) for cross-cloud + OSS compatibility. For pure AWS, X-Ray is still the simplest start. Chapter 6 — CloudTrail (audit log) CloudTrail records every API call in your account: who, when, what, from where. By default, a free 90-day event history is available in the console. For longer retention and analytical use, configure a trail that writes to S3 + optionally CloudWatch Logs. What CloudTrail tells you - Who created / deleted resources.- Who assumed a role.- Failed authentication attempts.- Console logins (with MFA status).- Source IPs of API calls. Couple with Athena to query CloudTrail logs in S3 cheaply. Chapter 7 — Systems Manager (SSM) SSM is a Swiss-army-knife. This post focuses on the four most common: RDS (managed relational), Aurora (RDS's cloud-native superset), DynamoDB (managed NoSQL key-value / document), and ElastiCache (managed Redis / Memcached). Chapter 1 — Pick the right data store Quick decision tree: The biggest mistake is choosing by familiarity rather than fit. Chapter 2 — RDS RDS (Relational Database Service) runs PostgreSQL, MySQL, MariaDB, Oracle, or SQL Server on EC2 + EBS, managed by AWS. You get: - Daily automated backups + point-in-time recovery (1-35 days).- Multi-AZ failover (synchronous replica in another AZ; automatic promotion).- Up to 15 read replicas for read scaling.- Maintenance windows for patching.- IAM authentication, KMS encryption at rest, TLS in transit. What you give up: full OS access. You can't run on an RDS host or install custom Postgres extensions outside AWS's approved list. Chapter 4 — DynamoDB DynamoDB is a fully managed NoSQL key-value + document store. Single-digit millisecond latency at any scale. No servers to provision, no AZ design — AWS handles all of it. A single EC2 in a single AZ is a single point of failure. The third post in the series puts the compute from Post 02 into the network from Post 01 and turns it into a system that survives an AZ outage and a traffic spike without human intervention. Chapter 1 — The load balancer family | LB | Layer | Protocols | When ||----|-------|-----------|------|| Application LB (ALB) | 7 | HTTP, HTTPS, WebSocket, gRPC | web APIs, microservices, host/path routing || Network LB (NLB) | 4 | TCP, UDP, TLS | extreme throughput, static IP, non-HTTP || Gateway LB (GLB) | 3 | IP via GENEVE | inserting 3rd-party network appliances || Classic LB (CLB) | 4 + 7 (legacy) | HTTP, TCP | only existing deployments | Most workloads use ALB. A minimal ASG runs on first boot — install your app, fetch config, register with discovery. Chapter 5 — Scaling policies Target tracking (start here) Pick a metric and a target; AWS adds/removes instances to keep the metric there. - "Average CPU = 50%" → scale out when > 50, in when < 50.- "ALB requests per target = 200" → scale to meet load. Step scaling Trigger at thresholds; add/remove a configurable count per step. Use when target tracking can't express the logic — e.g., asymmetric scale-out vs scale-in speeds. Scheduled Predictable patterns deserve scheduled scaling — flash sale, market open, weekday morning ramp. Trick: schedule warm-up 15 minutes before a known spike. EC2 + ASG instances take 1-3 minutes from request to "in service". Chapter 6 — Cooldowns, warm pools, instance refresh Cooldown A waiting period after a scaling action before evaluating again — prevents oscillation when metrics dip slightly post-action. Warm pool A pool of pre-initialised but stopped instances. When the ASG needs to scale, it starts (rather than provisions) one — seconds vs minutes. Cost: EBS storage of the warm instances. Instance refresh When you publish a new launch-template version (new AMI, new user-data), rolls existing instances out in batches. Pair with ALB weighted target groups or canary listener rules for safe rollouts. Chapter 7 — The textbook 3-tier reference Tier rules:- ALB lives in public subnets across ≥ 2 AZs.- App tier lives in private subnets across the same AZs.- DB tier lives in isolated subnets, accessible only from the app SG.- Each tier's security group only accepts traffic from the tier above's SG.- Outbound from app tier to internet via NAT (only for pulling packages, calling external APIs). This shape survives:- a single instance failure (ASG replaces it)- a single AZ failure (other AZs absorb traffic)- a deploy with bad code (canary catches it; instance refresh rolls back)- a traffic spike (ASG scales out) Chapter 8 — Cost considerations | Item | Approx (us-east-1) ||------|---------------------|| ALB | USD 16 / month base + LCU hours || NLB | USD 16 / month + NLCU || Data transfer out | USD 0.09/GB after 100 GB free || EC2 t3.micro | USD 7.50 / month at 24/7 on-demand || NAT gateway | USD 32 / month + USD 0.045 / GB processed || RDS db.t4g.micro multi-AZ | USD 25-30 / month | A minimal 3-tier shop: ASG of 2 × t3.micro + ALB + RDS Multi-AZ + 1 NAT ≈ USD 100 / month before traffic. Per-AZ NATs add USD 32 each for HA. Watch out: ALB LCU (Load Balancer Capacity Units) is metered on connections, requests, processed bytes, and rule evaluations. A high-traffic ALB can cost more than the EC2 behind it. Chapter 9 — Beyond EC2 + ASG Modern compute alternatives to consider for the app tier: - ECS Fargate — containers without managing EC2 hosts.- EKS — Kubernetes managed control plane.- App Runner — push a container, AWS does the rest.- Lambda behind ALB — short-lived, infrequent endpoints (Post 05). EC2 + ASG remains right when you need OS access, GPU, NVMe, or want the lowest level of abstraction. Otherwise consider going up the stack — Post 05 covers serverless, future posts cover containers. AWS Course — 02 · Compute & Storage — EC2, EBS, S3

With the network in place (Post 01), the next step is to put workloads onto it. AWS's workhorse trio for "run code + keep data" is EC2 (virtual machines), EBS (block storage attached to them), and S3 (object storage anyone in the account can use over HTTPS). Understanding the cost, durability, and access characteristics of each is the foundation for every later service — RDS sits on EBS, Lambda packages and container images live in S3, and the load balancer in Post 03 fronts EC2. > Series map: 00 Foundations → 01 Network First → 02 Compute & Storage (this post) → 03 Scale & Survive → 04 Managed Data → 05 Serverless & Events → 06 Workflow Orchestration → 07 Observability & Security → 08 IaC. AWS Course — 01 · Network First — VPC, Subnets, Routing, Security Groups
Created2026-05-19|Cloud AWS
Real AWS workloads start with the network, not the server. Where a workload lives — which VPC, which subnet, behind which gateway, allowed by which security rules — decides far more about its security posture, cost, and failure behaviour than the choice of instance type. This post is the deep dive on VPC, the virtual data centre that hosts almost everything else in the series. > Series map: 00 Foundations → 01 Network First (this post) → 02 Compute & Storage → 03 Scale & Survive → 04 Managed Data → 05 Serverless & Events → 06 Workflow Orchestration → 07 Observability & Security → 08 Infrastructure as Code. Why network before compute A tempting shortcut is to "just launch an EC2" and figure out the network later. The cost: every later change (adding a database, fronting with a load balancer, going multi-AZ, locking down for security review) becomes a re-architecture. Network-first means picking a small set of decisions once and reusing them for the next decade. The decisions:- Which region.- VPC CIDR range (which private IPs you'll own).- Subnet layout (how many AZs, public/private/isolated).- Internet egress path (IGW + NAT or VPC endpoints).- Security group baseline (who can talk to whom). Learning Outcome - Design a VPC CIDR that won't collide with other accounts you might peer with.- Lay out public / private / isolated subnets across 3 AZs.- Configure route tables, internet gateways, NAT gateways, and VPC endpoints.- Write security groups and (rarely) NACLs that are tight without being painful.- Recognise the cost levers: NAT gateway, cross-AZ traffic, data egress. Chapter 1 — VPC anatomy A VPC is a software-defined private network inside one AWS region. Each AWS account gets a default VPC per region (); production accounts almost always create custom ones. | Construct | What ||-----------|------|| VPC | the boundary; one or more CIDR ranges (IPv4 + optional IPv6) || Subnet | a CIDR slice of the VPC, lives in exactly one AZ || Route table | per-subnet (or default) rules: "for destination X, next hop is Y" || Internet Gateway (IGW) | one per VPC; enables inbound + outbound public internet || NAT Gateway | enables outbound-only public internet from private subnets || VPC endpoint | reach AWS services privately, no IGW/NAT needed || Security group | stateful per-ENI firewall || NACL | stateless per-subnet firewall | CIDR sizing A (e.g., ) gives 65,536 addresses — enough for a serious production environment. An AZ outage takes down at most a third of capacity. Combined with a load balancer (Post 03), the service stays up.2. Blast radius for security. Public subnets only host things designed to face the internet. Application servers and data live one or two hops inward.3. Cost vs availability tradeoff. Chapter 5 — Security groups (your everyday firewall) A security group is stateful: if a request matches an outbound rule, the reply is automatically allowed (and vice versa). You only configure one direction, not both. Default behaviour: no inbound, all outbound. Rules contain:- Protocol (TCP / UDP / ICMP / all)- Port range- Source / destination (CIDR, another security group, or a prefix list) Reference SGs by ID, not by IP When both ends are AWS resources, reference the other side's security group rather than its IP range. As IPs change (autoscaling, recreation), the SG reference stays correct. Chapter 6 — NACLs (the backup layer) A Network ACL is stateless and applied per subnet. Default rules allow all inbound + outbound. Custom NACLs default to deny. NACLs are evaluated in order (rule numbers); the first match wins. In practice most teams don't touch NACLs — security groups do all the real work. Reserve NACLs for blanket per-subnet rules (e.g., "isolated subnets reject any outbound traffic at all") or defence-in-depth against compromised SG state.
