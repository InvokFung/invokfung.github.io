3D Printing Course — 02 · Slicing in Depth
Created2026-08-26|Engineering 3D Printing
The slicer is where almost all of your decisions actually live. The printer executes; the slicer decides. Yet most people use it as a box with a "quality" dropdown, which means the interesting settings — the ones that decide whether a part is strong, accurate, fast or pretty — are being chosen by a vendor who has never seen your part. This post opens the box: what the slicer computes, what the G-code it emits actually says, and what each of the two hundred settings is really controlling. The organising principle throughout is that a slicer setting is only worth changing if you can state the physical mechanism it acts through. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth (this post) → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - Describe the slicer's computation from mesh to G-code, stage by stage, and say which stage a given artefact comes from.- Read G-code fluently enough to find a layer change, a retraction, a temperature command and an extrusion, and to compute how much filament a move deposits.- Choose layer height with reference to nozzle diameter, and state precisely what layer height does and does not control.- Explain why extrusion width is decoupled from nozzle diameter, and use that decoupling deliberately.- Set perimeter count, perimeter order and seam placement from the mechanism rather than from habit.- Specify solid skins by thickness rather than by layer count.- Compare infill patterns honestly, separating what is measurable at hobby level from what is not.- Reason about speed and acceleration well enough to know when a speed setting is not being reached at all.- Use modifiers, per-region settings and the quality features (ironing, adaptive layers, fuzzy skin, Arachne) for the cases each was built for.- Maintain a profile discipline that makes a good print reproducible six months later. Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes Terminology - Mesh — the triangle soup the slicer receives. Must be manifold (watertight, every edge shared by exactly two faces, consistent normals) for slicing to be well defined.- Layer polygon — the set of closed 2D contours produced by intersecting the mesh with a horizontal plane at one Z. Outer contours are the part; inner contours are holes.- Offset (inset) — shrinking a polygon inward by a fixed distance. Perimeter generation is repeated offsetting.- Perimeter / wall — a closed extrusion loop following the layer's contour.- Skin / solid layer — fully dense region on a top or bottom surface.- Sparse infill — the low-density interior lattice.- Toolpath — an ordered sequence of moves with extrusion amounts attached.- Seam — the point where a closed perimeter loop starts and ends.- Extrusion width — the intended width of one deposited road. A slicer parameter, not a nozzle property. The pipeline Two things are worth noticing about this list. Step 3 is why mesh quality matters. A plane–mesh intersection only yields closed loops if the mesh is closed. A hole in the mesh gives an open polyline, which the slicer must guess how to close — and its guess is where the weird stray extrusion in the middle of your part comes from. Fix the model, not the slicer settings. Step 5 is why "top layers" behaves unintuitively. Solid skin is not a property of the top face; it is a boolean result computed per layer. A shallow dome has a top surface at nearly every layer, so it gets skin over a large part of its height, and that is why gently sloped surfaces are slow and material-hungry. Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width Layer height versus nozzle diameter The widely used rule of thumb is a practical ceiling of roughly 75–80 % of nozzle diameter — about 0.30–0.32 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical constant, and it exists for a mechanical reason worth understanding. The flat annular face around the nozzle orifice acts as a shoe. It rides above the previous layer at the layer height and irons the emerging extrudate down into it, which is what creates the wide contact patch and the pressure that drives interdiffusion (post 00). As layer height approaches nozzle diameter, the shoe stops touching the extrudate at all: material simply falls out of the orifice as a round noodle, keys poorly to the layer below, and stacks unstably. Caveats in both directions: - High-flow hotends and internally-restructured nozzles change the melting side of the problem but not the shoe geometry. Some users run higher ratios successfully; verify on your own machine before designing around it.- Going very thin has its own floor. Below roughly a quarter of nozzle diameter, small Z errors and bed-mesh residuals become a large fraction of the layer, and print time grows without a proportional gain in quality. The first-layer exception The first layer routinely ignores the rule and runs thicker — commonly 0.2–0.3 mm even in fine profiles — for two reasons: a thicker layer swallows small bed irregularities and mesh residuals, and it produces a larger contact patch for adhesion. It is also frequently printed wider than other layers for the same reason. What layer height does and does not control | Layer height controls | Layer height does not control ||---|---|| Visible stepping on sloped and curved surfaces | detail in the XY plane — that is nozzle diameter and extrusion width || The Z resolution of features (a 0.15 mm step cannot exist in a 0.2 mm layer) | hole roundness or XY dimensional accuracy || Print time — roughly inversely, but not exactly | wall thickness || The number of layer interfaces per millimetre of height | whether a part is strong (interface count and interface quality pull in opposite directions; see below) || Overhang behaviour, indirectly (each layer's unsupported step scales with height) | support requirement | On strength, resist the confident claim in either direction. Thinner layers give more interfaces but each one is smaller, cools differently and reheats more often. The published results are material- and geometry-dependent, and the effect is generally much smaller than the effect of orientation (post 03) or of wall count. Spend your attention there instead. Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter This surprises people, and it is the single most useful conceptual unlock in the slicer. The width of a deposited road is set by how much material you push per unit of travel and how high the nozzle is, not by the orifice diameter. Push more, and the extrudate spreads sideways under the shoe until it is wider. Push less, and it is narrower. The orifice only limits how fast you can push it. Usable range is roughly 100–200 % of nozzle diameter. Below about 100 %, the road becomes unreliable and the nozzle drags. Above about 200 %, the extrudate escapes past the shoe's control and the surface becomes lumpy. How to use the decoupling deliberately: | Setting | Typical choice | Why ||---|---|---|| First layer | wider than default | more contact patch, better adhesion, more tolerance of bed variation || External perimeter | at or slightly below nozzle diameter | narrower roads follow corners more tightly and give better dimensional control on the visible surface || Internal perimeters | above nozzle diameter (often 110–120 %) | fewer, fatter roads for the same wall thickness — faster and fewer internal interfaces || Solid infill | above nozzle diameter | fewer passes to fill an area || Sparse infill | wider still | infill is invisible; width buys speed directly || Support interface | narrower | finer surface where the part rests on it | > Common pitfall: setting an extrusion width and forgetting that flow calibration (post 01) targets a specific width. Change your external perimeter width and your measured wall thickness changes, which looks like a flow error and is not. Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill Perimeter count Wall thickness ≈ perimeter count × extrusion width. Two perimeters at 0.45 mm gives a 0.9 mm wall; four gives 1.8 mm. Perimeter count is usually a better investment than infill density for stiffness and strength, and the reason is elementary beam mechanics: in bending, material's contribution to stiffness scales with the square of its distance from the neutral axis. The walls are the material furthest from the neutral axis. Infill in the middle of the section contributes comparatively little. Going from two walls to four on a bracket typically does more than doubling infill, and costs less time and material. Typical practice: 2 walls for decorative parts, 3–4 for general functional parts, 5+ for parts under real load or that need to be watertight or tapped. Perimeter order Two orders, both defensible: Inner first, external last (the usual default). The external perimeter is laid against already-solid inner walls, so it has something to lean on, and nothing is printed afterwards to disturb it. Generally better surface quality and better behaviour on overhanging walls. External first. The outer wall is laid before any inner wall can push it outward, which can improve dimensional accuracy on some machines. Costs surface quality and overhang reliability. Slicers in the PrusaSlicer lineage expose this as an "external perimeters first" toggle, and some offer a hybrid ordering. The honest guidance is that the effect size is machine-dependent: print a test part both ways, measure it with calipers and look at it, and decide for your machine. Post 05 returns to the accuracy side of this. Seams Every closed loop must start and stop somewhere. At that point the extruder transitions from zero pressure to steady pressure and back, and the result is a small defect — a blob, a notch, or both — stacked on every layer. Seam placement strategies: | Strategy | Result ||---|---|| Nearest | seam goes wherever travel is shortest; fastest, scattered, unpredictable || Aligned | seams stack into a single vertical line; ugly but predictable, and you can rotate it to the back || Rear | forced to the back of the plate || Random | scattered; hides the line at the cost of scattering small defects across the whole surface || Painted | you choose, per region, in the slicer — put it inside a corner where geometry hides it | Mechanically, a stack of aligned seams is a line of small stress concentrations and reduced wall continuity, so on a pressure-bearing or load-bearing part, do not let the seam line up with the region of highest stress. Reducing the seam artefact itself: tune pressure advance (post 01), and use the slicer's wipe and coasting options, which stop extruding slightly before the end of the loop and move the nozzle to smear the remainder. Some slicers additionally offer a scarf or ramped seam that spreads the transition over a Z ramp instead of a single point; where available, it is the most effective option for a visible cylindrical surface. Gap fill When a region is too narrow for another whole perimeter but too wide to ignore, classic perimeter generation leaves a gap and fills it with a short, thin extrusion. Gap fill is a common source of noise, vibration, blobs and stuttering motion, because it is many tiny moves with rapid flow changes. The modern answer is variable-width extrusion (Chapter 6), which mostly eliminates the problem by widening the perimeters themselves to close the gap. Chapter 5 — Skins and infill The thickness-not-count rule Solid layers are specified in most slicers as a count, which is a trap. What matters physically is total thickness of solid material. Halving your layer height silently halves your top skin. The result is pillowing: the top surface sags into the infill voids and shows holes and bumps. The rule: specify a thickness, then derive the count. Around 0.8–1.2 mm of top solid is a sensible general target; more if infill density is low, because thin skin has to bridge longer unsupported spans. Modern slicers have a "minimum shell thickness" setting that enforces this automatically — turn it on. Bottom skins can be thinner, because the build plate supports them. Monotonic ordering for the top solid layer is worth enabling where available: it forces solid infill lines to be laid in a consistent direction so each new road overlaps the previous one the same way, producing a visually uniform surface instead of a patchwork. Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features Per-feature speeds Slicers set a different speed for external perimeters, internal perimeters, solid infill, sparse infill, top solid, bridges, gap fill and travel. The important asymmetry: Outer-wall speed dominates surface quality, and costs very little total time. The external perimeter is the only extrusion anyone sees; it is where ringing lands, where pressure-advance error is visible, and where corner rounding shows. After the top solid layer is complete, the nozzle sweeps the surface again at very low flow, remelting and smoothing it. Produces a genuinely flat, near-glossy top face. Costs time proportional to top-surface area, and can push a bead of material to the edges if flow is too high. Only applies to flat, horizontal top surfaces. Adaptive layer height. Layer height varies with local surface slope — thin layers where the surface is shallow (where stepping is visible), thick where it is vertical (where stepping is invisible). The outer wall is deliberately jittered in XY by a small random amount, giving a matte, textured surface. It hides layer lines, hides seams, and improves grip on handles. Costs a little time and a little dimensional precision on the fuzzed surface — never fuzz a mating face. Better slicers let you paint it onto selected surfaces only. Variable-width extrusion (Arachne). The perimeter generator that, instead of laying fixed-width loops and dumping the remainder into gap fill, varies each loop's width to fill the available space exactly. Thin walls, tapering ribs, embossed text and small features all improve substantially. Chapter 4 — The TLS 1.3 handshake TLS (Transport Layer Security) gives three things: confidentiality (only the two ends can read), integrity (changes on the way are detected), and authentication (you reached the real ). TLS 1.3 was defined in RFC 8446 (August 2018). In July 2026 the IETF published RFC 9846, which obsoletes RFC 8446. The client guesses which key-exchange group the server accepts and sends a public key for it at once. This guess is what saves a round trip compared with TLS 1.2.2. ServerHello. The server sends its own public key. Both sides now run ECDHE (Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman, Ephemeral): each mixes its private key with the other's public key and gets the same shared secret. Someone watching the wire cannot compute it.3. Keys are derived from that secret. Everything after ServerHello is encrypted, including the certificate.4. CertificateVerify. Each side sends a MAC over the whole transcript. If an attacker changed any earlier message, the MACs do not match and the connection dies.6. Chapter 1 — The first layer: doctrine, tramming and Z-offset Terminology - Tramming — physically adjusting the bed so its plane is parallel to the plane the nozzle travels in. Often loosely called "levelling", which is misleading: gravity is irrelevant, parallelism is everything.- Z-offset — the distance between the point the printer calls Z = 0 and the point where the nozzle actually touches the bed. A single scalar, and the most consequential number on the machine.- Nozzle-to-bed gap — the physical clearance while printing the first layer. Deliberately smaller than the first-layer height, so that material is squashed.- Squish — the intentional compression of the first layer. It is what converts a round extrudate into a flat road with a wide contact patch.- Mesh bed levelling — probing a grid of points and compensating for bed shape by modulating Z during the print.- Probe repeatability — the spread of readings when the probe measures the same point many times. The floor on how good your mesh can be.- Elephant foot — the outward bulge at the bottom of a part caused by excessive first-layer squish, excessive bed temperature, or both. Why the first layer decides everything Three separate mechanisms, all rooted in the same event: 1. Adhesion is contact area. A squashed road has a wide flat contact patch against the plate; an unsquashed one touches along a line. Adhesion force scales with that contact patch, so a nozzle 50 µm too high can halve it.2. The first layer is the datum for every layer above it. Layer 2 is deposited onto layer 1 at a fixed Z step. If layer 1 is thinner than commanded, layer 2 is deposited into empty space and the error propagates upward as under-extrusion that never recovers. If layer 1 is thicker than commanded, layer 2 collides with it.3. Warping starts at the corners of the first layer. As upper layers cool and contract, they pull inward on the part. That force is resisted only by the bond between first layer and plate. A weak first layer does not resist it, so the corner lifts, and once a corner lifts the nozzle starts hitting the raised edge. The operational rule: watch the first layer finish before you leave the room. Nothing else in this hobby has a better ratio of attention spent to failures avoided. Step 1: mechanical prerequisites, before any of this Levelling compensates for shape. It cannot compensate for movement. Before touching Z-offset: - Check that the bed does not rock. Press each corner; if it moves, springs are too loose or the mounts are worn.- Check belt tension (X and Y): a belt should feel taut, and plucking it should give a defined note rather than a dull thud. Loose belts show up later as ringing and dimensional error that no calibration will fix.- Check that the gantry is square and, on dual-Z machines, that both Z motors are synchronised — Marlin's and Klipper's / exist for exactly this.- Check the nozzle is clean and cold-pulled free of debris. A blob of burnt plastic on the nozzle tip makes every subsequent measurement meaningless.- Heat everything to printing temperature before probing. Beds change shape by a measurable amount between cold and 60 °C; probes drift with temperature. Probing cold and printing hot is a common self-inflicted wound. Step 2: tram the bed Even with automatic levelling, the bed should be physically parallel to within a few tenths of a millimetre. On a four-screw bed the classic procedure is manual and works: 1. Heat bed and nozzle to the temperatures you will print at.2. Home all axes, then disable the steppers' Z compensation ( on Marlin, or simply skip mesh loading).3. Use them if available — they replace a subjective feel with a number. Step 3: set the Z-offset Two methods, both legitimate: The paper method. A sheet of ordinary office paper is roughly 0.1 mm thick, which is conveniently close to a good first-layer gap. Lower Z in 0.02–0.05 mm steps until the paper can still be moved but drags noticeably. The weakness is that "drags noticeably" is subjective, that paper thickness varies by brand, and that the paper compresses under the nozzle. A feeler gauge. Step 4: mesh bed levelling, and its limits A probe measures the bed at a grid of points and builds a height map. During printing, the firmware adds a small Z correction as a function of X and Y so the nozzle follows the bed's actual shape. A single fingerprint in the middle of the plate produces a print that adheres everywhere except a palm-sized region, and lifts there. Handle the sheet by its edges. Chapter 3 — Reading a first-layer test print Print a large single-layer patch — most slicers can do this, and dedicated first-layer test models are common. Look at it in raking light and, if in doubt, put a caliper on it. Too high (gap too large) - Individual extrusion lines are visibly separate, with valleys between them; you can see the plate through the gaps.- Each road is round-topped rather than flat-topped.- Poor adhesion: the patch can be lifted with a fingernail, or curls at the corners.- The surface feels ribbed.- Measured thickness is approximately the commanded first-layer height (no squish) or slightly less. Too low (gap too small) - Roads are pressed so wide they overlap, and excess material is pushed up as ridges between the lines.- Surface looks translucent, burnished or scratched; on textured plates the texture is smeared.- Audible scraping, and a visible line on the plate.- Extrusion becomes inconsistent because back-pressure is fighting the extruder — sometimes with under-extrusion appearing several centimetres later.- Downstream: pronounced elephant foot on real parts.- Measured thickness is noticeably below the commanded first-layer height. Correct - Adjacent roads have merged into a continuous sheet with no gaps and no raised ridges.- Line boundaries are faintly visible but the surface is flat.- The patch is uniformly matte (textured plate) or uniformly glossy (smooth plate) — uniformity is the signal, not the finish itself.- The patch peels off the cooled plate in one piece with moderate effort and shows a clean, evenly-textured underside.- Caliper reading is close to, and slightly under, the commanded first-layer height. > Common pitfall: judging the first layer at the centre of the plate only. Check all four corners and the centre. Uniformly wrong is a Z-offset problem; wrong in one region only is a tram/mesh problem. They have completely different fixes. Chapter 4 — The calibration sequence Do these in order. Each step's result depends on all the previous ones, so a step done out of order will have to be redone. 5.0 — Prerequisites Mechanical checks from Chapter 1, a trammed bed, a clean plate, dry filament, and a known-good vendor profile as the starting point. Do not begin with a profile you found on the internet. 5.1 — Extruder rotation distance / e-steps What it fixes: the machine delivering a different length of filament than it was asked for. Everything downstream is scaled by this error, so it is first. Procedure: 1. Heat the nozzle to printing temperature (the extruder must be able to actually push).2. Put the extruder in relative mode and disable any modifiers.3. Mark the filament at a known distance above the extruder inlet — 120 mm is convenient because it leaves 20 mm after a 100 mm extrusion.4. Command a slow 100 mm extrusion. Slow matters: fast extrusion can skip or grind and will corrupt the measurement.5. Measure the remaining distance from the inlet to the mark with calipers. Actual extruded = 120 − remaining.6. Repeat three times and average. Applying the result: Note the inversion: e-steps are steps per millimetre, rotation distance is millimetres per rotation, so the correction goes the opposite way. Getting this backwards doubles your error, which is an obvious enough result that you will notice. Acceptance: repeat the test after applying. Actual extruded should land within roughly a millimetre of 100 mm. If it does not converge, the drive gear is slipping, the tension is wrong, or the filament path has a restriction. Scope: this is a per-printer value. It does not change with filament brand. 5.2 — Temperature tower What it fixes: picking a nozzle temperature by evidence rather than by the number printed on the spool. Procedure: print a tower whose temperature drops (or rises) by 5 °C per block, using either a pre-built model with embedded temperature changes or a slicer height-triggered custom G-code ( at the layer where each block starts). What you evaluate on each block, and what each tells you: | Observation | Meaning ||---|---|| Layer separation when you try to snap the block | too cold — insufficient interdiffusion (post 00, Chapter 3) || Stringing between features | too hot, or a retraction problem (isolate later, not now) || Blobbing, drooping overhangs, glossy over-melted surface | too hot || Poor bridging, curled bridge strands | too cold, or too little cooling || Rough, matte, under-fused surface | too cold | Decision rule: do not just pick the prettiest block. Pick for the property the part needs. Functional part → the hottest temperature that does not string, droop or degrade, because layer bonding improves with temperature. Visual part → the coolest temperature that still fuses cleanly. Write the choice and the reason down. Scope: per material, per spool. Pigment loading alone shifts the optimum; a black and a white PLA from the same manufacturer regularly want different temperatures. 5.3 — Flow / extrusion multiplier What it fixes: the difference between the volume the slicer thinks it deposited and the volume actually deposited. Causes include filament diameter variation, nozzle wear, and material-specific die swell. Procedure (single-wall cube): 1. Slice a simple cube (or the standard single-wall calibration cube) with one perimeter, zero top layers, zero infill, and no gap fill. Vase mode also works.2. Note the extrusion width the slicer used for that wall — this is the target, and it is not the nozzle diameter (post 02 explains why).3. Print it at your chosen temperature.4. Measure the wall thickness with calipers at eight or more places — different heights, different faces, avoiding the seam and the corners. Use light, consistent pressure; plastic deforms under a caliper jaw.5. Average the measurements. Applying the result: Acceptance: reprint and remeasure. The spread between your individual measurements tells you how much precision you actually have — if measurements vary by more than a couple of hundredths of a millimetre, your measurement technique or the machine is the limiting factor, not the flow number. Modern slicers ship guided flow calibrations (OrcaSlicer's two-pass flow-rate test, for example) that use a printed surface-quality judgement instead of a caliper. They are faster and less prone to caliper-pressure error; the single-wall method remains the one that produces a number you can defend. Scope: per spool. This is the setting most worth re-checking on every new roll. 5.4 — Retraction What it fixes: oozing during travel moves, which shows as stringing, blobs and zits. Procedure: print a retraction tower or a two-pillar stringing test, stepping one variable per tower. Tune in this order: distance, then speed, then extra restart distance / wipe / Z-hop if still needed. Typical starting ranges — these depend on melt-zone volume, extruder architecture and Bowden tube length, so treat them as where to begin the tower, not as answers: | Setup | Retraction distance, typical starting range ||---|---|| Direct drive | roughly 0.5–2 mm || Bowden | roughly 3–7 mm, longer with a longer tube | Acceptance: the shortest retraction distance that eliminates strings. Over-retraction is not free: it pulls molten material back into the cold zone where it can jam, grinds a groove into the filament on repeated retractions at the same point, and causes gaps at the start of each extrusion. > Watch out: stringing has three plausible causes — too little retraction, too high a temperature, and wet filament. Wet filament strings and pops audibly and will defeat every retraction value you try. If you hear crackling from the nozzle, dry the filament before tuning anything. Scope: per material. 5.5 — Pressure advance / linear advance What it fixes: the lag between commanded flow and actual flow caused by pressure building and releasing in the melt zone. Symptoms are bulges after a deceleration and thin, starved lines after an acceleration — most visible at corners and at the start of each line. Klipper calls it pressure advance; Marlin calls it linear advance with a K factor. Same physics, different scaling, so the numbers are not interchangeable. Scope: per material (and re-check if you change hotend or extruder). 5.6 — Maximum volumetric flow What it fixes: the ceiling on print speed. Above it, the hotend cannot melt filament fast enough and the print silently under-extrudes. Procedure: print a flow-ramp test that increases volumetric flow with height — OrcaSlicer's "Max Volumetric Speed" test does this directly, and equivalent speed towers exist for other slicers. A typical sweep starts around 5 mm³/s and climbs in small steps. Reading it: find the height where the surface changes character — extrusion becomes visibly inconsistent, the finish shifts from even to rough or unevenly glossy, or the wall thins. That height's flow is the point of failure, not the working limit. Applying the result: set your profile's maximum volumetric speed 10–20 % below the failure point. The test is a best case: it does not include retractions, sharp corners, small layers or an already-hot enclosure. Dependency: this number moves with nozzle temperature. If you later raise the temperature, the ceiling rises too — and if you re-run 5.2, re-run this. Scope: per material, per hotend, per nozzle. 5.7 — Cooling What it fixes: overhang quality, bridging, small-feature definition — traded directly against layer adhesion (post 00, Chapter 3). Procedure: print an overhang/bridging test at several fan settings, or a small tower where fan speed steps with height. Also set minimum layer time (or "slow down for short layers"), which forces small cross-sections to print slowly enough to solidify. Starting points by material, all subject to measurement: | Material | Part cooling, typical starting point | Reason ||---|---|---|| PLA | high, often near maximum | crystallises quickly; benefits from fast solidification; layer adhesion stays adequate || PETG | moderate | needs some cooling for overhangs, but too much visibly weakens layer bonding || ABS / ASA | minimal or none, in an enclosure | high shrinkage; rapid cooling causes warping and delamination || TPU | low to moderate | slow-flowing; cooling helps definition but the material is tolerant || Nylon (PA) | low | strong, hygroscopic, and very sensitive to rapid cooling || PC | low | high glass transition; needs a warm environment to bond | > Trick: cooling is the one setting where "more is better" is actively wrong for functional parts. If a bracket keeps snapping along a layer line, drop the fan before you change anything else. Scope: per material. Chapter 5 — Discipline: per spool, and one variable at a time Why calibration is per spool, not per printer Split your settings into two buckets: | Belongs to the printer | Belongs to the spool / material ||---|---|| e-steps / rotation distance | nozzle temperature || XY and Z steps per mm | bed temperature || input shaper frequencies | flow / extrusion multiplier || bed mesh | retraction distance and speed || probe offsets | pressure advance || kinematic limits (max accel, jerk/square corner velocity) | maximum volumetric flow || — | cooling and minimum layer time | The right-hand column moves because filament varies. Diameter tolerance differs between manufacturers and between batches. Pigments and fillers change melt viscosity, thermal conductivity and optimum temperature. Moisture content changes everything at once. Even two spools of nominally identical material from one vendor can want a different flow number. Practically: create a slicer filament profile per spool type you buy, not per material category, and put the measured numbers in it. The tuning-order rule Never chase two variables at once. If you change temperature and flow together and the result improves, you have learned nothing you can reuse. The sequence in Chapter 4 is ordered by dependency: rotation distance scales all extrusion, temperature changes flow behaviour and the flow ceiling, flow changes how corners look, pressure advance is judged on corner appearance, and cooling changes both bonding and overhang quality. Keep a notebook — a text file is fine. For each change record: date, printer, spool, the setting changed, the old value, the new value, the test used, and the observation. Six months later this file is the only thing that distinguishes "my printer got worse" from "I changed something in March". What additive is genuinely good at The honest list is shorter than the marketing list: 1. Geometric complexity is free. Chapter 3 — The physics of FDM Terminology - Melt zone — the region inside the hotend where the filament is above its melting or softening point and behaves as a viscous fluid.- Heatbreak — the deliberately thin-walled, low-conductivity section between the heatsink and the heater block that keeps the melt zone short and well defined.- Heat creep — failure mode where the melt zone migrates upward past the heatbreak, softening filament in the cold section and jamming it.- Extrudate — the material that has left the nozzle.- Road / extrusion — one deposited line of extrudate. Its cross-section is roughly a rectangle with rounded ends, width × layer height.- Weld / interface — the boundary between two roads, either side by side within a layer or stacked between layers.- Volumetric flow — the rate at which the hotend can melt and deliver material, in mm³/s. The real speed limit of the machine. The filament is a plunger The extruder gears grip the filament and push it into the hotend. Solid filament above the melt zone acts as a piston; the molten polymer below it is the working fluid. This is a positive-displacement pump, and the consequences are worth stating explicitly: - Flow is commanded by distance, not by pressure. The firmware asks for "extrude 2.31 mm of filament", and the volume delivered is that length times the filament cross-section. If the filament diameter is not what the slicer assumed, every extrusion in the file is wrong by the same ratio.- The system is compliant. Molten polymer compresses slightly, the filament itself buckles slightly, the PTFE liner or Bowden tube stretches. Pressure therefore lags the commanded flow — which is exactly the problem that pressure advance / linear advance exists to correct (post 01).- The grip is the weak link. If the drive gear cannot push hard enough, it grinds a flat into the filament and stops gripping. Under-extrusion that gets progressively worse over minutes is nearly always this. Melting, and why volumetric flow is the real limit Heat has to travel from the heater block into the filament core by conduction, and polymers are poor thermal conductors. The maximum flow the hotend supports is set by how much filament it can bring to temperature per second — which depends on melt-zone length, nozzle temperature, and the material's own thermal properties. This is why the same hotend will happily run PLA far faster than PETG, and why raising nozzle temperature raises attainable flow. Practically: your printer does not have a "maximum speed" in mm/s. It has a maximum volumetric flow in mm³/s, and speed follows from it. If that number exceeds what the hotend can melt, the print under-extrudes no matter what the slicer says. Post 01 gives the measurement procedure for finding your own machine's ceiling; do not trust a headline figure from a product page, because it is measured with a specific material at a specific temperature that is probably not yours. Layer bonding is polymer chain diffusion This is the single most important piece of physics in the course. When a hot road is laid onto a cooler road below it, the two do not glue together and they do not melt into a homogeneous mass. What happens is interdiffusion: polymer chains near the interface, if they are above the glass transition and mobile enough, wriggle across the boundary by reptation — snake-like motion along the tube formed by neighbouring chains — and entangle with chains on the other side. The strength of the weld is a function of how many chains crossed and how far, which is a function of interface temperature and time above that temperature. The classic treatment is Wool and O'Connor's crack-healing theory, and it maps onto FDM almost directly. The engineering consequences follow immediately: - Hotter nozzle → stronger layer bond, up to the point where the material degrades or the part slumps. This is a real, repeatable lever.- More cooling → weaker layer bond. Part cooling fans buy overhang quality and surface detail and pay for it in Z strength. Post 04 and post 07 return to this trade.- Smaller layer height → more interfaces per millimetre, but each interface is also smaller and the part reheats more often. The net effect on strength is material-dependent and genuinely contested; do not repeat a blanket claim in either direction.- An enclosure raises ambient temperature, which slows cooling at the interface and improves bonding. This is most of why ABS, ASA and PC want an enclosure.- Long print times per layer let the previous layer cool further. A tall thin part and a tall wide part with identical settings do not have identical layer adhesion. Anisotropy, stated properly A load applied along the roads is carried by polymer chains that run continuously through the extrudate — essentially the bulk strength of the material, minus voids. A load applied across the layers is carried only by the chains that diffused across each weld interface, plus whatever mechanical interlock the road geometry provides. The Z direction is therefore always weaker, and the failure mode is different too: in-plane failure tends to be ductile yielding, while interlayer failure tends to be a clean, brittle-looking separation along a layer line. How much weaker? Published figures for Z-direction tensile strength as a fraction of in-plane strength scatter enormously — across materials, nozzle temperatures, layer heights, cooling settings, enclosure conditions and test-coupon geometry. Treat any single percentage you read as a statement about that study's conditions, not about your printer. What is robust, and what you should carry forward, is the ordering: in-plane strong, interlayer weak, and the gap is large enough to change the design. > Common pitfall: printing a hook, a lever or a bracket flat on the bed because it "fits better that way", so that the working load pulls the layers apart. This is the most common structural failure in hobby printing and it is entirely an orientation decision. Post 03 works the example through in detail. Chapter 4 — Printer anatomy The extruder: direct drive versus Bowden The extruder is the geared drive that grips and pushes the filament. Two architectures: Direct drive puts the extruder motor on the moving toolhead, immediately above the hotend. The filament path from gear to melt zone is short — typically tens of millimetres. Bowden puts the motor on the frame and pushes filament through a PTFE tube, often several hundred millimetres, to a hotend that carries no motor. | | Direct drive | Bowden ||---|---|---|| Toolhead mass | higher | lower || Achievable acceleration before ringing | lower | higher || Retraction distance needed | short (roughly a couple of millimetres) | long (often several times more) || Responsiveness to flow changes | good | sluggish; more pressure-advance compensation needed || Flexible filament (TPU) | workable | difficult to painful || Maintenance access | motor in the hot, moving area | motor accessible, tube is a wear item | Neither is "better". Direct drive trades acceleration for control; Bowden trades control for acceleration. Modern direct-drive extruders have got light enough that the historical acceleration argument is much weaker than it was. The hotend Stacked from cold to hot: heatsink (with a fan that must never be switched off while hot), heatbreak, heater block carrying a cartridge heater and a thermistor, and the nozzle. The heatbreak's whole job is to make the transition from cold to molten as short as possible. A long, sloppy transition zone means a long column of soft filament that behaves like a spring, degrading extrusion control, and it invites heat creep. Two hotend classes matter: - PTFE-lined: a PTFE tube runs all the way down to the nozzle. Cheap, forgiving, excellent for PLA and PETG. The ceiling is the PTFE itself, which begins to degrade at elevated temperature and releases decomposition products; the widely used practical limit is around 240–250 °C, and going above it is both a part-quality and an air-quality problem.- All-metal: no PTFE in the hot zone, so high-temperature materials become possible. In exchange the melt zone is less well constrained and heat creep is a more real risk, particularly with materials that soften gradually. The nozzle - Diameter sets the minimum feature width and, with layer height, the volumetric flow available. 0.4 mm is the default for good reasons: it balances detail against speed. 0.2 mm buys detail and costs enormous print time; 0.6 and 0.8 mm buy speed and strength on functional parts and cost fine detail.- Material matters as soon as you print composites. Brass conducts heat well and is soft; any filament loaded with carbon fibre, glass fibre, metal powder or glow-in-the-dark phosphor is abrasive and will wear a brass nozzle out of round in hours. Hardened steel, tool steel or a carbide/ruby-tipped nozzle is required for those, at a small cost in thermal conductivity.- Wear is invisible until it is not. A worn nozzle prints wider than commanded and loses corner definition. If dimensional behaviour drifts over months, measure the nozzle orifice or simply replace it — nozzles are consumables. The bed and adhesion surfaces The bed must be flat, at a controlled temperature, and chemically willing to hold the first layer and then let it go. Common surfaces, covered properly in post 01: - Smooth PEI — strong, chemically-driven adhesion; glossy first-layer finish; can hold some materials (PETG especially) hard enough to damage the sheet without a release agent.- Textured PEI — mechanical keying rather than pure chemical grip; matte first layer that hides imperfections; releases much more willingly as the bed cools. The forgiving default.- Glass — extremely flat, usually needs an adhesive (glue stick, hairspray); prints release as the glass cools.- Garolite / FR4 — the traditional answer for nylon, which sticks to it well and to most other things poorly. Removable spring-steel sheets on a magnetic base have become the norm because flexing the sheet is the single most reliable release mechanism. Motion systems | System | How it moves | Consequences ||---|---|---|| Bedslinger (Cartesian i3) | bed moves in Y, gantry carries X, Z lifts the gantry | simple, cheap, easy to service. Moving mass in Y includes the part, so ringing and accuracy get worse as the print gets taller and heavier. Tall thin parts are its weakness. || CoreXY | two frame-mounted motors drive both X and Y through a crossed belt path; the bed only moves in Z | constant, low moving mass regardless of part size; supports much higher acceleration; the natural choice for fast machines. Belt path is longer and belt tension/squareness matter more. || Delta | three vertical towers with arms meeting at an effector | very low moving mass, very high speed potential, tall cylindrical build volume. Position is a trigonometric function of three carriages, so calibration is fussy and accuracy varies across the bed. | Ringing (also called ghosting) is the ripple you see downstream of a sharp corner: the toolhead's acceleration step excites a mechanical resonance in the frame and gantry, and the vibration is printed into the surface. It is a function of moving mass, frame stiffness, belt tension and acceleration. The modern fix is input shaping — the firmware pre-shapes the motion command to cancel the dominant resonance, with the resonant frequency measured using an accelerometer. Klipper implements this as ; Marlin has its own input-shaping implementation in recent versions. Either way it is a measurement, not a preset: the frequency belongs to your machine. Chapter 5 — The resin process, and its safety requirements How MSLA actually works 1. The build plate descends into a vat of liquid photopolymer until the gap between plate (or the last cured layer) and the transparent film at the bottom equals one layer height.2. A UV LED array beneath the vat illuminates an LCD panel. The panel is driven as a mask: pixels for the current cross-section are transparent, everything else is opaque.3. The resin in the illuminated gap crosslinks and cures for the programmed exposure time. Because the whole layer is exposed at once, exposure time is independent of layer area — a full-plate layer takes the same time as a single tiny part.4. The plate lifts, peeling the cured layer off the film, then returns for the next layer. Cure chemistry is free-radical or cationic photopolymerisation initiated by UV, on desktop machines typically around the 405 nm range. The correct exposure time is a property of the resin, the panel and the layer height together, so it must be found by test — printed exposure-test models are the standard method, and every resin change means a new test. Lift and peel forces — the real difficulty The cured layer is stuck to two things: the previous layer (which is what you want) and the release film at the bottom of the vat (which you do not). Separating it requires overcoming adhesion and the suction created as resin has to flow in behind the retreating part. Peel force scales with the cross-sectional area being separated, which is why: - Large flat cross-sections parallel to the plate are the worst case, and are why parts are usually tilted rather than laid flat.- Hollowing a model with adequate drain holes dramatically reduces both peel force and resin consumption. Without drain holes, a hollow shell becomes a suction cup and can pull itself off the plate or deform.- Slow lift speed, a lift height that clears the part, and a light-off delay (a pause before exposure that lets resin flow back into the gap) all buy reliability at the cost of print time.- Failures show up as layers torn off and left stuck to the film, a part detached from the supports, or a "layer shift" that is really a partial delamination. Bottom layers get a much longer exposure to key the part to the plate. Over-exposing them causes elephant foot in exactly the same way that a too-low first layer does on FDM. Post-processing is part of the process, not an afterthought Drain the part, wash it in isopropyl alcohol (or water for water-washable resins) to remove uncured surface resin, dry it, then post-cure it under UV. Post-cure is not cosmetic — mechanical properties and, to a small degree, dimensions change between the green state and the fully cured state. Over-curing makes many resins more brittle. Safety — the non-negotiable part Uncured photopolymer resin is a skin and respiratory irritant, and formulations commonly contain sensitisers: substances where repeated exposure can trigger an allergic response that becomes progressively worse and can be effectively permanent. This is not a "be a bit careful" hazard; it is a "the third exposure can be much worse than the first" hazard, and it is the reason experienced resin users are strict about it. The working rules: - Nitrile gloves, always. Not latex — latex is not an adequate barrier for these chemistries. Change them when contaminated; do not touch door handles, phones or keyboards while gloved.- Eye protection when handling the vat, decanting resin, or working with a wash tank.- Ventilation. A dedicated ventilated space, extracted outdoors where possible. A closed cupboard in a bedroom is not ventilation. Enclosures with carbon filters reduce odour more reliably than they reduce hazard.- Skin contact → soap and warm water immediately. Do not wash resin off with IPA or any other solvent; solvents carry the resin into the skin rather than off it.- Read the SDS for the specific resin. Formulations differ substantially between vendors and even between product lines from one vendor, and the safety data sheet is the authoritative document.- Waste disposal. Never pour resin, resin-contaminated IPA or wash water down a drain. Cure liquid waste to a solid under UV or sunlight, then dispose of the solid according to local regulations. Contaminated IPA can be cured, the solids filtered out, and the IPA reused. Paper towels, gloves and failed prints are all contaminated waste until cured.- Keep it out of food areas and away from children and pets, permanently. > Watch out: the largest exposure risk with resin is not printing; it is the wash and cleanup step, where hands are near liquid resin and solvent for extended periods. Set that station up properly before the first print, not after the first mess. Chapter 6 — The pipeline: CAD → mesh → slicer → G-code → printer Every post in this series lives at one stage of this chain. Knowing which stage a problem belongs to is most of debugging. Stage by stage CAD holds exact geometry. A cylinder is a mathematical cylinder. Post 06 covers CAD for printing properly. Mesh export is lossy and irreversible. The solid is tessellated into triangles, and the tessellation tolerance you choose at export decides how faceted your curves are. Export too coarse and a 20 mm hole visibly becomes a polygon; export absurdly fine and you get a slow, enormous file for no benefit. - STL stores nothing but a bag of triangles. No units, no colour, no materials, no assembly structure, no metadata. The unit ambiguity is why models occasionally arrive at 1/25.4 scale.- 3MF is a modern zipped-XML format that carries units, transforms, colours, materials, multiple objects and slicer metadata, and is far less prone to invalid geometry. Prefer 3MF wherever both ends support it — which today is essentially all mainstream CAD and all mainstream slicers.- STEP is a true solid format; several slicers now import it directly and tessellate internally, which removes the export-tolerance decision from your hands but also removes a failure mode. Slicing turns the mesh into toolpaths and is the subject of post 02. This is where the overwhelming majority of your decisions actually live. G-code is a flat, human-readable list of machine commands. A one-line taste: Resin printers are the exception: they consume a stack of sliced images plus per-layer exposure and motion parameters, in vendor container formats (, , and friends) rather than G-code. Some FDM vendors also ship binary G-code containers to cut file size and parse time. Firmware parses the file, does look-ahead motion planning and acceleration limiting, runs the heater control loops and the safety interlocks. Post 01 gives an orientation to the firmware landscape. > Trick: when something is wrong, identify the stage before you change a setting. Wrong shape → CAD or mesh. Right shape, wrong toolpaths → slicer. Right toolpaths, wrong physical result → printer, material or calibration. Changing slicer settings to fix a mesh problem is the most common wasted afternoon in this hobby. Chapter 7 — Capabilities, limits, and how to buy a machine Can, reliably, with a tuned machine - Functional brackets, mounts, enclosures, spacers, adapters and cable management.- Jigs and fixtures — arguably the single highest-value application, because a fixture only has to be right once.- Replacement parts for things that are otherwise scrap: knobs, clips, feet, gears in low-load applications.- Prototypes for form, fit and ergonomics, same day.- Geometry that cannot be machined: internal channels, lattices, captive assemblies, print-in-place mechanisms.- Short-run production of tens of parts, where tooling would never pay for itself. Cannot, or not without much more work than expected - Hold a tolerance out of the box. Dimensional accuracy is achievable, but it is an outcome of calibration and compensation, not a default. Post 05.- Survive heat. PLA loses stiffness not far above room temperature and creeps under sustained load; a PLA part in a parked car or clamped under permanent tension is a slow failure. Material choice fixes this; post 04.- Be watertight or pressure-tight by default. Achievable with wall-count, material and sealing strategy, but never assume it.- Be food-safe. Layer crevices harbour bacteria, and neither the filament additives nor the brass nozzle are automatically food-contact rated. Treat "food safe" as an engineering claim requiring evidence.- Be optically clear. "Transparent" filaments give translucent parts with visible layer structure without significant post-processing.- Print large engineering-plastic parts without warping. ABS, ASA, PC and nylon shrink as they cool; large flat parts in these need an enclosure and good design, and sometimes still fail.- Beat injection moulding on cost at volume. At a few hundred identical parts, run the numbers again.- Run unattended without thought. Mains-powered heaters and a moving machine deserve a smoke alarm in the room, thermal-runaway protection enabled in firmware, and a hard look at anything you are tempted to leave running overnight. Buying criteria, not model names Specific model recommendations date within months. Criteria do not. Score candidates against these: 1. Build volume you actually need. Most parts most people print fit in a 200 mm cube. Paying for a larger machine you use twice is a worse deal than paying for a better small one.2. Enclosure — required or not? If ABS, ASA, PC or nylon are on the roadmap, an enclosure is close to mandatory. If it is PLA and PETG only, an enclosure is a nice-to-have that mostly buys quiet.3. Hotend class and maximum temperature. All-metal and a genuine 280–300 °C capability keep engineering materials available. PTFE-lined caps you around 240–250 °C.4. Extruder architecture. Direct drive if TPU or fine retraction control matters; either is fine otherwise.5. Motion system. CoreXY if speed and tall parts matter; bedslinger if simplicity, serviceability and price matter.6. Bed levelling and probe type. Some form of automatic mesh probing is close to standard now; what varies is probe technology and whether the machine can also tram the bed physically. Post 01 covers what auto-levelling does and does not fix.7. Firmware and profile openness. Can you flash your own firmware? Are slicer profiles open and editable? Can you use a third-party slicer? A closed ecosystem is convenient right up to the day you need something it does not do.8. Consumables lock-in. Does it require proprietary filament, proprietary build plates, or a cloud account to print? Check whether local printing works with no network.9. Spare parts and documentation. Can you buy a nozzle, a hotend, a belt, a mainboard? Is there a wiring diagram? This is what separates a five-year machine from a two-year one.10. Community size. The practical value of a large user base is that your specific failure has already been diagnosed by someone, in public, with photos.11. Safety features. Thermal runaway protection, sane mains wiring for a heated bed, a thermistor that is mechanically captive.12. Total cost. Add spare nozzles, extra build plates, filament, a dry box, and — for resin — gloves, IPA, a wash/cure station and ventilation. Resin's sticker price is the smaller half of its cost. > Common pitfall: buying for the maximum-speed number in the marketing material. Maximum speed is quoted at a flow rate and a quality level nobody prints at. The number that predicts your experience is reliable volumetric flow with the material you actually use, and no vendor prints that on the box.
