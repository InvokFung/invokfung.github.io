Piano Notes — 01 · Grade 1 Theory & Repertoire
Created2025-01-21|Music
ABRSM Grade 1 covers the foundations every musician needs: clefs, note values, simple time signatures, the first few keys, basic intervals, and the very first scales. This post pairs the syllabus with practice pieces graded at the same level, plus the memory tricks I wish someone had handed me on day one. > Series map: 00 Beginner Basics → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 to follow. Learning Outcome - Read fluently in treble and bass clef on the five staff lines, four spaces, and one ledger line above or below.- Identify all note values from semibreve to quaver, and matching rests.- Use 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 time signatures correctly.- Name notes in C, G, F majors and their key signatures.- Build major scales for C, G, F (one octave, hands separate).- Identify intervals between any two notes of the C major scale (number only).- Recognise the tonic, dominant, subdominant of those three keys.- Play 3 to 5 short pieces hands together at Grade 1 standard. Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap Terminology - Pitch (音高) — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.- Accidental — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.- Sharp (♯) — raise the pitch by one semitone.- Flat (♭) — lower the pitch by one semitone.- Natural (♮) — cancel a previous sharp or flat. An accidental affects every occurrence of that note in the same octave for the rest of the bar — then the barline resets it. A key signature is the only "global" accidental. Ledger lines (加線) Grade 1 requires one ledger above and one below in each clef. - Treble: one line above the staff = A5, one line below the staff = Middle C (C4).- Bass: one line above the staff = Middle C (C4) as well (the same note), one line below the staff = E2. > Trick: Middle C is the connector. It sits one ledger below the treble staff AND one ledger above the bass staff. Same key on the piano, two ways to write it. Drill this until it's automatic — Grade 2 and beyond use it constantly. Chapter 2 — Note Values (音符的時值) English / 中文 / symbol table | EN | 中文 | UK name | Beats in 4/4 ||----|------|---------|--------------|| Whole note | 全音符 | Semibreve | 4 || Half note | 二分音符 | Minim | 2 || Quarter note | 四分音符 | Crotchet | 1 || Eighth note | 八分音符 | Quaver | ½ | ABRSM uses the UK terms (semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver). American sheet music uses whole/half/quarter/eighth. Same notes, different names. Get used to both. Rests Every note value has a matching rest. The same beat counts apply — a crotchet rest is 1 beat of silence in 4/4. > Trick: I confused the whole-note rest (hangs down from a line) and the half-note rest (sits on top of a line). Memory aid: "the whole rest is a hole — it falls down. The half is half full — it sits up." Silly, never forgot it again. Dots and ties - A dot after a note adds half its value. Dotted minim = 2 + 1 = 3 beats.- A tie joins two notes of the same pitch — sum their durations.- A slur joins two notes of different pitches — phrasing, not duration. Chapter 3 — Time Signatures (拍號) Grade 1 covers 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 only. The top number = beats per bar. Place natural emphasis there; the bar lines exist precisely to tell you where to push slightly. Bar (小節) maths The notes in any bar must add up to the time signature's beat count. Silent counting is for performance, not learning. Chapter 4 — Keys and Key Signatures Grade 1 syllabus: C major, G major, F major. | Key | Sharps/Flats | Memory anchor ||-----|--------------|---------------|| C major | none | Easy default || G major | 1 sharp: F♯ | "Goes up" → sharp || F major | 1 flat: B♭ | "Falls down" → flat | Scale construction reminder Major scale pattern in semitones: W W H W W W H (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half). Apply from any tonic and you get a major scale. From C: all whites. From G: hits F♯ as the 7th. From F: hits B♭ as the 4th. > Trick: the major-scale pattern doesn't change — only the starting note does. If you can derive C major from scratch, you can derive every major scale by counting W W H W W W H with your finger on the keyboard. Don't memorise 12 scales; memorise one pattern and 12 starting points. Tonic, Dominant, Subdominant For any major scale: - Tonic (I) — the 1st degree. Home note. C in C major.- Subdominant (IV) — the 4th degree. F in C major.- Dominant (V) — the 5th degree. G in C major. These three notes form the primary triads of the key, the most important chords in any major piece. Half of pop and folk music is built from just I, IV, V. > Where I got stuck: I assumed "dominant" meant "loudest" or "main". It actually means the 5th, always. The label is positional, not musical. Once I stopped overthinking it, every scale fell into place. Chapter 5 — Intervals (Number Only) At Grade 1 you only need to identify the number of an interval (2nd, 3rd, etc.), not its quality (major / minor / perfect). Quality starts at Grade 2. Counting Count the lower note as 1, then count up the staff lines and spaces to the upper note. The count includes both notes. | Interval | Staff positions ||----------|-----------------|| Unison | Same line/space || 2nd | Adjacent line + space || 3rd | Line to next line, or space to next space || 4th | Line to space-after-the-next-line || 5th | Line to second line above || 6th | Space to third line above, or line to third space above || 7th | Line to third line above, or space to third space above (one short of an octave) || Octave (8th) | Same letter, seven steps up (line to space, or space to line) | > Trick: 3rds and 5ths "jump white" — they go line-to-line or space-to-space, looking visually stacked. 2nds and 4ths "step black" — one note sits on a line, the other in a space. Once your eye reads these shapes, you stop counting notes one-by-one. Chapter 6 — Hands-Together Coordination You played hands together in Post 00 already with "Twinkle". Grade 1 expands this. The technique: 1. Each hand alone, played until boring. Genuinely boring — no mistakes, no thinking.2. Hands together at half speed. Eyes on the page, not the hands.3. Pulse first, accuracy second. If one hand stumbles, don't stop the pulse. Drop the wrong-hand note rather than break the beat.4. Slow with metronome. Crotchet = 60. Once smooth, raise by 5 BPM increments. > Where I got stuck: my left hand was 6 weeks behind my right. The fix: I practised only the left hand for one full week. No right hand at all. Result: by the next week my left could play its own line cleanly, and hands-together suddenly worked. Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills Grade 1 ABRSM practical includes an aural test. The skills: - Clap back a rhythm — listen to a 2- or 3-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4; clap it back.- Sing back a short phrase — three or four notes, played twice.- Identify time signature — listen to a tune; say whether it's in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4. Practising aural without a teacher - Phone metronome + clap for rhythms. Tap a 4-bar pattern on the table, then clap it back without looking.- Sing scale degrees — call out "1, 2, 3" up the major scale while playing it. Builds the do-re-mi reflex.- Listen to any piece and tap your foot. If your tapping settles into pairs, it's 2/4 or 4/4. If into threes, it's 3/4. Public music is your unlimited test bank. > Trick: whistling is sneaky-good for aural training. If you can whistle the tune you've been practising, you've internalised the pitch shape. If you can only "sort of" whistle it, you haven't. The whistle is a free pitch test. Piano Notes — 00 · Beginner Basics & Your First Songs
Created2025-01-16|Music
The first post in a self-study piano series. Goal: by the end of this one you can sit at any keyboard, find any note in under five seconds, and play three short pieces from memory. Skip the marketing fluff and go straight to the muscle memory. > Series map: 00 Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 will follow. Learning Outcome - Sit comfortably at the keyboard with healthy posture.- Name every white key on sight — both directions, both clefs.- Find Middle C on any size piano without thinking.- Play three short pieces with both hands.- Recognise the difference between a piano, an upright, a digital, and a synth — and pick the right one for practice. Chapter 1 — Why piano is a good first instrument The keyboard is the clearest visual map of Western music. Every other instrument hides pitch behind a fingering chart; the piano lays the entire pitch space out in front of you, left-to-right, low-to-high. If you understand "how a scale looks" on the piano, you understand it on a guitar, a violin, or in a software synth — you just don't see it as plainly. A second practical reason: the piano is monophonically simple but polyphonically rich. Beginners can produce a clean tone from day one (press → sound), while advanced players spend a lifetime on the same instrument. Few instruments span that range. > Where I started: I wasted my first two weeks googling "best digital piano under $500" instead of practising. The truth is any 88-key weighted keyboard from a major brand (Yamaha, Roland, Casio, Kawai) is fine for the first year. The piano you have and play every day beats the piano you research and never own. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Acoustic (real) piano - Upright (直立式) — most homes. Footprint 150 cm wide, depth 60 cm. Tone is good but action is lighter than a grand.- Grand (平台式) — long horizontal action, more responsive. Babies (150 cm) to concert (275 cm). Expensive, needs tuning twice a year. Digital piano - Weighted action (重錘鍵盤) is non-negotiable for serious practice. "Semi-weighted" and "synth action" feel different and will delay your finger development.- 88 full keys preferred over 76 or 61 — exam pieces will eventually need the full range.- Built-in speakers are a "nice to have"; quality varies. Headphones bypass the problem and are quieter for housemates. What you actually need (the honest list) | Item | Necessity | Why ||------|-----------|-----|| 88-key fully-weighted digital, or any acoustic upright | Essential | Your hands form habits around action weight || Bench at correct height | Essential | Wrong height = wrist strain in 4 weeks || Metronome (app fine) | Essential | The most-skipped tool that fixes the most-common problem || Music stand with light | Highly recommended | You'll be reading every session || Headphones (digital) | Recommended | Practise at any hour without conflict || Pedal (sustain) | Eventually | Not for week 1; needed by week 8 | > Trick: the right bench height = your forearms parallel to the floor when fingers rest on the keys, wrists neither dropping nor arching. Sit too high → you push the keys down with extra force and tense the wrist. Sit too low → you reach up and the fingers fight gravity. Adjust this on day one. Re-check it monthly. Chapter 3 — The keyboard map Black-key landmarks The black keys come in groups of 2 and 3. The 2-group anchors C-D-E; the 3-group anchors F-G-A-B. - The white key immediately left of a 2-group is always C.- The white key immediately left of a 3-group is always F. These two anchors are the only ones you ever need to memorise. The other letters (D, E, G, A, B) are derived by counting in alphabetical order from C or F. > Trick: the 2-group is C-D-E; 3-group is F-G-A-B. Stack the consonants in your head: Car Drives East, Fish Goes Around Back. Cheesy works. Middle C The C closest to the centre of the keyboard. On an 88-key piano this is the fourth C from the bottom — C4 in scientific pitch notation. On a 61-key it's the third C. Either way, find the centre, find the 2-group nearest it, drop your right thumb on the white key to the left of that pair. Octaves Same letter, doubled frequency: $f{n+12} = 2 \cdot fn$. The keyboard pattern repeats every 12 keys (7 white + 5 black). Once you memorise the 7-white-key span (C D E F G A B), the entire 88-key keyboard is mapped. Chapter 4 — Posture and hand mechanics Sitting - Bench centred on Middle C.- Sit on the front third of the bench, not slumped back.- Feet flat on the floor. (Smaller players: get a footstool; dangling feet cause back tension.)- Elbows roughly at keyboard height.- Shoulders down and relaxed. No shrugging up to the ears. Hands - Curved fingers, as if holding a tennis ball.- Strike with the fingertip, not the pad.- Wrist is level with the back of the hand — flat, not collapsed, not arched.- Thumb plays with its side, slightly tucked under. Finger numbering | Hand | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 ||------|---|---|---|---|---|| Right | Thumb | Index | Middle | Ring | Little || Left | Thumb | Index | Middle | Ring | Little | Both hands use the same scheme — the thumb is always 1 for its own hand. Sheet music writes RH finger numbers above the staff, LH below. > Where I got stuck: I kept dropping my wrist when playing softly. Fix: I propped a coin on the back of my hand during slow practice. If it stayed put for eight bars, my wrist was level. Sounds gimmicky, fixes it in three days. Chapter 5 — Reading the staff (just enough for now) Only what you need for the three first songs is covered here. Full staff theory is in Post 01. Treble (right hand) Lines bottom to top: E G B D F — "Every Good Boy Does Fine".Spaces bottom to top: F A C E — already spells FACE. Bass (left hand) Lines: G B D F A — "Good Boys Do Fine Always".Spaces: A C E G — "All Cows Eat Grass". Middle C bridges both Middle C sits on the first ledger line below the treble staff and the first ledger line above the bass staff. The same note, written two ways. The grand staff joins the two clefs with a brace — right hand on top, left hand on bottom. > Analogy: the grand staff is two ladders bolted together at Middle C. Right hand climbs the top ladder (higher pitches), left hand climbs the bottom ladder (lower pitches). Middle C is the rung they share. Chapter 6 — Three first pieces These are the canonical first songs. I picked them because (1) the right-hand melody fits inside one hand position, (2) the left hand uses only two or three notes, and (3) you've heard them since you were five — your ear corrects you before your brain does. Piece 1 — "Ode to Joy" (Beethoven, Symphony No. 9) Hand position: RH thumb on Middle C. Repeat. Then play: Why this piece: the melody never leaves the five-finger span of one hand. You don't move your wrist. The whole song is your introduction to "fingers stay put, brain reads." > Trick: count "1 2 3 4" steadily through it — every note is one beat except the last D of each line, which is two beats. If you can clap it without playing, you can play it. Piece 2 — "Mary Had a Little Lamb" Hand position: RH thumb on Middle C. Why this piece: introduces a longer arc with repetition. You'll notice the same three-note shape repeats — once you spot the pattern your eye stops reading note-by-note. Piece 3 — "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star" — both hands RH: thumb on Middle C. LH: start with the 5th finger on the C an octave below Middle C (so 5 = C3, 3 = E3, 1 = G3). For your first attempt, just play the C3 long-held for each bar — one note per bar. Hold it under the right hand. Once that's solid, add G3 alternating where the harmony changes (bar 3, bar 6). > Where I got stuck: hands together on day three felt impossible. Fix: don't try hands together until each hand is boring on its own. "Boring" is the right level — if you have to think, you're not ready. Each hand should run on autopilot, and only then do you stack them. Chapter 7 — How to practise (the meta-skill) If you take one thing from this post, take this: slow practice with full attention beats fast practice with split attention by an order of magnitude. The brain encodes accuracy, not speed. Speed comes free once accuracy is locked in. Daily routine, 25–30 minutes | Block | Time | What ||-------|------|------|| Warm-up | 3 min | 5-finger pattern, C major position, RH then LH then both || Note-naming | 2 min | Pick a random key, name it out loud || Today's piece | 15 min | One section at a time, slow, no mistakes || Sight-read | 5 min | Brand-new short piece, no repeats — see how far you get || Cool-down | 2 min | Replay your most-mastered piece for fun | Slow practice rules 1. The last attempt before you stop is the one your brain rehearses overnight. > Trick I steal from sports coaches: finish every practice session by playing the easiest passage of the day one more time, slowly and perfectly. Going to bed on a clean run reinforces the right pattern in sleep. Going to bed frustrated reinforces the wrong one. Violin Notes — 06 · Grade 6 Double Stops, Higher Positions, Chromatic Scales

Grade 6 ABRSM violin is where the instrument doubles itself. Double stops — two strings sounded together — become a syllabus requirement. The right hand learns flying staccato, the left hand uses all positions through 5th regularly, and chromatic scales add 12-note fluency. The repertoire is now full Baroque sonatas, mid-Romantic showpieces, and the first complete concerto movements expected at concert level. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 (this post) → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play 3rds and 6ths double stops through one octave on selected keys.- Play chromatic scale (one or two octaves) starting on G or A.- Use 1st–5th positions fluently in a single piece.- Play three-octave scales in G, A, B♭ major (and some minor).- Use staccato volante ("flying staccato") in showpieces.- Identify ornaments + execute trills, mordents, turns idiomatically.- Perform a complete sonata movement + a Romantic showpiece. Chapter 1 — Double stops: the violin singing in two voices A double stop is two strings bowed simultaneously, each producing a different pitch. The bow must contact both strings at once. Bow angle for double stops The bow normally tilts to one side to contact one string. For double stops, the bow lies flat on both strings at the same angle. Bow arm height adjusts to suit. Easy double stops: open string + stopped string Examples:- Open E + 1st finger B on A = B4 + E5 (a 4th)- Open A + 1st finger F♯ on E = A4 + F♯5 (a 6th) These are the entry-level double stops; you've been hearing them in pieces since Grade 3 (as the drone effect). Stopped double stops: 3rds Two fingers on adjacent strings, both stopped. For a 3rd in 1st position: To get a 3rd, you need two fingers, one per string, giving notes 3 letters apart. In 1st position on A + E: The Grade 6 syllabus typically asks for a one-octave scale in 3rds in keys like G major. 6ths Two-finger spread; the two fingers are usually on different strings, one slightly behind the other. > Where I got stuck: my fingers couldn't independently stop adjacent strings at different positions. Fix: very slow practice with a tuner on each note separately, then together. 5 minutes per day, 6 weeks, before any scale in 3rds was reliable. Chapter 2 — Chromatic scale 12 semitones in one octave, played either with traditional fingering or sliding-finger chromatic. Traditional fingering (G chromatic ascending, one octave on G + D string) The same finger plays two consecutive semitones by sliding (1 plays G♯ then A; 2 plays A♯ then B). The finger slides up about ½ a finger-width. Galamian's chromatic fingering Uses a different finger pattern that avoids slides. Faster but harder to learn. > Trick: the slide-finger chromatic is more idiomatic and what most pieces expect. Practise it very slowly with metronome. The "click" of the finger sliding between two semitones should be inaudible — the bow disguises it. Chapter 3 — Flying staccato (staccato volante) A series of bouncing staccato notes all on one bow direction, the bow lifting between each. Distinct from slurred staccato (which stays on the string). Flying staccato lifts. How to learn it 1. Solid up-bow staccato first (easier direction). 4 notes per bow at slow tempo.2. Each note: bow stops and lifts a millimetre, then drops for the next note.3. Build to 8 notes per bow.4. Reverse to down-bow staccato. Used famously in: Sarasate Zigeunerweisen, Wieniawski concertos, encore showpieces. > Trick: the wrist + fingers produce the "stop+lift" motion; the arm stays steady. If your arm is jerking, you're forcing it. Relax, slow down. Chapter 4 — Higher positions: 6th, 7th, half position Half position The hand sits below 1st position — 1st finger plays just one semitone above the open string (B♭ on the A string). Used for low chromatic passages. 6th and 7th positions 6th pos on A: 1=G♯5.7th pos on A: 1=A5 (an octave above open A). In 7th position, the hand sits over the body of the violin. Thumb adjusts to a different position; left hand has limited mobility. Used mostly for very high passages in concertos and showpieces. > Where I got stuck: my left wrist collapsed in 7th position — the angle felt wrong. Fix: explicitly lift the left elbow forward (more underneath the violin) when shifting above 5th. The hand pivots more from underneath. Chapter 5 — Ornaments executed idiomatically Grade 6 demands playing, not just identifying, ornaments. Trill (tr or ⟵⟶) Rapid alternation between the written note and the note a step above. In Baroque: starts on the upper note. In Classical/Romantic: starts on the written note. The 2nd finger trills (lift-drop) while bow sustains. Trill speed: 6–10 oscillations per second. Practise slowly first, build speed. Mordent (𝆚) Quick three-note ornament: written note → step below (or above) → written note. Turn (∾) Four-note ornament: above-note → written → below-note → written. Appoggiatura (small note before main note) Takes half the value of the following note in Classical music. Acciaccatura (small note with slash through stem) A "crush" note — played as fast as possible, then immediately to the main note. Chapter 1 — Vibrato: the expression Vibrato is the small, rapid pitch oscillation that adds warmth and life to sustained notes. Without it, classical violin tone sounds naked. Three vibrato types | Type | Source | Speed | When ||------|--------|-------|------|| Wrist vibrato | Wrist rocks back and forth | Medium | Most repertoire || Arm vibrato | Whole forearm rocks | Slow + wide | Romantic, big tone || Finger vibrato | Finger joint flexes | Fast + narrow | Rare; modern works | Wrist is the standard first vibrato taught at Grade 5. Learning wrist vibrato — step by step 1. Air vibrato — without violin. Hold left arm up as if holding violin. Wave hand at the wrist, palm-up to palm-down motion, for 30 sec. This is the motion.2. Vibrato on knee. Sit. Place finger on knee. Rock wrist gently, finger stays in contact, pad rolls. Build motor pattern.3. Vibrato on violin neck (no string). Finger on the neck, not string. Rock wrist.4. Vibrato on string with finger anchored. 3rd finger on A (= D5). Bow a long D on the A string. Rock wrist; finger should roll (not slide). Pitch should wobble below the pitch and back to it.5. Slow metronome vibrato. 4 wobbles per beat at 60 bpm = 4 Hz. Build to 6 Hz over 6 months.6. Apply to scales. Vibrate every sustained note in a slow scale. > Where I got stuck: my wrist locked. Vibrato felt impossible for 3 months. Fix: spend 5 minutes per day on the "knee drill" — no violin, no string, just the wrist motion. The brain learns motion separately from instrument. Once the wrist could rock freely, transferring to the violin took one week. Pitch direction Vibrato should wobble below the in-tune pitch back up to it. Never above. The brain hears the upper limit of the oscillation as "the note". Where to start using it Don't vibrate every note. Start with:- Long notes (half-notes, whole-notes).- Phrase peak notes.- Final note of a phrase. By Grade 6, you'll vibrate almost every note that's longer than a quaver. At Grade 5, just the obvious ones. Chapter 2 — Shifting to 4th and 5th positions 4th position On the A string: 1=E5, 2=F♯5, 3=G♯5, 4=A5. Created2024-12-16|Music
Grade 4 ABRSM violin is where the left hand finally leaves first position. Real 2nd and 3rd position shifting becomes required, melodic minor enters the playing syllabus, more flat/sharp keys (E♭, E, A♭) appear, and the right hand learns spiccato — the controlled bouncing bow. The repertoire moves into early concertos and substantial Baroque sonatas. > Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 (this post) → 05 Grade 5 → … → 08 Grade 8. Learning Outcome - Play two-octave scales in all keys up to 3 sharps and 3 flats majors.- Play melodic minor in playing form (not just paper).- Shift cleanly to 2nd and 3rd position and back.- Begin spiccato (controlled bouncing bow) on open strings + simple scales.- Identify all interval qualities at sight.- Play 6–8 Grade 4 pieces including one early concerto movement. Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine 3rd position — the workhorse 3rd position is the second most used position after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd. Finding 3rd position On the A string:1. Play open A.2. Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).3. Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5. Other strings analogous:- G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.- D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.- E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D. The shift motion Three components, in this order:1. Release finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).2. Thumb and hand travel together up the neck.3. Press the new finger at the target. The finger that leads the shift is called the guide finger. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1. > Trick: practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a "scrape", the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide. 2nd position Less common than 3rd. The hand sits halfway between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F. 2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits just above 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches. Grade 4 requires playing. D melodic minor (one or two octaves) Ascending: D E F G A B C♯ D — raise 6 (B) and 7 (C♯).Descending: D C B♭ A G F E D — back to natural. Fingering (one octave on D + A): That B♭ on descent / B♮ on ascent on the same string is the tricky part. Same finger (1), different position. > Where I got stuck: my hand could not switch B♮ ↔ B♭ in one scale. Allocate one minor per day, rotate. Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce Spiccato is the foundation of all advanced bowing. The bow lifts off the string between notes — not because you lifted it, but because the bow's elasticity bounces it. How spiccato works The bow is flexible. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the "sweet spot" (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note. Spiccato practice progression 1. Drop test. Hold the bow over the A string, let it fall from 1 cm above, watch it bounce. Find the bow position where bounce is most controlled (usually 4–6 inches above the middle, near the balance point).2. One bounce per drop. Drop, lift, drop. Slow.3. Two bounces per drop without re-lifting. The bow bounces itself.4. Continuous bouncing on open string at metronome 60.5. Add scale notes. D major scale, spiccato.6. Speed up gradually to 100, then 120. > Trick: spiccato fails when you grip the bow. Relax thumb + pinky; let the bow do the work. If your wrist hurts after spiccato practice, you're forcing it. Stop, shake out, restart looser. Coming after the flat keys, E major feels easy because the fingers sit "high" and two open strings (E and A) are in the key. A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭) The hardest playable key at Grade 4. Two-octave A♭ is Grade 5+. > Trick: don't memorise A♭ note-by-note. Memorise it as a modified D major shape, with low 1 on the D string and a low everywhere else.
