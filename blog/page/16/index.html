Chapter 1 — Harmonics: the bell-like tones A harmonic is a high, pure, bell-like tone produced by lightly touching the string at specific nodes (rather than pressing down). Natural harmonics Found at division points of the open string: | Touch point on string | Resulting harmonic | Pitch ||-----------------------|--------------------|-------|| 1/2 (octave node) | 1st overtone | Open string + octave || 1/3 node | 2nd overtone | Octave + 5th || 1/4 node | 3rd overtone | Two octaves above open || 1/5 node | 4th overtone | Two octaves + maj 3rd | The 1/2 node on the A string is found by lightly touching with your 4th finger at the position where the 4th finger would normally play A5 (one octave above open A). The sound: a soft, ringing high A — an octave higher than expected. > Trick: harmonics need almost no finger pressure — just a feather touch. If you press, you get a muted scratchy note. If you brush, you get the pure overtone. Practise the touch on open A: bow long stroke, feather-touch where the 3rd finger sits in 1st position. The ringing high A (two octaves up) appears almost magically. Artificial harmonics Press one finger firmly (the "stopping finger") and touch with another finger a 4th higher (the "harmonic finger"). The pitch produced is two octaves above the stopped pitch. Notation: diamond-shape note head for the touched note, plus regular note for the stopped note. Used famously in: Tchaikovsky Violin Concerto, Paganini Caprices, Ravel Tzigane. Chapter 2 — All positions: fluency target By Grade 7, you've learned half, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th positions. Now: 8th position and fluency across all of them. 8th position On the A string: 1=B5, 2=C♯6, 3=D6, 4=E6 (right at the top of the violin's standard range for most pieces). The left hand pivots: thumb shifts to a different angle, hand approaches the bridge. The fluency goal You should be able to:1. Shift to any position without looking.2. Find the correct first finger pitch within a quarter-tone, on the first try.3. Choose appropriate position for expression (tone colour) — not just to reach a note. > Trick: practise a single piece entirely in 1st position, then again entirely in 3rd, then entirely in 5th (skipping notes you can't reach). The same melody in different positions sounds different — different string vibrations, different timbre. Pick the position that suits the music. Bow pressure increased before the stroke begins, released through the stroke, stops abruptly. Used for: marching feel, accented passages, opening of dramatic phrases. Ricochet ("rebound, thrown") Multiple notes from a single thrown bow. The bow is dropped from above, bounces 2–4 times naturally, lifted before it stops. Used in: Mendelssohn Concerto last movement, Paganini Caprices, Sarasate showpieces. Sautillé ("springing") Very fast spiccato — so fast that the bow no longer fully lifts off; the wood "springs" rapidly. Played in the middle of the bow with a loose grip. Used in: Vivaldi Four Seasons (Summer storm), Beethoven Concerto 1st mvt, Mendelssohn Concerto 1st mvt. > Where I got stuck: I couldn't transition from spiccato to sautillé. The hand wanted to "control" each bounce. Fix: extreme tempo experiment. Start a tremolo (very fast back-and-forth on one note). Gradually add finger changes. The wrist learns the bouncing speed first, then the fingers add pitches. Chapter 4 — Octave double stops Two notes an octave apart, played simultaneously. Fingering: 1st finger on lower string + 4th finger on the next string up (e.g., 1 on G + 4 on D for an octave). Octaves require:- 1st and 4th fingers to be exactly in tune with each other.- 3rd and 4th fingers stretched apart maximally. Practice Slow tuner work. Tune 1st finger first; freeze it. Add 4th finger; adjust until the octave rings (no beats). Then bow the double stop. Practise on every string pair: G+D, D+A, A+E. > Trick: octaves are the second-hardest interval to tune (after major 7ths). Beats are very audible; an octave that's even slightly off sounds painful. Practise daily for 5 minutes. Octave fluency takes 6 months minimum. Chapter 7 — Memorisation strategy By Grade 7, you should perform from memory. Strategy: 1. Analyse the structure first. Break the piece into sections (A-B-A, exposition-development-recap, theme + variations, etc.). Label each section.2. Memorise section by section — not start-to-finish.3. Memorise the hardest section first. Mental energy is highest at the start of practice; spend it on the toughest bars.4. Practise from any starting point. Open the score randomly; you should be able to start there from memory. This builds resilience for performance anxiety.5. Mental practice. Without the violin, mentally walk through the piece — every shift, every bow direction, every fingering. 10 minutes a day. Increases memory reliability dramatically.6. Slow performance practice. Once memorised, play the whole piece at 60% tempo from memory. Catches weak spots. > Where I got stuck: I memorised by repetition only — fingers learned the piece, but my brain didn't. Under stress (an audition), my fingers blanked. Fix: 10 minutes of mental practice daily. It feels useless at first, but after a month, memory failure rate dropped 80%.
