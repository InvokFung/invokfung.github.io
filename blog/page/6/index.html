Most Vue bugs that reach production are not template bugs. They are reactivity bugs: a value that stopped updating after a refactor, a watcher that fires three times or never, a list that re-renders on every keystroke, a store that leaks between SSR requests. 3D Printing Course — 03 · Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing
Created2026-05-06|Engineering 3D Printing
There is one decision in 3D printing that outweighs every slicer setting combined, and it is made in about two seconds by dragging a model around a build plate. Orientation simultaneously determines which direction the part is strong in, which surfaces come out smooth, how much support material is needed, how long the print takes, and how accurate the holes are — and those five objectives conflict with each other, so there is no orientation that wins on all of them. This post makes that trade-off explicit by working a real part through it, then covers the machinery of overhangs, bridging, holes and supports, and finishes with the design habits that let you avoid most of the problem in the first place. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing (this post) → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - Evaluate a part's orientation against five conflicting objectives and defend the choice you make.- Apply the anisotropy rule — load along layers, never across them — to a real bracket in three orientations.- State the 45-degree overhang guideline properly, derive where it comes from, and compute how it shifts with layer height and extrusion width.- Explain what makes a bridge succeed, and run a bridging test to find your machine's usable span.- Explain why horizontal holes come out undersized and egg-shaped, and apply the teardrop and chamfer fixes.- Choose between normal and tree supports, and set the Z-gap that decides whether supports snap off or fuse on.- Design overhangs out of a part rather than supporting them.- Apply the minimum wall, minimum feature, bottom-chamfer and text rules relative to your own extrusion width.- Split a part sensibly and join it with the right method for the material.- Design a print-in-place mechanism and know what determines its clearance. Chapter 1 — Orientation as the master decision Terminology - Build orientation — the rotation of the part relative to the build plate. Three rotational degrees of freedom, and every one of them matters.- Strength axis — the direction in which the part's layers run continuously, and therefore the direction it is strongest in.- Overhang — a downward-facing surface with nothing directly beneath it.- Bridge — an unsupported horizontal span with both ends anchored in solid material.- Support — sacrificial structure printed to hold up overhangs, then removed.- Interface layer — the dense top layers of a support, which form the surface the part rests on.- Z-gap / contact distance — vertical clearance between support top and part underside.- Self-supporting — geometry whose every downward face is shallow enough to print without support.- Scar — the rough surface left where support touched the part. The five conflicting objectives Every orientation is a bet across these: 1. Strength. The service load should run along the layers, not across them (post 00, Chapter 3).2. Surface quality. Vertical walls get layer lines; top faces get skin; downward faces get support scars; sloped faces get stepping.3. Support volume. More overhangs means more sacrificial material, more time, more cleanup, more scars.4. Print time. Roughly, taller means more layers means longer — but a tall thin part also has short layers with long minimum-layer-time waits, so it is not linear.5. Dimensional accuracy. XY features are accurate in a different way than Z features; holes behave completely differently depending on axis; the first layer is squashed. They conflict. The orientation that makes a bracket strongest usually also makes it tallest, slowest, and puts its bolt holes on their sides. Rank the objectives for this specific part before you rotate anything. For a functional bracket, strength and hole accuracy outrank surface finish. For a display model, the reverse. A worked part: the L-bracket Take a concrete part. An L-shaped bracket: two legs, 60 mm and 40 mm, each 4 mm thick and 30 mm wide, with two clearance holes through each leg. In service it is bolted to a wall by the short leg, and a load hangs from the end of the long leg, bending the bracket about the inside corner. Three orientations, all of which someone will suggest: Orientation A — flat on its back. The short leg lies on the plate, the long leg stands vertically. Layers are horizontal planes stacked up the long leg. Orientation B — L-profile flat on the plate. The L profile lies in the plane of the bed and the 30 mm width becomes the build height. Each layer is a complete L outline, 4 mm thick, repeated 30 mm tall. Orientation C — standing on the corner. The bracket balanced at 45°, corner down, both legs rising. | | A — flat on its back | B — L-profile flat | C — on the corner ||---|---|---|---|| Layer direction relative to the service load | tensile stress at the corner runs across layers | tensile stress runs along continuous roads that wrap the corner | stress at roughly 45° to layers || Strength under the design load | worst — a clean interlayer break at the inside corner is the predicted failure | best — the corner is a continuous loop of material every layer | intermediate || Support needed | none | none | yes, under the corner and the underside of both legs || Print time | short — 60 mm tall, big cross-sections | moderate — 30 mm tall, small L cross-sections, many layers of little material | longest — support plus a tall diagonal envelope || Bed footprint | small | large — the whole L profile | small || Hole axis | vertical through the short leg, horizontal through the long leg | horizontal through both legs | diagonal through both || Hole quality | mixed: one good, one egg-shaped | both need teardrop or chamfer treatment, or drilling | worst — diagonal holes are both overhung and stepped || Visible surface quality | good on the outer faces; layer lines up the long leg | layer lines wrap the profile; the two big flat faces are top-skin and bed-face | support scars on the underside of both legs || Adhesion risk | good — a big flat first layer | good — a long thin first layer, watch corner lift | poor — small contact area, needs a brim | The decision. For a load-bearing bracket, orientation B, with the holes handled by chamfering (Chapter 4) or by drilling them out afterwards. The strength difference between A and B is the largest single effect available in this entire course, and it costs nothing but bed space and a hole-detailing decision. For a bracket that carries nothing and just needs to look tidy on a shelf, orientation A is faster, uses less bed, and gives one good hole for free. That is the shape of the reasoning. It is not "which orientation is correct" but "which objective am I optimising, and what am I paying for it". > Trick: before rotating a part, write down in one sentence how it will be loaded in service. Half of all orientation mistakes are made by people who have not consciously answered that question. Chapter 2 — The anisotropy rule The rule: load should run along layers, never peel them apart. Everything in post 00, Chapter 3 reduces to this. Material along a road is continuous polymer. Material across a layer interface is held together only by the chains that diffused across during the moments the interface was hot. The interface is always the weaker path. The failure modes even look different, which makes diagnosis easy: | | In-plane failure | Interlayer failure ||---|---|---|| Fracture surface | ragged, drawn, fibrous | flat, follows a single layer line, often glassy || Warning before failure | visible yielding, whitening, deformation | usually none — it goes suddenly || Fracture path | wanders through the section | perfectly planar and horizontal | If you snap a printed part and the break is a flat horizontal plane, the part was oriented wrong. That is the whole diagnosis. Three orientations of the same bracket, mechanically Return to the L-bracket. The load bends the long leg about the inside corner. Bending puts the outside of the corner in tension. - Orientation A: the tensile stress at the outer corner is vertical, i.e. perpendicular to the horizontal layers. The load is directly peeling the interfaces apart, one at a time, starting at the outermost fibre where the stress is highest. Failure is a clean horizontal snap at the corner, at a load far below what the material could take.- Orientation B: the tensile stress runs around the L profile, which is exactly the direction the perimeter roads run. Continuous polymer carries the load. The interfaces are loaded in shear rather than tension, and shear is a far more favourable loading for a weld.- Orientation C: the stress is at roughly 45° to the interfaces, giving a mixed mode. Better than A, worse than B, and it costs support material and surface quality on top. Two corrections to the rule It is not "print everything standing up" or "print everything flat". The rule is about the load, not about the part. A hook loaded in its own plane wants that plane flat on the bed; a hook loaded out of its plane wants the opposite. Reason from the load path every time. Walls do more than infill. As post 02 noted, in bending, material far from the neutral axis dominates. A part in the right orientation with four walls and 15 % infill will usually beat the same part in the wrong orientation at 60 % infill. Orientation first, walls second, infill third. How much it droops depends on: - Cooling. More airflow means faster solidification and better overhangs. This is the same trade against layer adhesion as always.- Speed. A slower overhang perimeter gives each road time to set before the next lands on it.- Material. PLA solidifies quickly and overhangs well; PETG stays soft longer; ABS and ASA in a hot enclosure are the worst case.- Extrusion width. A wider road has more supported area relative to its overhang. 45° is simply an angle that is comfortably inside every one of these limits for common settings, which is why it survived as folklore. > Watch out: slicers do not agree on which end of the angle they measure from. PrusaSlicer's support threshold is stated with 90° meaning vertical; Cura's overhang angle is measured such that 0° supports everything and 90° supports nothing. The two conventions are complementary, and 45 is the one value where they happen to agree — which is exactly why the confusion goes unnoticed until you type 60. Check your slicer's tooltip before trusting the number. Measuring your own overhang threshold Print a stepped overhang test model with faces at 30°, 40°, 45°, 50°, 55°, 60°, 65° and 70° from vertical, with your normal profile and material. Inspect the undersides in raking light and pick the steepest angle whose underside you would accept on a real part. Then set your slicer's support threshold a few degrees conservative of that value. Repeat per material. The answer for PLA is not the answer for PETG. Bridging A bridge is different from an overhang: it is a road spanning from one anchor to another with nothing beneath any of it. It works because the extrudate is laid under tension — the nozzle is moving away from an anchored end, drawing the strand taut — and cools enough to hold its own weight before it sags appreciably. What a successful bridge needs: 1. Both ends anchored in solid material. A bridge to nowhere is a string.2. Slicer detection. Slicers identify bridging regions and apply dedicated bridge settings: full cooling, a specific bridge speed, and often a reduced bridge flow so the strand is drawn thinner and tauter.3. Unidirectional first layer. The first bridging layer is printed as parallel lines aligned with the span, not at the usual infill angle. Slicers do this automatically; when a bridge fails oddly, check the preview to see whether the slicer detected it as a bridge at all.4. Cooling. Bridging is the one place where maximum fan is nearly always right, whatever the material. Span limits depend on material, cooling, speed and width, so measure rather than quote. Print a bridging test with steadily increasing spans, and record the span at which sag becomes unacceptable for each material you use. PLA with strong cooling bridges impressively; PETG less so; ABS or ASA in a warm enclosure least of all — which is one more reason enclosed materials benefit from support-free design. Bridge failure modes and what they mean: | Symptom | Cause ||---|---|| Strands sag into a curve | too hot, too slow, or too little cooling || Strands snap mid-span | bridge flow too low, or speed too high || Strands pull the anchor walls inward | bridge speed too high with too much tension; slow it down || Surface above the bridge is lumpy | normal — the layer above a bridge is always the worst layer; add one more solid layer over it | Chapter 4 — Holes Vertical holes (axis parallel to Z) Each layer contains a circular inner contour, and the hole is formed by perimeter loops following it. This is the good case, but it is not perfect: - Consistently undersized. Roads following a concave inner curve are compressed on their inner edge, so material bulges into the bore. Add die swell and elastic recovery and vertical holes come out reliably a little small. This is systematic and repeatable, which means it is correctable — post 05 covers the compensation.- Polygonal. The hole is only as round as the mesh's tessellation. A coarsely exported 5 mm hole can be a visible dodecagon.- Seamed. The perimeter loop's start/stop lands somewhere in the bore, leaving a small ridge. Painted seam placement helps. Horizontal holes (axis parallel to the bed) This is the problem case, and the mechanism is worth seeing clearly. Walk up a horizontal cylindrical bore layer by layer. At the widest point of the circle the bore wall is vertical. As you climb toward the crown, the wall angle sweeps continuously from vertical through 45° and on to horizontal at the very top. So the upper part of every horizontal hole is an increasingly severe overhang, and the last stretch before the crown is effectively an unsupported bridge across the bore. The results are consistent: - The top of the bore sags into the hole, so the hole is undersized at the top.- The cross-section becomes egg-shaped or D-shaped rather than circular.- The overhanging surface is rough, so a shaft binds even where the hole is nominally large enough.- The effect worsens with larger diameters (longer unsupported span) and with taller layers. The fixes, in order of preference 1. Reorient so the hole is vertical. Always consider this first. It is free and it is the best result. It is also why hole axes should be part of the orientation decision in Chapter 1, not an afterthought. 2. Teardrop the hole. Replace the top arc of the circle with two straight faces at 45° from vertical, meeting at a point — the classic teardrop profile. Every surface then satisfies the self-supporting criterion and nothing droops. Cost: the hole is no longer a true circle, so it suits clearance holes, cable passes and bolt shanks rather than precision bearing seats. 3. Chamfer the top of the hole. A less intrusive variant: replace only the top of the arc with a short flat roof and two 45° chamfers, giving a hexagon-topped bore. Preserves more of the circular profile than a full teardrop, still self-supporting, and the flat roof is a short, reliable bridge. 4. Print undersize and machine to size. Model the hole a little small, then drill or ream it. This is the accurate answer for anything that must actually be round and on-size, and it is the standard practice for bearing seats and dowel holes. 5. Leave it and rely on bridging. For large holes with a well-tuned bridging profile, the crown can be acceptable. Check the slicer preview to confirm the crown is being treated as a bridge. > Common pitfall: teardropping a hole that a bolt head or a bearing has to sit in. The teardrop's point is above the nominal circle, so the hole is larger than nominal at the top and a press fit will be loose there. Teardrops belong on clearance features, not on fits. Chapter 5 — Supports Normal versus tree/organic | | Normal (linear/grid) supports | Tree / organic supports ||---|---|---|| Structure | vertical columns or a grid extruded straight down from every overhang | branching trunks that grow up from the plate or the model and reach out to touch only the points that need it || Material use | high | much lower || Print time | high | lower || Removal | often difficult, especially in deep pockets; comes off in fragments | usually snaps away in a few large pieces || Rigidity | high — good for very large flat overhangs | lower — branches can flex or topple on wide flat ceilings || Compute time | fast | can be slow on complex parts || Best for | large flat overhangs, tall parts needing stabilisation | organic shapes, figurines, parts with scattered small overhangs | Tree supports originated in the Cura engine; PrusaSlicer added its organic support implementation in version 2.6, and the PrusaSlicer-lineage slicers carry it. Both types are worth having in your repertoire — tree supports are not a strict upgrade. Interface layers The top few layers of a support can be printed denser than the body — the interface. A dense interface gives the part's downward face something continuous to land on, so the underside is smoother. The trade is direct: more interface density and more interface layers means a better underside and a harder removal. Where the machine allows it, an interface printed in a different material solves this outright. Water-soluble PVA or BVOH dissolves away completely. Alternatively, an interface in a material that bonds poorly to the part material — PETG under PLA is the classic pairing, and vice versa — releases cleanly with no dissolution needed. The Z-gap: the parameter that decides everything The Z-gap (contact distance, top contact Z distance) is the vertical clearance left between the top of the support and the part's underside. It is the single parameter that determines whether supports snap off or fuse on. - Too large: the part's first layer over the support has nothing to sit on, and droops badly. You get an ugly underside and, at the extreme, a failed region.- Too small, or zero: the support welds to the part. Removal tears the surface, or is simply not possible without damaging the part. A common starting point is one layer height. The correct value is a material property in practice, because it depends on how aggressively the material bonds to itself — PETG self-bonds far more strongly than PLA and generally needs a larger gap for the same removability. Procedure: find your Z-gap 1. Model or download a small test block with a flat overhanging ledge, and put four copies on the plate.2. Use per-object settings (post 02) to give each copy a different Z-gap: 0, 0.5×, 1.0× and 1.5× layer height.3. Print in the material you care about, with your normal profile.4. Remove the supports from each and judge two things: how hard removal was, and how good the resulting underside is.5. Choose the largest gap that still gives an acceptable underside. Record it in your material profile.6. Repeat per material. Do not carry a PLA value into a PETG profile. There is a matching XY separation parameter controlling horizontal clearance between support and vertical part walls; the same logic applies, and it matters most for supports inside pockets and against tall walls. Support strategy options - Support on build plate only. Supports are generated only where they can reach the plate, so nothing ever touches the model's own surfaces except the underside of true overhangs above the plate. No scars on model faces. It fails whenever an overhang sits above the part itself.- Painted supports (enforcers and blockers). Paint directly on the model where you want support and where you forbid it. Almost always better than adjusting the global angle threshold, which is a blunt instrument that adds support everywhere the threshold catches.- Support blockers as modifier volumes. Drop a box over a region that must stay clean. The scar Supports always leave a mark. The interface never separates perfectly cleanly, so a supported face is rougher than an unsupported one and dimensionally less reliable. Three responses, in order of quality: 1. Design the overhang away (Chapter 6). Best answer.2. Orient so the scar lands on a face nobody sees or measures. Second best.3. Accept it and post-process — knife, file, sanding. Real, but it is manual labour on every copy. Chapter 6 — Support-free design Support is a workaround. The superior answer, wherever the part's function permits, is geometry that does not need it. Chamfers instead of overhangs Under a horizontal ledge, boss or shelf, add a 45° chamfer rather than leaving a square underside. The chamfer is self-supporting, prints cleanly and adds material exactly where a bending stress concentration would otherwise sit. Note the asymmetry with fillets: a fillet under a ledge is not a good substitute, because a fillet's surface becomes tangent to horizontal at its lower end — the worst possible overhang, right where the geometry starts. Chamfer downward-facing transitions; fillet upward-facing ones. Self-supporting geometry throughout Once you have measured your machine's overhang threshold (Chapter 3), design to it. Cones instead of cylinders on downward faces, pyramidal instead of rectangular bosses, sloped instead of flat undersides. On a part designed this way, the support setting is simply "off", and that is worth a great deal of cleanup time over a print run. Sacrificial bridge layers Over a large horizontal internal hole or pocket, a single sacrificial layer of bridging material gives the layers above something to build on. Many slicers can add this automatically for internal features; it can also be modelled deliberately as a thin membrane to be drilled out afterwards. Split the part If a part cannot be made support-free in any single orientation, cut it. Two halves, each oriented for its own loads and each printing support-free, glued or bolted together, will often beat one supported part on strength, surface and time simultaneously. See Chapter 7 for where to cut and how to join. Print in place Sometimes the correct answer to "how do I assemble this" is "don't". Print-in-place hinges, captive nuts, chain links and compliant mechanisms remove assembly entirely. They are clearance problems, and that is the topic of Chapter 7's last section. Put text and detail where the process can print it Embossed text on a top or vertical face prints legibly. Engraved text on a top face prints legibly. Text on a downward face is a support problem and will be a mess. Move it. Chapter 7 — Designing for the process Minimum wall thickness relative to extrusion width A wall is built from an integer number of roads. If your extrusion width is 0.45 mm, then: A wall of 1.0 mm asks for 2.22 roads. Classic perimeter generation lays two roads and dumps the remaining 0.10 mm into gap fill; variable-width extrusion (post 02) instead widens both roads to 0.50 mm, which is better but still a compromise. The rule: design walls as whole multiples of your extrusion width, and treat two roads as the practical structural minimum for anything that carries load or needs to survive handling. Single-road walls exist and are useful for vase-mode parts and light enclosures, but they are not structure. Note the dependency: this rule is tied to your nozzle and profile. If you design a 0.90 mm wall and later print with a 0.6 mm nozzle, the wall becomes an awkward 1.5 roads. Parametrise the wall thickness in CAD (post 06) rather than hard-coding it. Minimum feature size - Nothing narrower than one extrusion width can exist. A 0.3 mm rib on a 0.45 mm profile is simply not printed, or is printed at 0.45 mm and is therefore wrong.- Embossed or engraved detail should be at least two extrusion widths wide and at least two layers deep or tall to be legible. Below that it disappears into the surface texture.- Small holes close up. A 1 mm vertical hole in a 0.4 mm nozzle profile has room for barely one road around it; expect it to be undersized and rough, and plan to drill it. Chamfer the bottom edge Elephant foot (post 01) bulges the bottom edge outward. Slicers offer a compensation setting that shrinks the first layer inward, but the better fix lives in the model: put a small chamfer — typically a few tenths of a millimetre at 45° — on every bottom edge. The chamfer gives the squashed first layer somewhere to bulge into rather than past the nominal footprint. The part still sits flat, the base dimension is preserved, and the fix travels with the model into any slicer, on any printer, forever. This is one of the highest value-per-effort habits in the whole discipline. Text - Cap height of roughly 4–5 mm and above reads reliably; below that, legibility depends heavily on the font and on your extrusion width.- Stroke width should be at least two extrusion widths. Thin serif and script fonts do not survive; a bold sans-serif does.- Relief depth of at least two layers, both for embossed and engraved.- Embossed text generally reads better than engraved on FDM, because engraved text's fine channels get partially filled.- On a vertical face, vertical resolution of the glyph is limited by layer height, so curves and thin horizontals suffer. Thin unsupported pillars A tall, thin pillar is the classic failed feature, and there are three separate reasons: 1. It has no time to cool. Each layer is tiny, so the nozzle returns before the previous layer has solidified, and the pillar turns into a soft blob.2. It has no rigidity. The nozzle's drag and the travel moves knock it around.3. It is unstable. A tall narrow column has a small base and a high aspect ratio. Fixes, in order: taper it (a cone is stronger and cools better than a cylinder); enable minimum layer time or slow down for short layers; print several parts at once so each pillar has time to cool between visits; add a sacrificial cooling tower next to it; or reorient so the pillar lies down. Splitting and joining Where to cut. Choose a plane that (a) lets each half print support-free, (b) puts each half's layers in the right direction for its own loads, (c) does not pass through the region of highest stress, and (d) leaves a joint you can align. Cutting straight through a bending corner puts the joint exactly where the part is trying hardest to break. Alignment features. Never rely on a plain butt joint plus glue for alignment. Add: - Dowel pins — printed pins and matching holes, or bought steel/brass pins in reamed holes (more accurate).- Dovetails or keyed slots — self-aligning and carry shear load without relying on the adhesive.- Lip and groove — a step around the joint that locates the halves and increases bonded area.- A screw plus a heat-set insert — a mechanical joint that can be taken apart again. Adhesives by material. Surface preparation dominates joint strength: abrade the mating faces, degrease them, and clamp. Too small and the parts fuse into a solid lump; too large and the mechanism is sloppy. Real Analysis — 05 · Sequences of Functions, Uniform Convergence and Power Series
Created2026-05-04|Mathematics Real Analysis
Much of analysis builds functions as limits: $e^x$ as a power series, $\sin x$ as a Taylor series, the Weierstrass function of post 03 as a sum of cosines, solutions of differential equations as limits of approximations. The question is always the same: which properties survive the limit? Continuity, integrals and derivatives can all be lost under the obvious "point by point" notion of convergence. This post introduces the stronger notion that repairs most of this, uniform convergence, proves exactly what it preserves (continuity and integrals) and what it does not (derivatives, without an extra hypothesis), and then applies it to power series: radius of convergence, term-by-term calculus, Abel's theorem, and the difference between a Taylor series and the function it came from. It closes the core course with the Weierstrass approximation theorem and a map of where analysis goes next. > Series map: 00 The Real Numbers & Completeness → 01 Sequences & Series → 02 Topology of ℝ & Continuity → 03 Differentiation & Taylor's Theorem → 04 The Riemann Integral → 05 Uniform Convergence & Power Series (this post). Learning Outcome - Define pointwise and uniform convergence of a sequence of functions, and tell them apart with $x^n$ on $[0, 1]$.- Use the sup-norm test and the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence.- Prove that a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (the $\varepsilon/3$ argument).- Prove that integrals commute with uniform limits, and show with an example that pointwise limits break this.- State the theorem on limits of derivatives and give the counterexample that shows why it needs uniform convergence of the derivatives.- State and prove the Weierstrass M-test and use it on series of functions.- Compute the radius of convergence with the Cauchy–Hadamard formula and the ratio test, and check the endpoints.- Prove that a power series converges uniformly on every closed interval inside its radius, and differentiate and integrate it term by term.- State Abel's theorem and use it to show $1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \dots = \ln 2$.- Explain why a smooth function need not equal its Taylor series, and state the Weierstrass approximation theorem with the Bernstein-polynomial idea. Chapter 1 — Pointwise versus uniform convergence Terminology - Sequence of functions — functions $fn : A \to \mathbb{R}$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$, all on the same domain $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.- Pointwise convergence — $fn \to f$ pointwise on $A$ if for each fixed $x \in A$, the number sequence $fn(x)$ converges to $f(x)$. In quantifiers: for every $x \in A$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ (which may depend on both $x$ and $\varepsilon$) with $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $n \geq N$.- Uniform convergence — $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ (depending on $\varepsilon$ only) with $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $n \geq N$ and all $x \in A$.- Sup norm — for a bounded $g : A \to \mathbb{R}$, $\lVert g\rVert\infty = \sup{x \in A}\lvert g(x)\rvert$.- Epsilon-tube — the band of width $\varepsilon$ on each side of the graph of $f$. Uniform convergence says: from some $N$ on, the whole graph of every $fn$ lies inside the tube. The only difference between the two definitions is the order of "there is $N$" and "for all $x$". Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence; the converse fails. Sup-norm test. $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$ if and only if $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty \to 0$ (where $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty$ is finite for large $n$). This is the definition rewritten: "$\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $x$" gives $\sup \leq \varepsilon$, and $\sup < \varepsilon$ gives it for all $x$. The key example: x to the n on [0, 1] Let $fn(x) = x^n$ on $[0, 1]$. For $0 \leq x < 1$, $x^n \to 0$ (post 01); at $x = 1$, $1^n = 1$. So the pointwise limit is $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \leq x < 1, \\ 1 & x = 1. \end{cases}$$ Every $fn$ is continuous, and the limit is not. The convergence is not uniform: the point $xn = (1/2)^{1/n}$ lies in $[0, 1)$ and $fn(xn) - f(xn) = \frac12$, so $\lVert fn - f\rVert\infty \geq \frac12$ for every $n$ (in fact the sup over $[0,1)$ is $1$). The "bad point" slides towards $1$: | $n$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 50 ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| $xn = (1/2)^{1/n}$ | $0.5$ | $0.7071$ | $0.8706$ | $0.9330$ | $0.9862$ | For each fixed $x < 1$ you eventually get close to $0$, but no single $N$ works for all $x$ at once. On a smaller domain the problem disappears. On $[0, \frac12]$, $\lVert x^n - 0\rVert\infty = (\frac12)^n \to 0$, so $x^n \to 0$ uniformly on $[0, \frac12]$, and indeed on $[0, r]$ for any $r < 1$. Damping the bad point. Multiply by $(1 - x)$: $kn(x) = x^n(1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$. The pointwise limit is $0$ everywhere (including $x = 1$, where $kn(1) = 0$). Setting $kn'(x) = x^{n-1}\bigl(n - (n+1)x\bigr) = 0$ gives the maximum at $x = \frac{n}{n+1}$, so $$\lVert kn\rVert\infty = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \frac{1}{n+1} < \frac{1}{n+1} \to 0.$$ So $kn \to 0$ uniformly on all of $[0, 1]$. For example, $\lVert k{10}\rVert\infty = \frac{10^{10}}{11^{11}} \approx 0.0350$. The factor $(1 - x)$ kills the values near $1$ exactly where $x^n$ misbehaved. A uniform example: smoothing the corner of the absolute value Let $gn(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 1/n^2}$ on $\mathbb{R}$. With $h = 1/n$, $$0 \leq \sqrt{x^2 + h^2} - \lvert x\rvert = \frac{h^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + h^2} + \lvert x\rvert} \leq \frac{h^2}{h} = h,$$ with equality at $x = 0$. So $\lVert gn - \lvert x\rvert\rVert\infty = \frac1n \to 0$: uniform convergence to $\lvert x\rvert$ on all of $\mathbb{R}$. With $\varepsilon = 0.25$, every $gn$ with $n \geq 5$ lies inside the $\varepsilon$-tube around $\lvert x\rvert$ (its largest error is $\frac15 = 0.2$), while $g2$ (error $0.5$) does not. Every $gn$ is smooth, and the limit $\lvert x\rvert$ has a corner. Uniform convergence preserved continuity but not differentiability. Chapter 2 explains both facts. The Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence Theorem. A sequence $(fn)$ converges uniformly on $A$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ with $$\lvert fn(x) - fm(x)\rvert < \varepsilon \quad \text{for all } m, n \geq N \text{ and all } x \in A.$$ Proof. ($\Rightarrow$) If $\lvert fn - f\rvert < \varepsilon/2$ everywhere for $n \geq N$, then $\lvert fn - fm\rvert \leq \lvert fn - f\rvert + \lvert f - fm\rvert < \varepsilon$. ($\Leftarrow$) For each fixed $x$, $(fn(x))$ is a Cauchy sequence of reals, so it converges (completeness, post 01); call the limit $f(x)$. Given $\varepsilon$, take $N$ from the hypothesis. For $n \geq N$ and any $x$, let $m \to \infty$ in $\lvert fn(x) - fm(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ to get $\lvert fn(x) - f(x)\rvert \leq \varepsilon$. The same $N$ works for every $x$, so the convergence is uniform. $\blacksquare$ The value of the criterion: it proves uniform convergence without knowing the limit in advance. The M-test in Chapter 3 is built on it. Chapter 2 — What uniform convergence preserves Continuity Theorem. If each $fn$ is continuous at $c \in A$ and $fn \to f$ uniformly on $A$, then $f$ is continuous at $c$. Proof (the $\varepsilon/3$ argument). Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $N$ with $\lvert fN(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon/3$ for all $x \in A$. Since $fN$ is continuous at $c$, choose $\delta$ with $\lvert fN(x) - fN(c)\rvert < \varepsilon/3$ for $\lvert x - c\rvert < \delta$. Then for such $x$, $$\lvert f(x) - f(c)\rvert \leq \lvert f(x) - fN(x)\rvert + \lvert fN(x) - fN(c)\rvert + \lvert fN(c) - f(c)\rvert < \frac\varepsilon3 + \frac\varepsilon3 + \frac\varepsilon3 = \varepsilon. \qquad \blacksquare$$ The first and third terms need the same $N$ at two different points, $x$ and $c$. That is exactly what uniformity supplies and pointwise convergence does not. The theorem also gives a quick proof that $x^n$ does not converge uniformly on $[0, 1]$: the limit is discontinuous. A partial converse (Dini's theorem). If $K$ is compact, each $fn$ is continuous on $K$, $fn(x) \geq f{n+1}(x)$ for all $x$ and $n$, and $fn \to f$ pointwise with $f$ continuous, then the convergence is uniform. (Rudin, Theorem 7.13.) All four hypotheses are needed; $x^n$ on $[0, 1]$ fails only "the limit is continuous". Integrals Theorem. If each $fn$ is Riemann integrable on $[a, b]$ and $fn \to f$ uniformly on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable and $$\inta^b f = \lim{n \to \infty} \inta^b fn.$$ Proof. Let $\varepsilonn = \lVert fn - f\rVert\infty$, which tends to $0$. Then $fn - \varepsilonn \leq f \leq fn + \varepsilonn$ on $[a, b]$ (so $f$ is bounded). Upper and lower sums respect these inequalities, so the upper and lower integrals of $f$ (post 04) satisfy $$\inta^b fn - \varepsilonn(b - a) \leq \underline{\inta^b} f \leq \overline{\inta^b} f \leq \inta^b fn + \varepsilonn(b - a).$$ The gap between the upper and lower integrals is at most $2\varepsilonn(b - a) \to 0$, so they are equal and $f$ is integrable. The same inequalities give $\left\lvert \inta^b f - \inta^b fn\right\rvert \leq \varepsilonn(b - a) \to 0$. $\blacksquare$ Pointwise convergence is not enough. Let $fn(x) = n x (1 - x^2)^n$ on $[0, 1]$. For $0 < x < 1$, $fn(x) \to 0$ (a geometric factor beats the factor $n$), and $fn(0) = fn(1) = 0$. So $fn \to 0$ pointwise. But $$\int0^1 n x (1 - x^2)^n\,dx = \left[-\frac{n}{2(n+1)}(1 - x^2)^{n+1}\right]0^1 = \frac{n}{2(n+1)} \to \frac12 \neq 0 = \int0^1 0\,dx.$$ The convergence is far from uniform: the maximum of $fn$, at $x = 1/\sqrt{2n + 1}$, grows without bound (about $1.34$ for $n = 10$ and $4.28$ for $n = 100$). A travelling spike keeps area $\approx \frac12$ while its base shrinks. Derivatives need more Uniform convergence of $fn$ says nothing about $fn'$. Two warnings: - $gn(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 1/n^2} \to \lvert x\rvert$ uniformly (Chapter 1): smooth functions, non-differentiable limit.- $hn(x) = \frac{\sin(n^2 x)}{n} \to 0$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ (the sup norm is $\frac1n$), but $hn'(x) = n\cos(n^2 x)$, and $hn'(0) = n \to \infty$. The derivatives do not converge to the derivative of the limit, which is $0$. The fix is to assume uniform convergence of the derivatives. Theorem (limits of derivatives). Let each $fn$ be differentiable on $[a, b]$. Suppose $(fn(x0))$ converges for some $x0 \in [a, b]$, and $(fn')$ converges uniformly on $[a, b]$. Then $(fn)$ converges uniformly on $[a, b]$ to a function $f$, and $f'(x) = \lim{n \to \infty} fn'(x)$ for every $x \in [a, b]$. (Rudin, Theorem 7.17.) Idea of proof (with the extra assumption that each $fn'$ is continuous). By FTC 2 (post 04), $fn(x) = fn(x0) + \int{x0}^x fn'$. The integrals converge uniformly by the integral theorem above, so $fn \to f(x) = f(x0) + \int{x0}^x g$ with $g = \lim fn'$, which is continuous as a uniform limit. FTC 1 gives $f' = g$. The general proof uses the Mean Value Theorem instead of integrals. > Watch out: "uniformly" in this theorem is about the derivatives. Uniform convergence of $fn$ alone never licenses swapping $\lim$ and $\frac{d}{dx}$. Chapter 3 — Series of functions and the Weierstrass M-test A series of functions $\sum{k=1}^{\infty} uk(x)$ converges pointwise or uniformly on $A$ when its partial sums $Sn(x) = \sum{k=1}^{n} uk(x)$ do. The three theorems of Chapter 2 become: | If the series converges uniformly on the interval and | then ||---|---|| each $uk$ is continuous | the sum is continuous || each $uk$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ | $\int \sum uk = \sum \int uk$ (integrate term by term) || each $uk$ is differentiable, $\sum uk'$ converges uniformly, $\sum uk(x0)$ converges | $(\sum uk)' = \sum uk'$ (differentiate term by term) | Theorem (Weierstrass M-test). Suppose $\lvert uk(x)\rvert \leq Mk$ for all $x \in A$ and all $k$, and $\sum Mk$ converges. Then $\sum uk$ converges uniformly (and absolutely) on $A$. Proof. For $n > m$ and every $x \in A$, $$\lvert Sn(x) - Sm(x)\rvert = \left\lvert \sum{k=m+1}^{n} uk(x)\right\rvert \leq \sum{k=m+1}^{n} Mk.$$ The right side does not depend on $x$, and it is small for large $m$ because $\sum Mk$ converges (Cauchy criterion for number series, post 01). So the partial sums satisfy the uniform Cauchy criterion of Chapter 1. $\blacksquare$ Worked example. $F(x) = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(kx)}{k^2}$. Take $Mk = \frac{1}{k^2}$; $\sum \frac1{k^2}$ converges (its value is $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$). So the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ and $F$ is continuous. Term-by-term differentiation would give $\sum \frac{\cos(kx)}{k}$, which diverges at $x = 0$ (harmonic series). So the M-test gives continuity, and says nothing about differentiability. The same test proves the continuity of the Weierstrass function in post 03: $\lvert a^n\cos(b^n\pi x)\rvert \leq a^n$ with $\sum a^n$ a convergent geometric series. > Common pitfall: the M-test is sufficient, not necessary. $\sum{k \geq 1} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}$ (constant functions) converges uniformly, trivially, but no summable $Mk \geq \frac1k$ exists. Chapter 4 — Power series Terminology - Power series about $x0$ — a series $\sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak (x - x0)^k$ with real coefficients $ak$. Below, $x0 = 0$ for simplicity.- Radius of convergence — the number $R \in [0, \infty]$ such that the series converges absolutely for $\lvert x\rvert < R$ and diverges for $\lvert x\rvert > R$. At $x = \pm R$ anything can happen.- Limit superior — $\limsup{k} bk = \lim{n \to \infty} \sup{k \geq n} bk$, which always exists in $[-\infty, \infty]$ (monotone convergence, post 01). Radius of convergence Theorem (Cauchy–Hadamard). Let $\beta = \limsup{k \to \infty} \lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$. Then the radius of convergence is $R = 1/\beta$ (with $R = \infty$ if $\beta = 0$ and $R = 0$ if $\beta = \infty$). Proof. Apply the root test (post 01) to $\sum \lvert ak x^k\rvert$: $\limsup \lvert ak x^k\rvert^{1/k} = \beta\lvert x\rvert$. If $\beta\lvert x\rvert < 1$ the series converges absolutely; if $\beta\lvert x\rvert > 1$ the terms do not tend to $0$, so it diverges. $\blacksquare$ Ratio formula. If $\lim \left\lvert \frac{ak}{a{k+1}}\right\rvert$ exists (in $[0, \infty]$), it equals $R$. When it applies it is easier; when it does not, the root formula still works. | Series | $R$ | At $x = R$ | At $x = -R$ ||---|---|---|---|| $\sum x^k$ | $1$ | diverges | diverges || $\sum x^k / k$ (from $k = 1$) | $1$ | diverges (harmonic) | converges (alternating) || $\sum x^k / k^2$ (from $k = 1$) | $1$ | converges | converges || $\sum x^k / k!$ | $\infty$ | — | — || $\sum k!\,x^k$ | $0$ | — | — | The first three show that all endpoint behaviours occur with the same radius. When the ratio test fails. Let $ak = 2^k$ for even $k$ and $ak = 1$ for odd $k$. The ratios $\lvert ak / a{k+1}\rvert$ alternate between values tending to $0$ and to $\infty$, so they have no limit. But $\lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$ is $2$ for even $k$ and $1$ for odd $k$, so $\limsup = 2$ and $R = \frac12$. Uniform convergence inside the radius Theorem. If $0 < r < R$, the power series converges uniformly on $[-r, r]$. Consequently it converges uniformly on every compact subset of $(-R, R)$, and its sum is continuous on $(-R, R)$. Proof. Since $r < R$, the series converges absolutely at $x = r$, so $\sum \lvert ak\rvert r^k$ converges. On $[-r, r]$, $\lvert ak x^k\rvert \leq \lvert ak\rvert r^k = Mk$. Apply the M-test. A compact $K \subset (-R, R)$ is closed and bounded, so its max of $\lvert x\rvert$ is some $r < R$ and $K \subseteq [-r, r]$. Every $x \in (-R, R)$ lies in some $[-r, r]$, and continuity is local. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: the convergence need not be uniform on the whole open interval. For $\sum x^k$ on $(-1, 1)$ the tail after $x^n$ is $\sum{k > n} x^k = \frac{x^{n+1}}{1 - x}$, which is unbounded as $x \to 1^-$ for every $n$. Term-by-term differentiation and integration Theorem. Let $f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak x^k$ with radius $R > 0$. Then on $(-R, R)$: $$f'(x) = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} k\,ak x^{k-1}, \qquad \int0^x f(t)\,dt = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{ak}{k+1} x^{k+1},$$ and both new series also have radius $R$. Hence $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $(-R, R)$, and $ak = \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}$: a power series is the Taylor series of its own sum. Proof sketch. Since $k^{1/k} \to 1$, $\limsup (k\lvert ak\rvert)^{1/k} = \limsup \lvert ak\rvert^{1/k}$, so the differentiated series has the same radius (similarly for the integrated one). On any $[-r, r]$ with $r < R$ the differentiated series converges uniformly, so the derivative theorem (Chapter 2) applies. Integration follows from the integral theorem. Repeating gives all derivatives, and evaluating the $k$-th derivative at $0$ leaves only $k!\,ak$. $\blacksquare$ Worked example 1. From $\frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum{k \geq 0} x^k$ on $(-1, 1)$, differentiate: $\frac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = \sum{k \geq 1} k x^{k-1}$. Multiply by $x$ and put $x = \frac12$: $$\sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{2^k} = \frac{1/2}{(1 - 1/2)^2} = 2.$$ Worked example 2. From $\frac{1}{1 + t} = \sum{k \geq 0} (-1)^k t^k$ on $(-1, 1)$, integrate from $0$ to $x$: $$\ln(1 + x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \dots = \sum{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}x^k, \qquad \lvert x\rvert < 1.$$ Worked example 3. $E(x) = \sum{k \geq 0} \frac{x^k}{k!}$ has $R = \infty$. Differentiating term by term gives $E' = E$, with $E(0) = 1$. So $E(x) = e^x$ (the function $E(x)e^{-x}$ has zero derivative, hence is constant, by post 03). Abel's theorem at the edge The formula for $\ln(1 + x)$ was proved for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$. At $x = 1$ the series $1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \dots$ converges (alternating series test, post 01), but does it converge to $\ln 2$? Theorem (Abel). Suppose $\sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak$ converges. Then $f(x) = \sum ak x^k$ converges for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$ and $$\lim{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak.$$ Proof. Let $s = \sum ak$. Replacing $a0$ by $a0 - s$ changes $f$ by the constant $-s$ and makes the sum $0$, so assume $s = 0$. Let $sn = a0 + \dots + an$; then $sn \to 0$, so $(sn)$ is bounded and $\sum sk x^k$ converges for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$. Summation by parts ($ak = sk - s{k-1}$) gives, for $0 < x < 1$, $$f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{\infty} ak x^k = (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{\infty} sk x^k.$$ Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ with $\lvert sk\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $k > N$. Then $$\lvert f(x)\rvert \leq (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{N}\lvert sk\rvert + \varepsilon(1 - x)\sum{k > N} x^k \leq (1 - x)\sum{k=0}^{N}\lvert sk\rvert + \varepsilon.$$ The first term tends to $0$ as $x \to 1^-$, so $\lvert f(x)\rvert < 2\varepsilon$ for $x$ close enough to $1$. Hence $f(x) \to 0 = s$. $\blacksquare$ Apply it to $ak = \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}$: the left side is $\lim{x \to 1^-}\ln(1 + x) = \ln 2$ by continuity of $\ln$. So $$1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \frac14 + \dots = \ln 2 \approx 0.693147.$$ The same argument with $\arctan x = \sum{k \geq 0} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}$ gives $1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots = \frac{\pi}{4}$. (These series converge slowly: the first 10 terms of the $\ln 2$ series sum to $0.6456$.) > Common pitfall: Abel's theorem needs the series at $x = 1$ to converge. For $ak = (-1)^k$, $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x} \to \frac12$ as $x \to 1^-$, but $\sum (-1)^k$ diverges; the limit $\frac12$ is not a sum in the ordinary sense. Chapter 5 — Taylor series, approximation, and what comes next Taylor series versus the function For a $C^\infty$ function $f$, the Taylor series at $0$ is $\sum \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k$. Two things can go wrong: 1. The series can have radius $0$ (there are $C^\infty$ functions like this).2. The series can converge, to a different function. Case 2 is the function of post 03: $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ with $f(0) = 0$. All derivatives at $0$ vanish, so its Taylor series is $0 + 0x + 0x^2 + \dots$, with radius $\infty$ and sum $0$. It equals $f$ only at $x = 0$. The link to Taylor's theorem is the remainder: the Taylor series of $f$ converges to $f(x)$ exactly when $Rn(x) \to 0$. For $e^x$ at any $x$, the Lagrange remainder satisfies $\lvert Rn(x)\rvert \leq e^{\lvert x\rvert}\frac{\lvert x\rvert^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \to 0$, so $e^x$ equals its Taylor series everywhere. A function is real analytic at $x0$ when it equals a convergent power series on some interval around $x0$. By the term-by-term theorem, such a power series must be the Taylor series. So: analytic ⇒ $C^\infty$, but not conversely. The Weierstrass approximation theorem Not every continuous function is a power series, but every continuous function is uniformly close to a polynomial. Theorem (Weierstrass, 1885). Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a polynomial $p$ with $\lvert f(x) - p(x)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. Bernstein's constructive proof (idea). On $[0, 1]$, define $$Bn f(x) = \sum{k=0}^{n} f\!\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)\binom{n}{k} x^k (1 - x)^{n-k}.$$ The weights $\binom{n}{k}x^k(1-x)^{n-k}$ are the binomial probabilities of $k$ successes in $n$ trials with success probability $x$. They add up to $1$ and concentrate near $k/n \approx x$ as $n$ grows, so $Bn f(x)$ is a weighted average of values of $f$ near $x$. Uniform continuity of $f$ (post 02) turns this into $\lVert Bn f - f\rVert\infty \to 0$. Worked example. For $f(x) = x^2$ a direct computation gives $$Bn f(x) = x^2 + \frac{x(1 - x)}{n},$$ so the error is $\frac{x(1-x)}{n}$, largest at $x = \frac12$, and $\lVert Bn f - f\rVert\infty = \frac{1}{4n}$. An error of at most $0.01$ needs $n \geq 25$. The convergence is uniform but slow; Bernstein polynomials are valued for their shape-preserving properties (they are the basis of Bézier curves), not their speed. Compare with the Weierstrass function of post 03: a continuous, nowhere-differentiable function can still be approximated uniformly, to any accuracy, by polynomials, which are as smooth as functions get. Uniform limits keep continuity and lose everything finer. A look ahead - Metric spaces. Uniform convergence is convergence in the metric $d(f, g) = \lVert f - g\rVert\infty$ on $C[a, b]$. Chapter 1's Cauchy criterion, together with the continuity theorem of Chapter 2, says this space is complete, and Weierstrass says polynomials are dense in it. The same words (complete, dense, compact) from posts 00–02 now describe spaces of functions.- Lebesgue integration. The spike example $n x(1 - x^2)^n$ shows that pointwise limits and integrals do not commute in general. Lebesgue's theory says exactly when they do (monotone and dominated convergence), without asking for uniformity.- Fourier series. Replacing powers $x^k$ by $\cos(kx)$ and $\sin(kx)$ gives Fourier series. Their convergence is subtler than for power series: the Fourier series of a discontinuous square wave converges pointwise at every point but never uniformly, which ties back to the continuity theorem of Chapter 2. The slicer is where almost all of your decisions actually live. The printer executes; the slicer decides. Yet most people use it as a box with a "quality" dropdown, which means the interesting settings — the ones that decide whether a part is strong, accurate, fast or pretty — are being chosen by a vendor who has never seen your part. This post opens the box: what the slicer computes, what the G-code it emits actually says, and what each of the two hundred settings is really controlling. Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes Terminology - Mesh — the triangle soup the slicer receives. A slicer parameter, not a nozzle property. The pipeline Two things are worth noticing about this list. Step 3 is why mesh quality matters. A plane–mesh intersection only yields closed loops if the mesh is closed. A hole in the mesh gives an open polyline, which the slicer must guess how to close — and its guess is where the weird stray extrusion in the middle of your part comes from. Fix the model, not the slicer settings. Step 5 is why "top layers" behaves unintuitively. Solid skin is not a property of the top face; it is a boolean result computed per layer. A shallow dome has a top surface at nearly every layer, so it gets skin over a large part of its height, and that is why gently sloped surfaces are slow and material-hungry. Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width Layer height versus nozzle diameter The widely used rule of thumb is a practical ceiling of roughly 75–80 % of nozzle diameter — about 0.30–0.32 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical constant, and it exists for a mechanical reason worth understanding. The flat annular face around the nozzle orifice acts as a shoe. It rides above the previous layer at the layer height and irons the emerging extrudate down into it, which is what creates the wide contact patch and the pressure that drives interdiffusion (post 00). As layer height approaches nozzle diameter, the shoe stops touching the extrudate at all: material simply falls out of the orifice as a round noodle, keys poorly to the layer below, and stacks unstably. Caveats in both directions: - High-flow hotends and internally-restructured nozzles change the melting side of the problem but not the shoe geometry. Some users run higher ratios successfully; verify on your own machine before designing around it.- Going very thin has its own floor. Below roughly a quarter of nozzle diameter, small Z errors and bed-mesh residuals become a large fraction of the layer, and print time grows without a proportional gain in quality. The first-layer exception The first layer routinely ignores the rule and runs thicker — commonly 0.2–0.3 mm even in fine profiles — for two reasons: a thicker layer swallows small bed irregularities and mesh residuals, and it produces a larger contact patch for adhesion. It is also frequently printed wider than other layers for the same reason. What layer height does and does not control | Layer height controls | Layer height does not control ||---|---|| Visible stepping on sloped and curved surfaces | detail in the XY plane — that is nozzle diameter and extrusion width || The Z resolution of features (a 0.15 mm step cannot exist in a 0.2 mm layer) | hole roundness or XY dimensional accuracy || Print time — roughly inversely, but not exactly | wall thickness || The number of layer interfaces per millimetre of height | whether a part is strong (interface count and interface quality pull in opposite directions; see below) || Overhang behaviour, indirectly (each layer's unsupported step scales with height) | support requirement | On strength, resist the confident claim in either direction. Thinner layers give more interfaces but each one is smaller, cools differently and reheats more often. The published results are material- and geometry-dependent, and the effect is generally much smaller than the effect of orientation (post 03) or of wall count. Spend your attention there instead. Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter This surprises people, and it is the single most useful conceptual unlock in the slicer. The width of a deposited road is set by how much material you push per unit of travel and how high the nozzle is, not by the orifice diameter. Push more, and the extrudate spreads sideways under the shoe until it is wider. Push less, and it is narrower. The orifice only limits how fast you can push it. Usable range is roughly 100–200 % of nozzle diameter. Below about 100 %, the road becomes unreliable and the nozzle drags. Above about 200 %, the extrudate escapes past the shoe's control and the surface becomes lumpy. How to use the decoupling deliberately: | Setting | Typical choice | Why ||---|---|---|| First layer | wider than default | more contact patch, better adhesion, more tolerance of bed variation || External perimeter | at or slightly below nozzle diameter | narrower roads follow corners more tightly and give better dimensional control on the visible surface || Internal perimeters | above nozzle diameter (often 110–120 %) | fewer, fatter roads for the same wall thickness — faster and fewer internal interfaces || Solid infill | above nozzle diameter | fewer passes to fill an area || Sparse infill | wider still | infill is invisible; width buys speed directly || Support interface | narrower | finer surface where the part rests on it | > Common pitfall: setting an extrusion width and forgetting that flow calibration (post 01) targets a specific width. Change your external perimeter width and your measured wall thickness changes, which looks like a flow error and is not. Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill Perimeter count Wall thickness ≈ perimeter count × extrusion width. Two perimeters at 0.45 mm gives a 0.9 mm wall; four gives 1.8 mm. Perimeter count is usually a better investment than infill density for stiffness and strength, and the reason is elementary beam mechanics: in bending, material's contribution to stiffness scales with the square of its distance from the neutral axis. The walls are the material furthest from the neutral axis. Infill in the middle of the section contributes comparatively little. Going from two walls to four on a bracket typically does more than doubling infill, and costs less time and material. Typical practice: 2 walls for decorative parts, 3–4 for general functional parts, 5+ for parts under real load or that need to be watertight or tapped. Perimeter order Two orders, both defensible: Inner first, external last (the usual default). The external perimeter is laid against already-solid inner walls, so it has something to lean on, and nothing is printed afterwards to disturb it. Generally better surface quality and better behaviour on overhanging walls. External first. The outer wall is laid before any inner wall can push it outward, which can improve dimensional accuracy on some machines. Costs surface quality and overhang reliability. Slicers in the PrusaSlicer lineage expose this as an "external perimeters first" toggle, and some offer a hybrid ordering. The honest guidance is that the effect size is machine-dependent: print a test part both ways, measure it with calipers and look at it, and decide for your machine. Post 05 returns to the accuracy side of this. Seams Every closed loop must start and stop somewhere. At that point the extruder transitions from zero pressure to steady pressure and back, and the result is a small defect — a blob, a notch, or both — stacked on every layer. Seam placement strategies: | Strategy | Result ||---|---|| Nearest | seam goes wherever travel is shortest; fastest, scattered, unpredictable || Aligned | seams stack into a single vertical line; ugly but predictable, and you can rotate it to the back || Rear | forced to the back of the plate || Random | scattered; hides the line at the cost of scattering small defects across the whole surface || Painted | you choose, per region, in the slicer — put it inside a corner where geometry hides it | Mechanically, a stack of aligned seams is a line of small stress concentrations and reduced wall continuity, so on a pressure-bearing or load-bearing part, do not let the seam line up with the region of highest stress. Reducing the seam artefact itself: tune pressure advance (post 01), and use the slicer's wipe and coasting options, which stop extruding slightly before the end of the loop and move the nozzle to smear the remainder. Some slicers additionally offer a scarf or ramped seam that spreads the transition over a Z ramp instead of a single point; where available, it is the most effective option for a visible cylindrical surface. Gap fill When a region is too narrow for another whole perimeter but too wide to ignore, classic perimeter generation leaves a gap and fills it with a short, thin extrusion. Gap fill is a common source of noise, vibration, blobs and stuttering motion, because it is many tiny moves with rapid flow changes. The modern answer is variable-width extrusion (Chapter 6), which mostly eliminates the problem by widening the perimeters themselves to close the gap. Chapter 5 — Skins and infill The thickness-not-count rule Solid layers are specified in most slicers as a count, which is a trap. What matters physically is total thickness of solid material. Halving your layer height silently halves your top skin. The result is pillowing: the top surface sags into the infill voids and shows holes and bumps. The rule: specify a thickness, then derive the count. Around 0.8–1.2 mm of top solid is a sensible general target; more if infill density is low, because thin skin has to bridge longer unsupported spans. Modern slicers have a "minimum shell thickness" setting that enforces this automatically — turn it on. Bottom skins can be thinner, because the build plate supports them. Monotonic ordering for the top solid layer is worth enabling where available: it forces solid infill lines to be laid in a consistent direction so each new road overlaps the previous one the same way, producing a visually uniform surface instead of a patchwork. Infill patterns, honestly First, what is reliably measurable at hobby level and what is not. Measurable, repeatable, and worth optimising: estimated and actual print time, material mass (weigh the part), whether the pattern self-intersects within a layer (which makes the nozzle run over its own deposits, adding noise and artefacts), and whether the pattern is 2D (identical every layer) or 3D (varies with Z, giving out-of-plane structure). Not reliably measurable without a test rig, standardised coupons and repeated samples: the small percentage strength differences between patterns that appear in blog comparisons. These figures are usually a single specimen, in a single load direction, on a single machine, and they do not generalise. The efficient move is almost always: add walls before you add infill, for the second-moment-of-area reason in Chapter 4. Over-packing solid infill causes over-extrusion, dimensional growth and worse surfaces. If you need a solid part, increase walls until they meet in the middle. Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features Per-feature speeds Slicers set a different speed for external perimeters, internal perimeters, solid infill, sparse infill, top solid, bridges, gap fill and travel. The important asymmetry: Outer-wall speed dominates surface quality, and costs very little total time. The external perimeter is the only extrusion anyone sees; it is where ringing lands, where pressure-advance error is visible, and where corner rounding shows. It is also a small fraction of total extruded volume on most parts. Halving outer-wall speed typically adds a few percent to print time and produces a visibly better part. This is the best quality-per-minute trade in the whole slicer. Conversely, sparse infill speed is nearly free to raise — until it hits the volumetric flow ceiling you measured in post 01. Acceleration is the real print-time variable On small parts the toolhead rarely reaches its commanded speed at all. Consequences: - Raising the speed setting on a part made of short segments changes nothing. Raising acceleration does.- Raising acceleration excites resonance, which is what input shaping (post 01) exists to manage. The two settings are coupled.- Jerk (Marlin's classic model), junction deviation and Klipper's square corner velocity all describe the same thing: how much speed is retained through a direction change. Raising it speeds up detailed parts and adds ringing.- Minimum layer time / "slow down for short layers" forces small cross-sections to take long enough to solidify. It is a cooling setting wearing a speed setting's clothes. Cooling Set from material, as measured in post 01. Briefly: PLA wants a lot, PETG a moderate amount, ABS/ASA very little in an enclosure, nylon and PC little. Cooling buys overhang and detail quality and pays in layer adhesion; that trade never goes away. The quality features Ironing. After the top solid layer is complete, the nozzle sweeps the surface again at very low flow, remelting and smoothing it. Produces a genuinely flat, near-glossy top face. Costs time proportional to top-surface area, and can push a bead of material to the edges if flow is too high. Only applies to flat, horizontal top surfaces. Adaptive layer height. Layer height varies with local surface slope — thin layers where the surface is shallow (where stepping is visible), thick where it is vertical (where stepping is invisible). Excellent on organic and curved shapes, useless on prisms, and it complicates any setting that is expressed per layer. Some slicers additionally let you paint a height range by hand. Fuzzy skin. The outer wall is deliberately jittered in XY by a small random amount, giving a matte, textured surface. It hides layer lines, hides seams, and improves grip on handles. Costs a little time and a little dimensional precision on the fuzzed surface — never fuzz a mating face. Better slicers let you paint it onto selected surfaces only. Variable-width extrusion (Arachne). The perimeter generator that, instead of laying fixed-width loops and dumping the remainder into gap fill, varies each loop's width to fill the available space exactly. Thin walls, tapering ribs, embossed text and small features all improve substantially. It originated in the Cura engine and became the default perimeter generator in PrusaSlicer from version 2.5; OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio, both of PrusaSlicer lineage, carry it too. The classic fixed-width generator remains selectable and is occasionally preferred when you want perfectly uniform road width for its own sake. Modifiers and per-region settings The most under-used capability in every modern slicer. You are not limited to one setting per part. - Per-object settings — different infill, walls or supports for each object on the plate.- Height ranges — different settings for a Z band, e.g. more solid layers around a threaded region.- Modifier meshes — drop a box or cylinder into the scene and any setting inside its volume is overridden. The canonical use is 100 % infill only inside a screw boss, or extra walls only around a bearing seat.- Negative volumes — subtract geometry at slice time without touching the CAD model. Useful for a quick clearance test.- Support blockers and enforcers — force supports on or off in a region rather than fighting the global threshold.- Painted seams and painted supports — direct manual control on the model surface. > Trick: a modifier mesh is the correct answer almost every time you are tempted to raise a global setting for the sake of one feature. Raising global infill from 20 % to 60 % because one boss needs to be solid costs you the whole part's print time; a modifier box costs you the boss. Chapter 1 — The first layer: doctrine, tramming and Z-offset Terminology - Tramming — physically adjusting the bed so its plane is parallel to the plane the nozzle travels in. Often loosely called "levelling", which is misleading: gravity is irrelevant, parallelism is everything.- Z-offset — the distance between the point the printer calls Z = 0 and the point where the nozzle actually touches the bed. A single scalar, and the most consequential number on the machine.- Nozzle-to-bed gap — the physical clearance while printing the first layer. Deliberately smaller than the first-layer height, so that material is squashed.- Squish — the intentional compression of the first layer. It is what converts a round extrudate into a flat road with a wide contact patch.- Mesh bed levelling — probing a grid of points and compensating for bed shape by modulating Z during the print.- Probe repeatability — the spread of readings when the probe measures the same point many times. The floor on how good your mesh can be.- Elephant foot — the outward bulge at the bottom of a part caused by excessive first-layer squish, excessive bed temperature, or both. Why the first layer decides everything Three separate mechanisms, all rooted in the same event: 1. Adhesion is contact area. A squashed road has a wide flat contact patch against the plate; an unsquashed one touches along a line. Adhesion force scales with that contact patch, so a nozzle 50 µm too high can halve it.2. The first layer is the datum for every layer above it. Layer 2 is deposited onto layer 1 at a fixed Z step. If layer 1 is thinner than commanded, layer 2 is deposited into empty space and the error propagates upward as under-extrusion that never recovers. If layer 1 is thicker than commanded, layer 2 collides with it.3. Warping starts at the corners of the first layer. As upper layers cool and contract, they pull inward on the part. That force is resisted only by the bond between first layer and plate. A weak first layer does not resist it, so the corner lifts, and once a corner lifts the nozzle starts hitting the raised edge. The operational rule: watch the first layer finish before you leave the room. Nothing else in this hobby has a better ratio of attention spent to failures avoided. Step 1: mechanical prerequisites, before any of this Levelling compensates for shape. It cannot compensate for movement. Before touching Z-offset: - Check that the bed does not rock. Press each corner; if it moves, springs are too loose or the mounts are worn.- Check belt tension (X and Y): a belt should feel taut, and plucking it should give a defined note rather than a dull thud. Loose belts show up later as ringing and dimensional error that no calibration will fix.- Check that the gantry is square and, on dual-Z machines, that both Z motors are synchronised — Marlin's and Klipper's / exist for exactly this.- Check the nozzle is clean and cold-pulled free of debris. A blob of burnt plastic on the nozzle tip makes every subsequent measurement meaningless.- Heat everything to printing temperature before probing. Beds change shape by a measurable amount between cold and 60 °C; probes drift with temperature. Probing cold and printing hot is a common self-inflicted wound. Step 2: tram the bed Even with automatic levelling, the bed should be physically parallel to within a few tenths of a millimetre. On a four-screw bed the classic procedure is manual and works: 1. Heat bed and nozzle to the temperatures you will print at.2. Home all axes, then disable the steppers' Z compensation ( on Marlin, or simply skip mesh loading).3. Move the nozzle over screw 1, drop to Z = 0, adjust the screw until a sheet of paper drags with light, consistent friction.4. Repeat for screws 2, 3, 4, then repeat the whole cycle at least twice — adjusting one corner moves the others.5. Check the centre. If the centre is markedly high or low relative to all four corners, the bed is dished or crowned; that is what mesh compensation is for. Some machines use screw-tilt-calibrate helpers ( in Klipper, in Marlin) that probe and tell you how far and which way to turn each screw. Use them if available — they replace a subjective feel with a number. Step 3: set the Z-offset Two methods, both legitimate: The paper method. A sheet of ordinary office paper is roughly 0.1 mm thick, which is conveniently close to a good first-layer gap. Lower Z in 0.02–0.05 mm steps until the paper can still be moved but drags noticeably. The weakness is that "drags noticeably" is subjective, that paper thickness varies by brand, and that the paper compresses under the nozzle. A feeler gauge. A 0.1 mm feeler gauge is a known, incompressible thickness. Same procedure, but the result is a number you can reproduce next month. If you intend to be systematic, buy a feeler gauge set. Live adjustment during the first layer. The most practical method on a machine that supports baby-stepping: start a large first-layer test, and adjust Z live while watching the extrusion until it looks correct (Chapter 3), then save. This is the method that converges fastest because it optimises the thing you actually care about — the printed result — rather than a proxy. > Trick: record your Z-offset in your notes with the build plate it belongs to. Every plate has a different thickness, and swapping a smooth PEI sheet for a textured one changes the correct offset by an amount you will otherwise rediscover by scraping the plate. Step 4: mesh bed levelling, and its limits A probe measures the bed at a grid of points and builds a height map. During printing, the firmware adds a small Z correction as a function of X and Y so the nozzle follows the bed's actual shape. Probe technologies differ in what they physically sense: | Probe type | Senses | Strengths | Limitations ||---|---|---|---|| Inductive (proximity sensor) | proximity of a conductive/ferrous target | no moving parts, fast, cheap | only works over a metal bed; readings drift with the sensor's own temperature; senses the metal plate, not the print surface on top of it || BLTouch / CRTouch style | mechanical contact of a deployed pin | works over any surface material, well understood | deploying mechanism is a moving part that eventually fails; has an X/Y offset from the nozzle, so it cannot probe the bed edges || Eddy current | distance to a conductive plate, continuously | very fast, can scan while moving, no deployment | needs temperature compensation and calibration against a known reference; conductive plate required || Strain gauge / load cell | the force when the nozzle itself touches the bed | zero X/Y offset because the probe point is the nozzle; senses the real print surface | sensitive to what is on the nozzle tip; needs a clean nozzle every time | Whatever the type, measure its repeatability: in Klipper, in Marlin. Probe the same point ten times and look at the standard deviation. If repeatability is worse than a few tens of micrometres, your mesh is noise and you should fix that before trusting it. What auto-levelling does not fix This list saves more time than anything else in the chapter. - It does not set your Z-offset. A mesh describes the bed's shape; the offset describes the gap. You still set the gap yourself.- It does not fix a tilted bed. Compensation is applied by moving Z during motion, which skews the part's geometry and, on a badly tilted bed, can exceed the compensation range or run the Z motor at absurd speeds. Tram first, compensate second.- It does not fix a bed that moves. If the bed rocks, the mesh you measured is not the bed you print on.- It does not fix a warped plate beyond mesh resolution. A sharp local dent between probe points is invisible to a 5×5 grid.- It does not fix a dirty plate. Grease under the first layer defeats any Z value.- It does not fix an unsquare gantry. If X sags in the middle, the correction changes as Z increases, and a mesh probed at Z ≈ 0 does not know that.- It does not persist automatically. Check whether your start G-code actually loads the mesh ( / ). A probed-but-not-loaded mesh is a common silent failure. Chapter 2 — Build surfaces and adhesion The surfaces | Surface | Adhesion mechanism | Grip | Release | Best for | Watch out ||---|---|---|---|---|---|| Smooth PEI (spring steel) | chemical/polar interaction with the hot polymer | strong | good once cool, by flexing the sheet | PLA, PETG with a release agent, glossy first-layer finish | PETG can bond hard enough to tear the PEI off; use a glue-stick release layer || Textured PEI (powder-coated) | mechanical keying into the texture | strong but distributed | excellent — parts often pop free as the bed cools | the general-purpose default; PLA, PETG, TPU, ABS/ASA | texture is imprinted into the part's bottom face; hides first-layer imperfections, which cuts both ways when diagnosing || Glass | mostly adhesive-assisted; very flat | weak bare, strong with glue/hairspray | excellent as glass cools | perfectly flat bottom faces | heavy, can chip; needs an adhesive for most materials || Garolite / FR4 | mechanical + surface chemistry | strong for nylon specifically | good | nylon (PA), which sticks to garolite and to little else | mediocre for PLA || Adhesive sheets (PET/BuildTak-style) | tack | strong | varies | budget option, replaceable | consumable; gouges easily | Cleaning: soap and water beats IPA Isopropyl alcohol is a fine solvent for the last print's release agent, but it is a poor degreaser for skin oils — it dissolves them and then, as it flashes off, redeposits a thin film across the whole plate. Washing the removable sheet in a sink with warm water and ordinary dish soap, then drying with a clean paper towel, physically carries the oil away. Practical regime: - Every few prints: wipe with IPA (99 % if you can get it; the water in 70 % dries slowly).- When adhesion drops, or every 10–20 prints: soap and water wash in the sink.- Never: aggressive solvents, abrasive scourers, or metal tools on PEI. Scrape stuck plastic with a plastic scraper, or reheat the bed and let the plastic release. Why fingerprints matter PEI's adhesion is chemical. Skin oil is a non-polar film sitting between the polymer and the plate — it is a release agent, and a very effective one. A single fingerprint in the middle of the plate produces a print that adheres everywhere except a palm-sized region, and lifts there. Handle the sheet by its edges. This is not fussiness; it is the most common cause of "my bed suddenly stopped working". Chapter 3 — Reading a first-layer test print Print a large single-layer patch — most slicers can do this, and dedicated first-layer test models are common. Look at it in raking light and, if in doubt, put a caliper on it. Too high (gap too large) - Individual extrusion lines are visibly separate, with valleys between them; you can see the plate through the gaps.- Each road is round-topped rather than flat-topped.- Poor adhesion: the patch can be lifted with a fingernail, or curls at the corners.- The surface feels ribbed.- Measured thickness is approximately the commanded first-layer height (no squish) or slightly less. Too low (gap too small) - Roads are pressed so wide they overlap, and excess material is pushed up as ridges between the lines.- Surface looks translucent, burnished or scratched; on textured plates the texture is smeared.- Audible scraping, and a visible line on the plate.- Extrusion becomes inconsistent because back-pressure is fighting the extruder — sometimes with under-extrusion appearing several centimetres later.- Downstream: pronounced elephant foot on real parts.- Measured thickness is noticeably below the commanded first-layer height. Correct - Adjacent roads have merged into a continuous sheet with no gaps and no raised ridges.- Line boundaries are faintly visible but the surface is flat.- The patch is uniformly matte (textured plate) or uniformly glossy (smooth plate) — uniformity is the signal, not the finish itself.- The patch peels off the cooled plate in one piece with moderate effort and shows a clean, evenly-textured underside.- Caliper reading is close to, and slightly under, the commanded first-layer height. > Common pitfall: judging the first layer at the centre of the plate only. Check all four corners and the centre. Uniformly wrong is a Z-offset problem; wrong in one region only is a tram/mesh problem. They have completely different fixes. Chapter 4 — The calibration sequence Do these in order. Each step's result depends on all the previous ones, so a step done out of order will have to be redone. 4.0 — Prerequisites Mechanical checks from Chapter 1, a trammed bed, a clean plate, dry filament, and a known-good vendor profile as the starting point. Do not begin with a profile you found on the internet. 4.1 — Extruder rotation distance / e-steps What it fixes: the machine delivering a different length of filament than it was asked for. Everything downstream is scaled by this error, so it is first. Procedure: 1. Heat the nozzle to printing temperature (the extruder must be able to actually push).2. Put the extruder in relative mode and disable any modifiers.3. Mark the filament at a known distance above the extruder inlet — 120 mm is convenient because it leaves 20 mm after a 100 mm extrusion.4. Command a slow 100 mm extrusion. Slow matters: fast extrusion can skip or grind and will corrupt the measurement.5. Measure the remaining distance from the inlet to the mark with calipers. Actual extruded = 120 − remaining.6. Repeat three times and average. Applying the result: Note the inversion: e-steps are steps per millimetre, rotation distance is millimetres per rotation, so the correction goes the opposite way. Getting this backwards doubles your error, which is an obvious enough result that you will notice. Acceptance: repeat the test after applying. Actual extruded should land within roughly a millimetre of 100 mm. If it does not converge, the drive gear is slipping, the tension is wrong, or the filament path has a restriction. Scope: this is a per-printer value. It does not change with filament brand. 4.2 — Temperature tower What it fixes: picking a nozzle temperature by evidence rather than by the number printed on the spool. Procedure: print a tower whose temperature drops (or rises) by 5 °C per block, using either a pre-built model with embedded temperature changes or a slicer height-triggered custom G-code ( at the layer where each block starts). What you evaluate on each block, and what each tells you: | Observation | Meaning ||---|---|| Layer separation when you try to snap the block | too cold — insufficient interdiffusion (post 00, Chapter 3) || Stringing between features | too hot, or a retraction problem (isolate later, not now) || Blobbing, drooping overhangs, glossy over-melted surface | too hot || Poor bridging, curled bridge strands | too cold, or too little cooling || Rough, matte, under-fused surface | too cold | Decision rule: do not just pick the prettiest block. Pick for the property the part needs. Functional part → the hottest temperature that does not string, droop or degrade, because layer bonding improves with temperature. Visual part → the coolest temperature that still fuses cleanly. Write the choice and the reason down. Scope: per material, per spool. Pigment loading alone shifts the optimum; a black and a white PLA from the same manufacturer regularly want different temperatures. 4.3 — Flow / extrusion multiplier What it fixes: the difference between the volume the slicer thinks it deposited and the volume actually deposited. Causes include filament diameter variation, nozzle wear, and material-specific die swell. Procedure (single-wall cube): 1. Slice a simple cube (or the standard single-wall calibration cube) with one perimeter, zero top layers, zero infill, and no gap fill. Vase mode also works.2. Note the extrusion width the slicer used for that wall — this is the target, and it is not the nozzle diameter (post 02 explains why).3. Print it at your chosen temperature.4. Measure the wall thickness with calipers at eight or more places — different heights, different faces, avoiding the seam and the corners. Use light, consistent pressure; plastic deforms under a caliper jaw.5. Average the measurements. Applying the result: Acceptance: reprint and remeasure. The spread between your individual measurements tells you how much precision you actually have — if measurements vary by more than a couple of hundredths of a millimetre, your measurement technique or the machine is the limiting factor, not the flow number. Modern slicers ship guided flow calibrations (OrcaSlicer's two-pass flow-rate test, for example) that use a printed surface-quality judgement instead of a caliper. They are faster and less prone to caliper-pressure error; the single-wall method remains the one that produces a number you can defend. Scope: per spool. This is the setting most worth re-checking on every new roll. 4.4 — Retraction What it fixes: oozing during travel moves, which shows as stringing, blobs and zits. Procedure: print a retraction tower or a two-pillar stringing test, stepping one variable per tower. Tune in this order: distance, then speed, then extra restart distance / wipe / Z-hop if still needed. Typical starting ranges — these depend on melt-zone volume, extruder architecture and Bowden tube length, so treat them as where to begin the tower, not as answers: | Setup | Retraction distance, typical starting range ||---|---|| Direct drive | roughly 0.5–2 mm || Bowden | roughly 3–7 mm, longer with a longer tube | Acceptance: the shortest retraction distance that eliminates strings. Over-retraction is not free: it pulls molten material back into the cold zone where it can jam, grinds a groove into the filament on repeated retractions at the same point, and causes gaps at the start of each extrusion. > Watch out: stringing has three plausible causes — too little retraction, too high a temperature, and wet filament. Wet filament strings and pops audibly and will defeat every retraction value you try. If you hear crackling from the nozzle, dry the filament before tuning anything. Scope: per material. 4.5 — Pressure advance / linear advance What it fixes: the lag between commanded flow and actual flow caused by pressure building and releasing in the melt zone. Symptoms are bulges after a deceleration and thin, starved lines after an acceleration — most visible at corners and at the start of each line. Klipper calls it pressure advance; Marlin calls it linear advance with a K factor. Same physics, different scaling, so the numbers are not interchangeable. Procedure (Klipper): use the tuning-tower mechanism, which ramps the parameter continuously with height: Print the standard tuning tower object, then find the height where corners are sharpest and line width is most uniform through the corner. The value is — with the factor above, a good band at 8 mm means a pressure advance of 0.04. Procedure (Marlin): use the K-factor test pattern (Marlin publishes a generator) and set with . Read the pattern for the K where the fast-to-slow transition shows no bulge and the slow-to-fast transition shows no gap. Reported typical values span a wide range and depend strongly on architecture — direct-drive setups sit far lower than Bowden ones, because Bowden systems store much more compliance. Do not carry a number across from a different machine; run the tower. Scope: per material (and re-check if you change hotend or extruder). 4.6 — Maximum volumetric flow What it fixes: the ceiling on print speed. Above it, the hotend cannot melt filament fast enough and the print silently under-extrudes. Procedure: print a flow-ramp test that increases volumetric flow with height — OrcaSlicer's "Max Volumetric Speed" test does this directly, and equivalent speed towers exist for other slicers. A typical sweep starts around 5 mm³/s and climbs in small steps. Reading it: find the height where the surface changes character — extrusion becomes visibly inconsistent, the finish shifts from even to rough or unevenly glossy, or the wall thins. That height's flow is the point of failure, not the working limit. Applying the result: set your profile's maximum volumetric speed 10–20 % below the failure point. The test is a best case: it does not include retractions, sharp corners, small layers or an already-hot enclosure. Dependency: this number moves with nozzle temperature. If you later raise the temperature, the ceiling rises too — and if you re-run 4.2, re-run this. Scope: per material, per hotend, per nozzle. 4.7 — Cooling What it fixes: overhang quality, bridging, small-feature definition — traded directly against layer adhesion (post 00, Chapter 3). Procedure: print an overhang/bridging test at several fan settings, or a small tower where fan speed steps with height. Also set minimum layer time (or "slow down for short layers"), which forces small cross-sections to print slowly enough to solidify. Starting points by material, all subject to measurement: | Material | Part cooling, typical starting point | Reason ||---|---|---|| PLA | high, often near maximum | crystallises quickly; benefits from fast solidification; layer adhesion stays adequate || PETG | moderate | needs some cooling for overhangs, but too much visibly weakens layer bonding || ABS / ASA | minimal or none, in an enclosure | high shrinkage; rapid cooling causes warping and delamination || TPU | low to moderate | slow-flowing; cooling helps definition but the material is tolerant || Nylon (PA) | low | strong, hygroscopic, and very sensitive to rapid cooling || PC | low | high glass transition; needs a warm environment to bond | > Trick: cooling is the one setting where "more is better" is actively wrong for functional parts. If a bracket keeps snapping along a layer line, drop the fan before you change anything else. Scope: per material. Chapter 5 — Discipline: per spool, and one variable at a time Why calibration is per spool, not per printer Split your settings into two buckets: | Belongs to the printer | Belongs to the spool / material ||---|---|| e-steps / rotation distance | nozzle temperature || XY and Z steps per mm | bed temperature || input shaper frequencies | flow / extrusion multiplier || bed mesh | retraction distance and speed || probe offsets | pressure advance || kinematic limits (max accel, jerk/square corner velocity) | maximum volumetric flow || — | cooling and minimum layer time | The right-hand column moves because filament varies. Diameter tolerance differs between manufacturers and between batches. Pigments and fillers change melt viscosity, thermal conductivity and optimum temperature. Moisture content changes everything at once. Even two spools of nominally identical material from one vendor can want a different flow number. Practically: create a slicer filament profile per spool type you buy, not per material category, and put the measured numbers in it. The tuning-order rule Never chase two variables at once. If you change temperature and flow together and the result improves, you have learned nothing you can reuse. The sequence in Chapter 4 is ordered by dependency: rotation distance scales all extrusion, temperature changes flow behaviour and the flow ceiling, flow changes how corners look, pressure advance is judged on corner appearance, and cooling changes both bonding and overhang quality. Keep a notebook — a text file is fine. For each change record: date, printer, spool, the setting changed, the old value, the new value, the test used, and the observation. Six months later this file is the only thing that distinguishes "my printer got worse" from "I changed something in March". Chapter 1 — Additive versus subtractive Terminology - Additive manufacturing (AM) — building a part by adding material, usually in layers, from a digital model. The formal ISO/ASTM term; "3D printing" is the colloquial synonym.- Subtractive manufacturing — starting from a solid blank and removing material: milling, turning, drilling, grinding, laser and waterjet cutting.- Formative manufacturing — forcing material into a shape without adding or removing it: injection moulding, casting, forging, sheet bending.- Layer — one planar slice of the part, deposited or cured in a single pass. The layer is the fundamental quantum of almost all AM processes.- Anisotropy — direction-dependent material properties. A part that is strong along X and weak along Z is anisotropic.- Build volume — the maximum envelope the machine can print inside, usually quoted as X × Y × Z in millimetres. What additive is genuinely good at The honest list is shorter than the marketing list: 1. Geometric complexity is free. A lattice, an internal channel that curves, a topology-optimised bracket — all cost the same per gram as a plain block. On a mill, every feature costs a setup, a tool and a toolpath.2. Quantity one is cheap. There is no tooling. The marginal cost of the first part equals the marginal cost of the thousandth, which is exactly the property injection moulding does not have.3. Iteration latency is hours, not weeks. The design loop closes the same day. For fixtures, jigs and prototypes this is the entire value proposition.4. Internal voids and enclosed geometry. A hollow shell with internal ribs is trivial additively and often impossible subtractively without splitting the part.5. On-demand spares. A discontinued knob or bracket becomes a file rather than a supply-chain problem. What subtractive is still better at 1. Tolerance and surface finish out of the machine. A milled feature can hold tolerances an order of magnitude tighter than a hobby FDM part, with a surface that needs no post-processing.2. Isotropic material properties. Stock aluminium or acetal has the same strength in every direction because it was never built in layers.3. Certified material provenance. You can buy a billet with a mill certificate. Filament data sheets are far weaker evidence.4. Volume economics. Above a few hundred identical parts, moulding or machining wins on cost per part, usually decisively.5. Flatness, roundness and bore quality. A reamed hole is round. A printed hole is a polygon approximation with a seam in it — post 03 and post 05 spend real time on this. | Criterion | Additive (desktop) | Subtractive (CNC) | Formative (injection moulding) ||---|---|---|---|| Tooling cost | none | fixturing only | high, dominates low volumes || Cost at quantity 1 | lowest | moderate | prohibitive || Cost at quantity 10 000 | worst | moderate | lowest || Geometric complexity | nearly free | expensive | limited by draft and ejection || Internal channels | easy | hard or impossible | needs cores/inserts || Achievable tolerance | loosest | tightest | tight and repeatable || Material isotropy | poor (FDM) to good (SLS) | excellent | excellent || Lead time for a change | hours | days | weeks (retool) | > Trick: the question is never "can I print this?" but "is printing the cheapest path to a correct part?" Chapter 2 — The process families Four families cover essentially everything a reader of this course will encounter. Only the first two are realistically owned at home; the third is bought as a service; the fourth is named for completeness. FDM / FFF — fused deposition modelling A thermoplastic filament is pushed through a heated nozzle and deposited as a road of molten polymer that fuses to its neighbours. "FDM" was a Stratasys trademark; "FFF" (fused filament fabrication) is the vendor-neutral name for the identical process. This is the overwhelming majority of desktop machines and the default assumption of this course unless stated otherwise. Resin — SLA and MSLA A photopolymer resin is cured layer by layer with ultraviolet light. SLA steers a laser spot across the surface; MSLA (masked stereolithography) shines a UV LED array through an LCD panel that masks the whole layer image at once, so exposure time is independent of how much area is being cured. Every affordable desktop resin printer today is MSLA. SLS — selective laser sintering A laser fuses regions of a bed of polymer powder (usually nylon PA12). Unfused powder supports the part, so overhangs and captive geometry need no support structures. Industrial and service-bureau territory; a handful of "benchtop" SLS machines exist but the powder handling is a serious commitment. Metal processes — one line Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF/SLM/DMLS), binder jetting and directed energy deposition all exist and all produce genuinely useful metal parts, but they are capital equipment with inert-gas, powder-safety and post-machining requirements that put them entirely outside the hobby envelope — buy metal parts as a service, do not plan to own the machine. Chapter 3 — The physics of FDM Terminology - Melt zone — the region inside the hotend where the filament is above its melting or softening point and behaves as a viscous fluid.- Heatbreak — the deliberately thin-walled, low-conductivity section between the heatsink and the heater block that keeps the melt zone short and well defined.- Heat creep — failure mode where the melt zone migrates upward past the heatbreak, softening filament in the cold section and jamming it.- Extrudate — the material that has left the nozzle.- Road / extrusion — one deposited line of extrudate. Its cross-section is roughly a rectangle with rounded ends, width × layer height.- Weld / interface — the boundary between two roads, either side by side within a layer or stacked between layers.- Volumetric flow — the rate at which the hotend can melt and deliver material, in mm³/s. The real speed limit of the machine. The filament is a plunger The extruder gears grip the filament and push it into the hotend. Solid filament above the melt zone acts as a piston; the molten polymer below it is the working fluid. This is a positive-displacement pump, and the consequences are worth stating explicitly: - Flow is commanded by distance, not by pressure. The firmware asks for "extrude 2.31 mm of filament", and the volume delivered is that length times the filament cross-section. If the filament diameter is not what the slicer assumed, every extrusion in the file is wrong by the same ratio.- The system is compliant. Molten polymer compresses slightly, the filament itself buckles slightly, the PTFE liner or Bowden tube stretches. Pressure therefore lags the commanded flow — which is exactly the problem that pressure advance / linear advance exists to correct (post 01).- The grip is the weak link. If the drive gear cannot push hard enough, it grinds a flat into the filament and stops gripping. Under-extrusion that gets progressively worse over minutes is nearly always this. Melting, and why volumetric flow is the real limit Heat has to travel from the heater block into the filament core by conduction, and polymers are poor thermal conductors. The maximum flow the hotend supports is set by how much filament it can bring to temperature per second — which depends on melt-zone length, nozzle temperature, and the material's own thermal properties. This is why the same hotend will happily run PLA far faster than PETG, and why raising nozzle temperature raises attainable flow. Practically: your printer does not have a "maximum speed" in mm/s. It has a maximum volumetric flow in mm³/s, and speed follows from it. If that number exceeds what the hotend can melt, the print under-extrudes no matter what the slicer says. Post 01 gives the measurement procedure for finding your own machine's ceiling; do not trust a headline figure from a product page, because it is measured with a specific material at a specific temperature that is probably not yours. Layer bonding is polymer chain diffusion This is the single most important piece of physics in the course. When a hot road is laid onto a cooler road below it, the two do not glue together and they do not melt into a homogeneous mass. What happens is interdiffusion: polymer chains near the interface, if they are above the glass transition and mobile enough, wriggle across the boundary by reptation — snake-like motion along the tube formed by neighbouring chains — and entangle with chains on the other side. The strength of the weld is a function of how many chains crossed and how far, which is a function of interface temperature and time above that temperature. The classic treatment is Wool and O'Connor's crack-healing theory, and it maps onto FDM almost directly. The engineering consequences follow immediately: - Hotter nozzle → stronger layer bond, up to the point where the material degrades or the part slumps. This is a real, repeatable lever.- More cooling → weaker layer bond. Part cooling fans buy overhang quality and surface detail and pay for it in Z strength. Post 04 and post 07 return to this trade.- Smaller layer height → more interfaces per millimetre, but each interface is also smaller and the part reheats more often. The net effect on strength is material-dependent and genuinely contested; do not repeat a blanket claim in either direction.- An enclosure raises ambient temperature, which slows cooling at the interface and improves bonding. This is most of why ABS, ASA and PC want an enclosure.- Long print times per layer let the previous layer cool further. A tall thin part and a tall wide part with identical settings do not have identical layer adhesion. Anisotropy, stated properly A load applied along the roads is carried by polymer chains that run continuously through the extrudate — essentially the bulk strength of the material, minus voids. A load applied across the layers is carried only by the chains that diffused across each weld interface, plus whatever mechanical interlock the road geometry provides. The Z direction is therefore always weaker, and the failure mode is different too: in-plane failure tends to be ductile yielding, while interlayer failure tends to be a clean, brittle-looking separation along a layer line. How much weaker? Published figures for Z-direction tensile strength as a fraction of in-plane strength scatter enormously — across materials, nozzle temperatures, layer heights, cooling settings, enclosure conditions and test-coupon geometry. Treat any single percentage you read as a statement about that study's conditions, not about your printer. What is robust, and what you should carry forward, is the ordering: in-plane strong, interlayer weak, and the gap is large enough to change the design. > Common pitfall: printing a hook, a lever or a bracket flat on the bed because it "fits better that way", so that the working load pulls the layers apart. This is the most common structural failure in hobby printing and it is entirely an orientation decision. Post 03 works the example through in detail. Chapter 4 — Printer anatomy The extruder: direct drive versus Bowden The extruder is the geared drive that grips and pushes the filament. Two architectures: Direct drive puts the extruder motor on the moving toolhead, immediately above the hotend. The filament path from gear to melt zone is short — typically tens of millimetres. Bowden puts the motor on the frame and pushes filament through a PTFE tube, often several hundred millimetres, to a hotend that carries no motor. | | Direct drive | Bowden ||---|---|---|| Toolhead mass | higher | lower || Achievable acceleration before ringing | lower | higher || Retraction distance needed | short (roughly a couple of millimetres) | long (often several times more) || Responsiveness to flow changes | good | sluggish; more pressure-advance compensation needed || Flexible filament (TPU) | workable | difficult to painful || Maintenance access | motor in the hot, moving area | motor accessible, tube is a wear item | Neither is "better". Direct drive trades acceleration for control; Bowden trades control for acceleration. Modern direct-drive extruders have got light enough that the historical acceleration argument is much weaker than it was. The hotend Stacked from cold to hot: heatsink (with a fan that must never be switched off while hot), heatbreak, heater block carrying a cartridge heater and a thermistor, and the nozzle. The heatbreak's whole job is to make the transition from cold to molten as short as possible. A long, sloppy transition zone means a long column of soft filament that behaves like a spring, degrading extrusion control, and it invites heat creep. Two hotend classes matter: - PTFE-lined: a PTFE tube runs all the way down to the nozzle. Cheap, forgiving, excellent for PLA and PETG. The ceiling is the PTFE itself, which begins to degrade at elevated temperature and releases decomposition products; the widely used practical limit is around 240–250 °C, and going above it is both a part-quality and an air-quality problem.- All-metal: no PTFE in the hot zone, so high-temperature materials become possible. In exchange the melt zone is less well constrained and heat creep is a more real risk, particularly with materials that soften gradually. The nozzle - Diameter sets the minimum feature width and, with layer height, the volumetric flow available. 0.4 mm is the default for good reasons: it balances detail against speed. 0.2 mm buys detail and costs enormous print time; 0.6 and 0.8 mm buy speed and strength on functional parts and cost fine detail.- Material matters as soon as you print composites. Brass conducts heat well and is soft; any filament loaded with carbon fibre, glass fibre, metal powder or glow-in-the-dark phosphor is abrasive and will wear a brass nozzle out of round in hours. Hardened steel, tool steel or a carbide/ruby-tipped nozzle is required for those, at a small cost in thermal conductivity.- Wear is invisible until it is not. A worn nozzle prints wider than commanded and loses corner definition. If dimensional behaviour drifts over months, measure the nozzle orifice or simply replace it — nozzles are consumables. The bed and adhesion surfaces The bed must be flat, at a controlled temperature, and chemically willing to hold the first layer and then let it go. Common surfaces, covered properly in post 01: - Smooth PEI — strong, chemically-driven adhesion; glossy first-layer finish; can hold some materials (PETG especially) hard enough to damage the sheet without a release agent.- Textured PEI — mechanical keying rather than pure chemical grip; matte first layer that hides imperfections; releases much more willingly as the bed cools. The forgiving default.- Glass — extremely flat, usually needs an adhesive (glue stick, hairspray); prints release as the glass cools.- Garolite / FR4 — the traditional answer for nylon, which sticks to it well and to most other things poorly. Removable spring-steel sheets on a magnetic base have become the norm because flexing the sheet is the single most reliable release mechanism. Motion systems | System | How it moves | Consequences ||---|---|---|| Bedslinger (Cartesian i3) | bed moves in Y, gantry carries X, Z lifts the gantry | simple, cheap, easy to service. Moving mass in Y includes the part, so ringing and accuracy get worse as the print gets taller and heavier. Tall thin parts are its weakness. || CoreXY | two frame-mounted motors drive both X and Y through a crossed belt path; the bed only moves in Z | constant, low moving mass regardless of part size; supports much higher acceleration; the natural choice for fast machines. Belt path is longer and belt tension/squareness matter more. || Delta | three vertical towers with arms meeting at an effector | very low moving mass, very high speed potential, tall cylindrical build volume. Position is a trigonometric function of three carriages, so calibration is fussy and accuracy varies across the bed. | Ringing (also called ghosting) is the ripple you see downstream of a sharp corner: the toolhead's acceleration step excites a mechanical resonance in the frame and gantry, and the vibration is printed into the surface. It is a function of moving mass, frame stiffness, belt tension and acceleration. The modern fix is input shaping — the firmware pre-shapes the motion command to cancel the dominant resonance, with the resonant frequency measured using an accelerometer. Klipper implements this as ; Marlin has its own input-shaping implementation in recent versions. Either way it is a measurement, not a preset: the frequency belongs to your machine. Chapter 5 — The resin process, and its safety requirements How MSLA actually works 1. The build plate descends into a vat of liquid photopolymer until the gap between plate (or the last cured layer) and the transparent film at the bottom equals one layer height.2. A UV LED array beneath the vat illuminates an LCD panel. The panel is driven as a mask: pixels for the current cross-section are transparent, everything else is opaque.3. The resin in the illuminated gap crosslinks and cures for the programmed exposure time. Because the whole layer is exposed at once, exposure time is independent of layer area — a full-plate layer takes the same time as a single tiny part.4. The plate lifts, peeling the cured layer off the film, then returns for the next layer. Cure chemistry is free-radical or cationic photopolymerisation initiated by UV, on desktop machines typically around the 405 nm range. The correct exposure time is a property of the resin, the panel and the layer height together, so it must be found by test — printed exposure-test models are the standard method, and every resin change means a new test. Lift and peel forces — the real difficulty The cured layer is stuck to two things: the previous layer (which is what you want) and the release film at the bottom of the vat (which you do not). Separating it requires overcoming adhesion and the suction created as resin has to flow in behind the retreating part. Peel force scales with the cross-sectional area being separated, which is why: - Large flat cross-sections parallel to the plate are the worst case, and are why parts are usually tilted rather than laid flat.- Hollowing a model with adequate drain holes dramatically reduces both peel force and resin consumption. Without drain holes, a hollow shell becomes a suction cup and can pull itself off the plate or deform.- Slow lift speed, a lift height that clears the part, and a light-off delay (a pause before exposure that lets resin flow back into the gap) all buy reliability at the cost of print time.- Failures show up as layers torn off and left stuck to the film, a part detached from the supports, or a "layer shift" that is really a partial delamination. Bottom layers get a much longer exposure to key the part to the plate. Over-exposing them causes elephant foot in exactly the same way that a too-low first layer does on FDM. Post-processing is part of the process, not an afterthought Drain the part, wash it in isopropyl alcohol (or water for water-washable resins) to remove uncured surface resin, dry it, then post-cure it under UV. Post-cure is not cosmetic — mechanical properties and, to a small degree, dimensions change between the green state and the fully cured state. Over-curing makes many resins more brittle. Safety — the non-negotiable part Uncured photopolymer resin is a skin and respiratory irritant, and formulations commonly contain sensitisers: substances where repeated exposure can trigger an allergic response that becomes progressively worse and can be effectively permanent. This is not a "be a bit careful" hazard; it is a "the third exposure can be much worse than the first" hazard, and it is the reason experienced resin users are strict about it. The working rules: - Nitrile gloves, always. Not latex — latex is not an adequate barrier for these chemistries. Change them when contaminated; do not touch door handles, phones or keyboards while gloved.- Eye protection when handling the vat, decanting resin, or working with a wash tank.- Ventilation. A dedicated ventilated space, extracted outdoors where possible. A closed cupboard in a bedroom is not ventilation. Enclosures with carbon filters reduce odour more reliably than they reduce hazard.- Skin contact → soap and warm water immediately. Do not wash resin off with IPA or any other solvent; solvents carry the resin into the skin rather than off it.- Read the SDS for the specific resin. Formulations differ substantially between vendors and even between product lines from one vendor, and the safety data sheet is the authoritative document.- Waste disposal. Never pour resin, resin-contaminated IPA or wash water down a drain. Cure liquid waste to a solid under UV or sunlight, then dispose of the solid according to local regulations. Contaminated IPA can be cured, the solids filtered out, and the IPA reused. Paper towels, gloves and failed prints are all contaminated waste until cured.- Keep it out of food areas and away from children and pets, permanently. > Watch out: the largest exposure risk with resin is not printing; it is the wash and cleanup step, where hands are near liquid resin and solvent for extended periods. Set that station up properly before the first print, not after the first mess. Chapter 6 — The pipeline: CAD → mesh → slicer → G-code → printer Every post in this series lives at one stage of this chain. Knowing which stage a problem belongs to is most of debugging. Stage by stage CAD holds exact geometry. A cylinder is a mathematical cylinder. Post 06 covers CAD for printing properly. Mesh export is lossy and irreversible. The solid is tessellated into triangles, and the tessellation tolerance you choose at export decides how faceted your curves are. Export too coarse and a 20 mm hole visibly becomes a polygon; export absurdly fine and you get a slow, enormous file for no benefit. - STL stores nothing but a bag of triangles. No units, no colour, no materials, no assembly structure, no metadata. The unit ambiguity is why models occasionally arrive at 1/25.4 scale.- 3MF is a modern zipped-XML format that carries units, transforms, colours, materials, multiple objects and slicer metadata, and is far less prone to invalid geometry. Prefer 3MF wherever both ends support it — which today is essentially all mainstream CAD and all mainstream slicers.- STEP is a true solid format; several slicers now import it directly and tessellate internally, which removes the export-tolerance decision from your hands but also removes a failure mode. Slicing turns the mesh into toolpaths and is the subject of post 02. This is where the overwhelming majority of your decisions actually live. G-code is a flat, human-readable list of machine commands. A one-line taste: Resin printers are the exception: they consume a stack of sliced images plus per-layer exposure and motion parameters, in vendor container formats (, , and friends) rather than G-code. Some FDM vendors also ship binary G-code containers to cut file size and parse time. Firmware parses the file, does look-ahead motion planning and acceleration limiting, runs the heater control loops and the safety interlocks. Post 01 gives an orientation to the firmware landscape. > Trick: when something is wrong, identify the stage before you change a setting. Wrong shape → CAD or mesh. Right shape, wrong toolpaths → slicer. Right toolpaths, wrong physical result → printer, material or calibration. Changing slicer settings to fix a mesh problem is the most common wasted afternoon in this hobby. Buying criteria, not model names Specific model recommendations date within months. Criteria do not. Score candidates against these: 1. Build volume you actually need. Most parts most people print fit in a 200 mm cube. Paying for a larger machine you use twice is a worse deal than paying for a better small one.2. Enclosure — required or not? If ABS, ASA, PC or nylon are on the roadmap, an enclosure is close to mandatory. If it is PLA and PETG only, an enclosure is a nice-to-have that mostly buys quiet.3. Hotend class and maximum temperature. All-metal and a genuine 280–300 °C capability keep engineering materials available. PTFE-lined caps you around 240–250 °C.4. Extruder architecture. Direct drive if TPU or fine retraction control matters; either is fine otherwise.5. Motion system. CoreXY if speed and tall parts matter; bedslinger if simplicity, serviceability and price matter.6. Add spare nozzles, extra build plates, filament, a dry box, and — for resin — gloves, IPA, a wash/cure station and ventilation. "The area under the curve" is a picture, not a definition. This post turns it into one. You trap the area between an upper sum that overestimates it and a lower sum that underestimates it, and call the function integrable when the trap can be closed as tightly as you like. From that single idea come proofs that every continuous function and every monotone function is integrable, a function that is not integrable (the Dirichlet function), a surprising one that is (Thomae's function), and both halves of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the result that links this post to the derivatives of post 03. Chapter 1 — Partitions and Darboux sums Terminology - Bounded function — throughout, $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$ and $\lvert f(x)\rvert \leq B$ for all $x$. Riemann integration is only defined for bounded functions on closed, bounded intervals; everything else is handled by improper integrals (Chapter 6).- Partition — a finite set $P = \{x0, x1, \dots, xn\}$ with $a = x0 < x1 < \dots < xn = b$. The $k$-th subinterval is $[x{k-1}, xk]$, of length $\Delta xk = xk - x{k-1}$.- Mesh — the length of the longest subinterval, $\maxk \Delta xk$.- Refinement — $Q$ refines $P$ if $P \subseteq Q$ (it keeps every point of $P$ and may add more). The common refinement of $P1$ and $P2$ is $P1 \cup P2$.- Local sup and inf — $Mk = \sup\{f(x) : x \in [x{k-1}, xk]\}$ and $mk = \inf\{f(x) : x \in [x{k-1}, xk]\}$. These exist because $f$ is bounded (completeness, post 00).- Upper and lower sums — $$U(f, P) = \sum{k=1}^{n} Mk\,\Delta xk, \qquad L(f, P) = \sum{k=1}^{n} mk\,\Delta xk.$$ Since $mk \leq Mk$, $L(f, P) \leq U(f, P)$ for every partition. Picture tall rectangles that cover the graph and short rectangles that sit under it. The refinement lemma Lemma. If $P \subseteq Q$, then $$L(f, P) \leq L(f, Q) \leq U(f, Q) \leq U(f, P).$$ Refining can only raise lower sums and lower upper sums. Proof. By induction it is enough to add one point $z$ to $P$, inside $[x{k-1}, xk]$. Only the $k$-th term of the upper sum changes. It is replaced by $$M'\,(z - x{k-1}) + M''\,(xk - z),$$ where $M'$ and $M''$ are the sups of $f$ over $[x{k-1}, z]$ and $[z, xk]$. A sup over a smaller set is no bigger, so $M', M'' \leq Mk$, and the new term is at most $Mk(xk - x{k-1})$. So $U(f, Q) \leq U(f, P)$. The argument for lower sums is the same with infs, which can only go up. $\blacksquare$ Corollary. For any two partitions $P1, P2$: $L(f, P1) \leq U(f, P2)$. Proof. With the common refinement $Q = P1 \cup P2$: $L(f, P1) \leq L(f, Q) \leq U(f, Q) \leq U(f, P2)$. $\blacksquare$ Upper and lower integrals Every lower sum is a lower bound for the set of upper sums, so by completeness the following exist and satisfy $L(f,P) \leq \underline{\int} f \leq \overline{\int} f \leq U(f,P)$ for every $P$: $$\underline{\inta^b} f = \supP L(f, P), \qquad \overline{\inta^b} f = \infP U(f, P).$$ Definition. A bounded $f$ is (Riemann) integrable on $[a, b]$ if $\underline{\inta^b} f = \overline{\inta^b} f$. The common value is $\inta^b f$ or $\inta^b f(x)\,dx$. > Watch out: this is the Darboux form of the definition. The Riemann-sum form picks a sample point $tk$ in each subinterval and asks that $\sum f(tk)\Delta xk$ tend to a limit as the mesh tends to $0$. The two definitions give the same integrable functions and the same values (Bartle and Sherbert, Section 7.4). The Darboux form is easier to prove things with, because upper and lower sums do not depend on any choice of points. Chapter 2 — The Riemann criterion and a worked example Theorem (Riemann criterion). A bounded $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$ if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a partition $P$ with $$U(f, P) - L(f, P) < \varepsilon.$$ Proof. ($\Leftarrow$) For any such $P$, $$0 \leq \overline{\int} f - \underline{\int} f \leq U(f, P) - L(f, P) < \varepsilon.$$ The difference is a non-negative number smaller than every $\varepsilon > 0$, so it is $0$. ($\Rightarrow$) Let $I$ be the common value and $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $I = \infP U$, there is $P1$ with $U(f, P1) < I + \varepsilon/2$. Since $I = \supP L$, there is $P2$ with $L(f, P2) > I - \varepsilon/2$. For the common refinement $P = P1 \cup P2$ the refinement lemma gives $$U(f, P) - L(f, P) \leq U(f, P1) - L(f, P2) < \varepsilon. \qquad \blacksquare$$ The criterion is what you use in practice: you never compute a sup over all partitions, you exhibit one good partition for each $\varepsilon$. Worked example: the integral of x squared on [0, 1] Let $f(x) = x^2$ and let $Pn$ be the uniform partition $xk = k/n$, $k = 0, \dots, n$, so every $\Delta xk = 1/n$. Since $f$ is increasing on $[0, 1]$, on $[x{k-1}, xk]$ the inf is at the left end and the sup at the right end: $$mk = \left(\frac{k-1}{n}\right)^2, \qquad Mk = \left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^2.$$ Using $\sum{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$: $$U(f, Pn) = \frac{1}{n^3}\sum{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}, \qquad L(f, Pn) = \frac{1}{n^3}\sum{k=1}^{n} (k-1)^2 = \frac{(n-1)(2n-1)}{6n^2}.$$ Subtracting, $$U(f, Pn) - L(f, Pn) = \frac{1}{n^3}\bigl(n^2 - 0^2\bigr) = \frac{1}{n},$$ because the two sums share every term except the largest square $n^2$ and the smallest $0^2$. Given $\varepsilon > 0$, any $n > 1/\varepsilon$ works, so $f$ is integrable. Both sums tend to $\frac{2}{6} = \frac13$, and $L(f, Pn) \leq \int0^1 x^2\,dx \leq U(f, Pn)$ for every $n$, so $$\int0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac13.$$ | $n$ | $L(f, Pn)$ | $U(f, Pn)$ | $U - L$ ||---|---|---|---|| 4 | $7/32 = 0.21875$ | $15/32 = 0.46875$ | $1/4$ || 10 | $57/200 = 0.285$ | $77/200 = 0.385$ | $1/10$ || 100 | $0.32835$ | $0.33835$ | $1/100$ | > Trick: for an increasing function on a uniform partition, the gap rectangles stack into a single column of width $\frac{b-a}{n}$ and height $f(b) - f(a)$. So $U - L = \frac{(f(b) - f(a))(b - a)}{n}$ at once. That is the whole proof of the next chapter's monotone theorem. Chapter 3 — Which functions are integrable? Monotone functions Theorem. If $f$ is monotone on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable. Proof. Say $f$ is increasing (it is then bounded by $f(a)$ and $f(b)$). On the uniform partition $Pn$, $Mk = f(xk)$ and $mk = f(x{k-1})$, so the sum telescopes: $$U(f, Pn) - L(f, Pn) = \frac{b - a}{n}\sum{k=1}^{n}\bigl(f(xk) - f(x{k-1})\bigr) = \frac{(b - a)\bigl(f(b) - f(a)\bigr)}{n}.$$ This is smaller than any given $\varepsilon$ once $n$ is large. $\blacksquare$ A monotone function may have infinitely many jumps (for instance one at every $1/k$), and it is still integrable. Continuous functions Theorem. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, then $f$ is integrable. Proof. A continuous function on the compact set $[a, b]$ is bounded (EVT) and uniformly continuous (post 02). Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $\delta > 0$ such that $\lvert x - y\rvert < \delta$ implies $\lvert f(x) - f(y)\rvert < \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}$. Take any partition $P$ with mesh $< \delta$. On each subinterval, $f$ attains its max and min (EVT again) at points $sk, tk$ with $\lvert sk - tk\rvert < \delta$, so $Mk - mk = f(sk) - f(tk) < \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}$. Therefore $$U(f, P) - L(f, P) = \sum{k=1}^{n} (Mk - mk)\,\Delta xk < \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}\sum{k=1}^{n} \Delta xk = \varepsilon. \qquad \blacksquare$$ > Common pitfall: plain continuity is not enough for this proof. You need one $\delta$ that works on every subinterval at once, which is exactly uniform continuity. That is why the theorem is stated on a closed, bounded interval. Finitely many discontinuities Theorem. If $f$ is bounded on $[a, b]$ and continuous except at finitely many points, then $f$ is integrable. Sketch. Say $\lvert f\rvert \leq B$ and the bad points are $c1, \dots, cm$. Put a small interval of length $\eta$ around each $cj$. Their total contribution to $U - L$ is at most $2B \cdot m\eta$, which is small when $\eta$ is. On the remaining closed intervals $f$ is continuous, so the previous theorem makes their contribution as small as you like. $\blacksquare$ Changing a function at finitely many points does not change whether it is integrable, or the value of the integral. A function that is not integrable: Dirichlet Let $D(x) = 1$ if $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $D(x) = 0$ otherwise, on $[0, 1]$. Every subinterval of positive length contains both rationals and irrationals (density of $\mathbb{Q}$ and of the irrationals, post 00), so $Mk = 1$ and $mk = 0$ for every $k$ and every partition. Therefore $U(D, P) = 1$ and $L(D, P) = 0$ always, the upper integral is $1$, the lower integral is $0$, and $D$ is not Riemann integrable. A function that is integrable: Thomae Thomae's function on $[0, 1]$ is $$T(x) = \begin{cases} 1/q & \text{if } x = p/q \text{ in lowest terms with } q \geq 1, \\ 0 & \text{if } x \text{ is irrational}, \end{cases}$$ with $T(0) = 1$ (as $0 = 0/1$). It is continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every rational (post 02). Even so: Claim. $T$ is integrable on $[0, 1]$ and $\int0^1 T = 0$. Proof. Every subinterval contains an irrational, so $mk = 0$ and $L(T, P) = 0$ for every $P$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. The points where $T(x) \geq \varepsilon/2$ are the fractions $p/q$ with $q \leq 2/\varepsilon$; there are finitely many, say $N$. Choose a partition in which each of these $N$ points lies in subintervals of total length less than $\varepsilon/2$ (for example, give each point its own interval of length less than $\frac{\varepsilon}{4N}$; a point on a boundary touches at most two). On those subintervals $Mk \leq 1$, contributing less than $\varepsilon/2$. On all other subintervals $Mk < \varepsilon/2$, contributing at most $\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot 1$. So $U(T, P) < \varepsilon$. By the Riemann criterion $T$ is integrable, and since every lower sum is $0$, the integral is $0$. $\blacksquare$ For a feel of the numbers: with $\varepsilon = \frac15$, the points where $T \geq \frac1{10}$ are the fractions in $[0, 1]$ with denominator at most $10$, and there are exactly $33$ of them. Chapter 4 — Properties of the integral Let $f, g$ be integrable on $[a, b]$ and $k \in \mathbb{R}$. | Property | Statement ||---|---|| Linearity | $f + g$ and $kf$ are integrable; $\int (f + g) = \int f + \int g$ and $\int kf = k\int f$ || Monotonicity | if $f \leq g$ on $[a, b]$ then $\inta^b f \leq \inta^b g$ || Additivity | for $a < c < b$: $f$ integrable on $[a, b]$ iff integrable on $[a, c]$ and $[c, b]$, and $\inta^b f = \inta^c f + \intc^b f$ || Triangle inequality | $\lvert f\rvert$ is integrable and $\lvert \inta^b f\rvert \leq \inta^b \lvert f\rvert$ || Bound | if $m \leq f \leq M$ then $m(b - a) \leq \inta^b f \leq M(b - a)$ || Products | $fg$ is integrable | Proof of the sum rule. On any subinterval, $\sup(f + g) \leq \sup f + \sup g$ and $\inf(f + g) \geq \inf f + \inf g$. So $U(f + g, P) \leq U(f, P) + U(g, P)$ and $L(f + g, P) \geq L(f, P) + L(g, P)$. Given $\varepsilon$, take a common refinement where both $U - L$ gaps are below $\varepsilon/2$; then the gap for $f + g$ is below $\varepsilon$, and its integral is squeezed between $L(f,P) + L(g,P)$ and $U(f,P) + U(g,P)$, which both lie within $\varepsilon$ of $\int f + \int g$. $\blacksquare$ Proof of monotonicity. $g - f \geq 0$ has every lower sum $\geq 0$, so $\int (g - f) \geq 0$; then use linearity. $\blacksquare$ Proof of the triangle inequality. On any subinterval, for $x, y$ in it, $\bigl\lvert \lvert f(x)\rvert - \lvert f(y)\rvert \bigr\rvert \leq \lvert f(x) - f(y)\rvert$, so the oscillation $\sup\lvert f\rvert - \inf\lvert f\rvert$ is at most $Mk - mk$. Hence $U(\lvert f\rvert, P) - L(\lvert f\rvert, P) \leq U(f, P) - L(f, P)$ and $\lvert f\rvert$ is integrable. Since $-\lvert f\rvert \leq f \leq \lvert f\rvert$, monotonicity gives $-\int\lvert f\rvert \leq \int f \leq \int \lvert f\rvert$. $\blacksquare$ Convention. Define $\intb^a f = -\inta^b f$ and $\inta^a f = 0$. Then the additivity formula holds for any order of $a, b, c$. Mean Value Theorem for integrals. If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, there is $c \in [a, b]$ with $\inta^b f = f(c)(b - a)$. Proof. With $m, M$ the min and max of $f$ (EVT), the bound property gives $m \leq \frac{1}{b-a}\inta^b f \leq M$, and the IVT gives a $c$ where $f$ takes this average value. $\blacksquare$ For example, $\int0^2 x^2\,dx = \frac83$, so $c^2 \cdot 2 = \frac83$, $c = \frac{2}{\sqrt 3}$: the same point as the MVT example for $x^3$ in post 03, and that is no coincidence, because $\frac{x^3}{3}$ is an antiderivative of $x^2$. Chapter 5 — The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Part 1: integrating then differentiating Theorem (FTC, part 1). Let $f$ be integrable on $[a, b]$ and define $$F(x) = \inta^x f(t)\,dt, \qquad x \in [a, b].$$ Then $F$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ (in fact Lipschitz). If $f$ is continuous at $c \in [a, b]$, then $F$ is differentiable at $c$ and $F'(c) = f(c)$ (one-sided at an endpoint). Proof. Let $\lvert f\rvert \leq B$. For $x < y$, additivity and the bound give $\lvert F(y) - F(x)\rvert = \left\lvert \intx^y f\right\rvert \leq B(y - x)$. So $F$ is Lipschitz, hence continuous. Now let $f$ be continuous at $c$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Choose $\delta$ with $\lvert f(t) - f(c)\rvert < \varepsilon$ for $\lvert t - c\rvert < \delta$. For $0 < \lvert x - c\rvert < \delta$, $$\frac{F(x) - F(c)}{x - c} - f(c) = \frac{1}{x - c}\intc^x \bigl(f(t) - f(c)\bigr)\,dt,$$ and the right side has absolute value at most $\frac{1}{\lvert x - c\rvert}\cdot \varepsilon \lvert x - c\rvert = \varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$ So every continuous function has an antiderivative. One famous use: define $\ln x = \int1^x \frac{dt}{t}$ for $x > 0$. Then FTC 1 gives $\ln'(x) = \frac1x$ at once. Worked example. Let $G(x) = \int0^{x^2} \cos(t^2)\,dt$. With $F(u) = \int0^{u} \cos(t^2)\,dt$, FTC 1 gives $F'(u) = \cos(u^2)$, and $G(x) = F(x^2)$. By the chain rule (post 03), $$G'(x) = \cos\bigl((x^2)^2\bigr)\cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^4).$$ Part 2: differentiating then integrating Theorem (FTC, part 2). Let $F$ be differentiable on $[a, b]$ (one-sided at the endpoints) and suppose $f = F'$ is integrable on $[a, b]$. Then $$\inta^b f = F(b) - F(a).$$ Proof. Take any partition $P$. Apply the Mean Value Theorem (post 03) to $F$ on each $[x{k-1}, xk]$: there is $tk$ in the open subinterval with $F(xk) - F(x{k-1}) = F'(tk)\Delta xk = f(tk)\Delta xk$. Summing, the left side telescopes: $$F(b) - F(a) = \sum{k=1}^{n} f(tk)\,\Delta xk.$$ Since $mk \leq f(tk) \leq Mk$, this gives $L(f, P) \leq F(b) - F(a) \leq U(f, P)$ for every $P$. So $F(b) - F(a)$ lies between the lower and upper integrals, which are equal. $\blacksquare$ > Watch out: the hypothesis "$F'$ is integrable" cannot be dropped. $F(x) = x^2\sin(1/x^2)$ with $F(0) = 0$ is differentiable on $[-1, 1]$, but $F'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x^2) - \frac{2}{x}\cos(1/x^2)$ for $x \neq 0$ is unbounded near $0$, so it is not Riemann integrable. A derivative can even be bounded and still not Riemann integrable (Volterra's function, 1881). Integration by parts and substitution Integration by parts. If $u, v$ are differentiable on $[a, b]$ and $u', v'$ are integrable, then $$\inta^b u\,v' = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) - \inta^b u'\,v.$$ This is FTC 2 applied to $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$. Example: $\int0^1 x e^x\,dx = \bigl[x e^x\bigr]0^1 - \int0^1 e^x\,dx = e - (e - 1) = 1$. Substitution. If $\varphi : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuously differentiable and $f$ is continuous on an interval containing $\varphi([a, b])$, then $$\inta^b f(\varphi(t))\,\varphi'(t)\,dt = \int{\varphi(a)}^{\varphi(b)} f(u)\,du.$$ Proof: both sides, as functions of the upper limit $b$, have the same derivative (FTC 1 and the chain rule) and agree when $b = a$. Example: with $u = x^2 + 1$, $\int0^1 2x(x^2 + 1)^3\,dx = \int1^2 u^3\,du = \frac{2^4 - 1^4}{4} = \frac{15}{4}$. Chapter 6 — Improper integrals The Riemann integral needs a bounded function on a closed, bounded interval. An improper integral relaxes one of these by a limit. - Unbounded interval. If $f$ is integrable on $[a, R]$ for every $R > a$, define $\inta^\infty f = \lim{R \to \infty} \inta^R f$ when the limit exists (the integral converges).- Unbounded function. If $f$ is integrable on $[a + \eta, b]$ for every small $\eta > 0$, define $\inta^b f = \lim{\eta \to 0^+} \int{a+\eta}^b f$. The p-test. For $p > 0$: $$\int1^R x^{-p}\,dx = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1 - R^{1-p}}{p - 1} & p \neq 1, \\ \ln R & p = 1. \end{cases}$$ As $R \to \infty$: if $p > 1$ then $R^{1-p} \to 0$ and the integral converges to $\frac{1}{p - 1}$; if $p \leq 1$ it diverges to $\infty$. Near $0$ the roles swap: $\int0^1 x^{-p}\,dx$ converges (to $\frac{1}{1 - p}$) exactly when $p < 1$. | Integral | Converges? | Value ||---|---|---|| $\int1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx$ | yes ($p = 2 > 1$) | $1$ || $\int1^\infty x^{-1}\,dx$ | no ($p = 1$) | diverges || $\int0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx$ | yes ($p = \frac12 < 1$) | $2$ || $\int0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$ | yes | $1$ | This mirrors the $p$-series test of post 01: $\sum 1/k^p$ converges exactly when $p > 1$. The integral test makes the link precise: for $f$ positive and decreasing on $[1, \infty)$, $\sum f(k)$ and $\int1^\infty f$ converge or diverge together. > Common pitfall: $\int{-\infty}^{\infty} f$ is defined as $\int{-\infty}^0 f + \int0^\infty f$ with both limits existing separately. $\lim{R \to \infty}\int{-R}^{R} x\,dx = 0$, but $\int{-\infty}^\infty x\,dx$ diverges. Chapter 7 — Lebesgue's criterion, and why Lebesgue integration exists Measure zero A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ has measure zero if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ it can be covered by countably many open intervals whose total length is less than $\varepsilon$. - Every finite set has measure zero.- Every countable set $\{a1, a2, \dots\}$ has measure zero: cover $ak$ by an interval of length $\varepsilon/2^{k+1}$; the total is at most $\varepsilon/2$. So $\mathbb{Q}$ has measure zero (it is countable, post 00).- An interval $[c, d]$ with $c < d$ does not have measure zero. Theorem (Lebesgue's criterion). A function $f : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable if and only if $f$ is bounded and the set of points where $f$ is discontinuous has measure zero. This explains every example in the post at once: | Function | Discontinuities | Integrable? ||---|---|---|| continuous $f$ | none | yes || monotone $f$ | countable (jumps) | yes || Thomae $T$ | the rationals: countable | yes || Dirichlet $D$ | every point of $[0, 1]$ | no || $x^{-1/2}$ on $(0, 1]$ | — but unbounded | not Riemann; only improper | The proof is in Abbott, Section 7.6, and Rudin, Theorem 11.33. Why a better integral was needed The Riemann integral does not behave well under limits. Enumerate the rationals in $[0, 1]$ as $q1, q2, \dots$ and let $fn$ be $1$ at $q1, \dots, qn$ and $0$ elsewhere. Each $fn$ has finitely many discontinuities, so it is integrable with $\int0^1 fn = 0$. The functions increase and are bounded by $1$, and $fn(x) \to D(x)$ at every $x$. But the limit $D$ is not Riemann integrable, so "$\int fn \to \int \lim fn$" is not even meaningful. Lebesgue's integral (1902) partitions the range of $f$ instead of its domain, and measures the sets where $f$ takes values in each range slice. It integrates $D$ (answer $0$, since $\mathbb{Q}$ has measure zero), agrees with the Riemann integral whenever the latter exists, and satisfies clean limit theorems such as the Monotone and Dominated Convergence Theorems. The earlier posts explained how the engine, the event loop, objects, async code and modules work. This one is about what happens when they misbehave in front of real users: a page that feels sluggish on a mid-range phone, a tab that grows to 2 GB over an afternoon, a Node service whose p99 latency doubles after a deploy, a stack trace that points at . The tools are mature: Core Web Vitals for what users feel, the Performance and Memory panels for why, V8's profilers for Node, source maps, and real-user monitoring for how often. The skill is using them in the right order, measuring before changing anything, and not being fooled by your own benchmark. > Series map: 00 Engines & Runtime → 01 Event Loop & Concurrency → 02 Objects, Prototypes & Metaprogramming → 03 Async Engineering → 04 Modules, Packaging & TypeScript at Scale → 05 Performance, Memory & Production Debugging (this post) (future: see the extensions table at the end). Chapter 1 — Core Web Vitals Terminology - Field data — measurements from real users (RUM, or Chrome's CrUX data set). Core Web Vitals are judged on field data.- Lab data — a synthetic run (Lighthouse, a DevTools recording) on one device and network. Good for debugging, not for judging.- p75 — the 75th percentile. A page "passes" a metric when at least 75% of page views are good, judged separately for mobile and desktop.- LCP — Largest Contentful Paint — when the largest image or text block in the viewport was rendered.- INP — Interaction to Next Paint — roughly the worst latency from a click, tap or key press to the next frame, over the whole page visit. It replaced FID as a Core Web Vital on 12 March 2024.- CLS — Cumulative Layout Shift — the largest burst of unexpected layout shifts. Shifts are grouped into session windows of at most 5 s, with gaps under 1 s. Shifts within 500 ms of user input do not count. Thresholds | Metric | Good | Needs improvement | Poor ||---|---|---|---|| LCP | ≤ 2.5 s | 2.5 s – 4.0 s | > 4.0 s || INP | ≤ 200 ms | 200 ms – 500 ms | > 500 ms || CLS | ≤ 0.1 | 0.1 – 0.25 | > 0.25 | Where the time goes Each metric breaks down into parts, and each part has different fixes: | Metric | Sub-part | Typical fix ||---|---|---|| LCP | Time to first byte (TTFB) | CDN, caching, faster server rendering || LCP | Resource load delay | Make the LCP image discoverable in the HTML; ; no lazy-loading above the fold || LCP | Resource load duration | Right-sized, modern image formats; fewer competing downloads || LCP | Element render delay | Less render-blocking CSS/JS; do not hide content until hydration || INP | Input delay | Other work already running on the main thread: long tasks, hydration, third-party scripts || INP | Processing duration | The event handlers themselves: less work, yield early, defer non-visual work || INP | Presentation delay | Rendering the next frame: DOM size, style and layout cost, forced layouts | > Common pitfall: optimising a Lighthouse score. Lighthouse runs without a user, so it cannot measure INP at all. It reports Total Blocking Time as a lab proxy. A page can score 100 and still fail INP for real users who click during hydration. Measuring in the field The library (maintained by the Chrome team) deals with the edge cases for you: back/forward-cache restores, prerendering, tabs opened in the background, and reporting the final CLS and INP values when the page is hidden. Write your own code only if you have a reason to. Chapter 2 — Reading the Performance panel Record with the CPU throttled (a 4× slowdown is a common starting point) and with a realistic network profile. Profile a production build with source maps. Chapter 3 — Long tasks and yielding The main thread runs one task at a time (Post 01). Chapter 4 — Memory leaks in the browser JavaScript is garbage-collected: an object is freed once nothing reachable from a root (globals, the stack, active handles) references it. A leak is therefore not "forgotten ". It is an unintended reference path from a root. Four paths cause most real leaks: | Leak | Retaining path | Fix ||---|---|---|| Detached DOM | A removed element still referenced from JS: an array, a , a closure, a component instance | Drop references on unmount; hold elements in / if you must || Listeners and subscriptions | //an event bus holds the listener, the listener closes over a component | Remove in cleanup; plus one || Closures | A long-lived callback captures a large scope: the whole response, not the one field it needs | Capture only what you use; null out big locals || Unbounded caches | A module-level keyed by request, user or URL, never evicted | LRU with a size bound, TTLs, for object keys | Timers ( never cleared) and global stores that keep every past page's data are variants of the same idea. Terminology - Shallow size — memory held by the object itself.- Retained size — memory that would be freed if this object were collected: itself plus everything only it keeps alive. Sort by this.- Retainers — the chain of references from a GC root to the object. The fix is almost always to break one link in this chain.- Distance — the number of references from the nearest root. Many objects at an unexpectedly large distance often point to a growing structure. The three-snapshot technique 1. Load the page, do the action once to warm caches, take snapshot 1.2. Repeat the suspect action (open and close the dialog, navigate away and back) several times. Take snapshot 2.3. Repeat the same number of times again. Take snapshot 3.4. In snapshot 3, choose Objects allocated between snapshots 1 and 2. Objects created then and still alive after another round are leak candidates. Anything that belongs to a closed dialog should be gone. Useful filters: type in the class filter to list detached DOM trees, and open Retainers on one of them. The first entry under your own code (not framework internals) is usually the bug. The Allocation instrumentation on timeline profile records allocations over time. Blue bars are allocations still alive at the end, grey bars were collected. Blue bars that keep appearing on every repeat of an action are the leak, and selecting one shows the allocating stack. Allocation sampling has much lower overhead and suits long sessions. > Watch out: in DevTools retains while the console is open, so leaks can appear only with DevTools attached. Clear the console before a snapshot.
