Chapter 1 — Fields and vector spaces

Terminology

- Field — a set $F$ with an addition and a multiplication that satisfy the usual rules of arithmetic: both operations are associative and commutative, multiplication distributes over addition, there are elements $0 \neq 1$ with $a + 0 = a$ and $1a = a$, every $a$ has a negative $-a$, and every nonzero $a$ has an inverse $a^{-1}$. Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{F}2 = \{0, 1\}$ with arithmetic mod 2. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are not a field ($2$ has no integer inverse).- Scalars — the elements of the field. In this series $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ unless stated.

Everything in Linear Algebra 00 and 01 — elimination, pivots, inverses, LU — used only these field rules, so it works word for word over any field.

The definition

A vector space over $F$ is a set $V$ with an addition $V \times V \to V$ and a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ such that, for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and $a, b \in F$:

1. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$ (commutativity).2. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$ (associativity).3. There is an element $\mathbf{0} \in V$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$ for all $\mathbf{v}$ (additive identity).4. For each $\mathbf{v}$ there is $\mathbf{w}$ with $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ (additive inverse).5. $a(b\mathbf{v}) = (ab)\mathbf{v}$.6. $1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$.7. $a(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = a\mathbf{u} + a\mathbf{v}$.8. $(a + b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{v}$.

The elements of $V$ are called vectors, whatever they actually are.

First consequences

Proposition. In any vector space: (a) the zero vector is unique; (b) each $\mathbf{v}$ has exactly one additive inverse, written $-\mathbf{v}$; (c) $0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$; (d) $a\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$; (e) $(-1)\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{v}$.

Proof. (a) If $\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{0}'$ are both identities, $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}' = \mathbf{0}'$. (b) If $\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{w}'$ are both inverses of $\mathbf{v}$, then $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}') = (\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w}' = \mathbf{w}'$. (c) $0\mathbf{v} = (0 + 0)\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} + 0\mathbf{v}$; add $-(0\mathbf{v})$ to both sides. (d) The same with $a\mathbf{0} = a(\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0})$. (e) $\mathbf{v} + (-1)\mathbf{v} = (1 + (-1))\mathbf{v} = 0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, so $(-1)\mathbf{v}$ is the inverse of $\mathbf{v}$, by (b). $\blacksquare$

Examples

| Space | Vectors | Operations ||---|---|---|| $F^n$ | columns $(x1, \dots, xn)$ | entry by entry || $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ | polynomials of degree at most $n$ | add coefficients; scale coefficients || $\mathcal{P}(F)$ | all polynomials | same || $F^S$ | all functions $f : S \to F$ on a set $S$ | $(f + g)(s) = f(s) + g(s)$, $(af)(s) = a f(s)$ || $C[a, b]$ | continuous real functions on $[a, b]$ | pointwise || $M{m \times n}(F)$ | $m \times n$ matrices | entry by entry |

$F^n$ is really the special case $F^S$ with $S = \{1, \dots, n\}$: a column is a function from positions to numbers.

Non-examples. The first quadrant $\{(x, y) : x, y \ge 0\}$ fails axiom 4 — $(1, 0)$ has no inverse inside it. The line $\{(x, y) : y = x + 1\}$ has no zero vector. The polynomials of degree exactly 2 are not closed under addition: $(t^2 + t) + (-t^2) = t$.

Chapter 2 — Subspaces

Terminology

- Subspace — a subset $U \subseteq V$ that is itself a vector space with the same addition and scalar multiplication.

Theorem (subspace test). A subset $U$ of a vector space $V$ is a subspace if and only if

1. $\mathbf{0} \in U$;2. $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in U \Rightarrow \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} \in U$ (closed under addition);3. $a \in F$, $\mathbf{u} \in U \Rightarrow a\mathbf{u} \in U$ (closed under scalar multiplication).

Proof. A subspace clearly satisfies 2 and 3, and it contains its own zero vector $\mathbf{z}$ with $\mathbf{z} + \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}$; adding $-\mathbf{z}$ in $V$ shows $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, so 1 holds. Conversely, suppose 1–3 hold. Conditions 2 and 3 make addition and scalar multiplication land in $U$. Axioms 1, 2, 5–8 are identities that hold for all vectors of $V$, so in particular for those of $U$. Axiom 3 holds by condition 1. For axiom 4: if $\mathbf{u} \in U$ then $-\mathbf{u} = (-1)\mathbf{u} \in U$ by condition 3 and part (e) of the proposition. $\blacksquare$

> Trick: conditions 2 and 3 together are the same as "closed under linear combinations": $a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w} \in U$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in U$ and scalars $a, b$. Check $\mathbf{0}$ first — it is the fastest way to reject a non-subspace.

Examples and non-examples

- Null space $N(A) = \{\mathbf{x} \in F^n : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\}$ is a subspace of $F^n$: $A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, and $A(a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w}) = aA\mathbf{u} + bA\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$.- Column space $C(A) = \{A\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in F^n\}$, the span of the columns, is a subspace of $F^m$.- $\{p \in \mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R}) : p(1) = 0\}$ is a subspace; $\{p : p(1) = 5\}$ is not (no zero polynomial).- The symmetric $n \times n$ matrices form a subspace of $M{n \times n}$.- The solutions $y$ of $y'' + y = 0$ form a subspace of the twice-differentiable functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.- The solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ are not a subspace (Linear Algebra 00, Chapter 7): $\mathbf{0}$ is not a solution.- The union of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a subspace: $(1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1)$ is in neither.

Intersections are subspaces. If $U$ and $W$ are subspaces, so is $U \cap W$: it contains $\mathbf{0}$, and a linear combination of vectors in both lies in both. Unions usually are not, as the axes show; Chapter 8 replaces the union by the sum $U + W$.

Chapter 3 — Span and linear independence in general

The definitions from Linear Algebra 00 carry over unchanged. For a finite list $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k$ in $V$:

- $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k)$ is the set of all linear combinations $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{v}k$. By convention the span of the empty list is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$.- The list spans $V$ if its span is all of $V$.- The list is linearly independent if $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{v}k = \mathbf{0}$ forces $a1 = \dots = ak = 0$; otherwise it is dependent. The empty list is independent.

Proposition. $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k)$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ containing every $\mathbf{v}i$.

Proof. It contains $\mathbf{0}$, and a combination of combinations is a combination, so it is a subspace; it contains each $\mathbf{v}i$. Any subspace containing all $\mathbf{v}i$ is closed under linear combinations, so it contains the span. $\blacksquare$

The linear dependence lemma

Lemma. If $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}m$ is linearly dependent, then there is an index $j$ such that

1. $\mathbf{v}j \in \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}{j-1})$, and2. removing $\mathbf{v}j$ from the list does not change its span.

Proof. Take a relation $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + am\mathbf{v}m = \mathbf{0}$ with not all $ai = 0$, and let $j$ be the largest index with $aj \neq 0$. Then

$$\mathbf{v}j = -\frac{a1}{aj}\mathbf{v}1 - \dots - \frac{a{j-1}}{aj}\mathbf{v}{j-1},$$

which proves 1. (If $j = 1$ this says $\mathbf{v}1 = \mathbf{0}$, the span of the empty list.) For 2, in any combination of the full list, replace $\mathbf{v}j$ by the right-hand side above; the result is a combination of the list without $\mathbf{v}j$. $\blacksquare$

Independence outside Fⁿ

- In $\mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R})$, the list $1, t, t^2$ is independent: if $a + bt + ct^2$ is the zero polynomial, all its coefficients are 0.- In the functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $\sin$ and $\cos$ are independent: if $a\sin x + b\cos x = 0$ for all $x$, then $x = 0$ gives $b = 0$ and $x = \pi/2$ gives $a = 0$.

> Common pitfall: "$a\sin x + b\cos x = 0$" means equality as functions, for every $x$. Finding one $x$ where a combination is zero proves nothing; you must show the combination is zero everywhere only when all weights are zero.

Chapter 4 — Basis, the exchange lemma and dimension

Terminology

- Basis of $V$ — a list of vectors in $V$ that is linearly independent and spans $V$.- Finite-dimensional — $V$ is spanned by some finite list. Otherwise infinite-dimensional.- Standard basis of $F^n$ — $\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n$. Of $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ — $1, t, \dots, t^n$.

Theorem (unique representation). A list $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}n$ is a basis of $V$ if and only if every $\mathbf{v} \in V$ can be written as $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + an\mathbf{v}n$ in exactly one way.

Proof. Spanning is "at least one way". If a basis gave two ways, subtracting them gives $\sum (ai - bi)\mathbf{v}i = \mathbf{0}$, so every $ai = bi$ by independence. Conversely, uniqueness for $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (which is $0\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + 0\mathbf{v}n$) is exactly independence. $\blacksquare$

The Steinitz exchange lemma

Lemma (Steinitz exchange). Let $V$ be a vector space. Suppose $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}m$ is linearly independent in $V$ and $\mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$. Then $m \le n$. Moreover, after renumbering the $\mathbf{w}$'s, the list

$$\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}m, \mathbf{w}{m+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$$

also spans $V$.

Proof. We show by induction on $k = 0, 1, \dots, m$ that $k \le n$ and that, after renumbering, $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k, \mathbf{w}{k+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$. For $k = 0$ this is the hypothesis.

Assume it holds for some $k < m$. Since the list $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k, \mathbf{w}{k+1}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$ spans $V$, we can write

$$\mathbf{u}{k+1} = a1\mathbf{u}1 + \dots + ak\mathbf{u}k + b{k+1}\mathbf{w}{k+1} + \dots + bn\mathbf{w}n.$$

If every $bj$ were 0 (in particular, if no $\mathbf{w}$'s were left, $k = n$), then $\mathbf{u}{k+1}$ would be a combination of $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}k$, contradicting independence of the $\mathbf{u}$'s. So some $bj \neq 0$; hence $k + 1 \le n$. Renumber so that $b{k+1} \neq 0$ and solve for $\mathbf{w}{k+1}$:

$$\mathbf{w}{k+1} = \frac{1}{b{k+1}}\Bigl(\mathbf{u}{k+1} - \sum{i=1}^{k} ai\mathbf{u}i - \sum{j=k+2}^{n} bj\mathbf{w}j\Bigr).$$

So $\mathbf{w}{k+1}$ lies in the span of $\mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}{k+1}, \mathbf{w}{k+2}, \dots, \mathbf{w}n$. That span therefore contains the whole old spanning list, so it is $V$. This completes the step. At $k = m$ we get $m \le n$ and the stated spanning list. $\blacksquare$

Small example. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ take the independent $\mathbf{u}1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $\mathbf{u}2 = (0, 1, 1)$ and the spanning $\mathbf{e}1, \mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3$. Since $\mathbf{u}1 = \mathbf{e}1 + \mathbf{e}2$ has a nonzero coefficient on $\mathbf{e}1$, swap it out: $\mathbf{u}1, \mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3$ spans. Then $\mathbf{u}2 = 0\,\mathbf{u}1 + \mathbf{e}2 + \mathbf{e}3$; swap out $\mathbf{e}2$: $\mathbf{u}1, \mathbf{u}2, \mathbf{e}3$ spans $\mathbb{R}^3$. (Three vectors that span $\mathbb{R}^3$ are automatically a basis — see the theorem at the end of this chapter.)

Dimension is well defined

Theorem. In a finite-dimensional vector space, any two bases have the same length.

Proof. Let $B1$ and $B2$ be bases, of lengths $m$ and $n$. $B1$ is independent and $B2$ spans, so $m \le n$ by Steinitz. Swap roles: $n \le m$. $\blacksquare$

This common length is the dimension $\dim V$.

| Space | Dimension | A basis ||---|---|---|| $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ | 0 | the empty list || $F^n$ | $n$ | $\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n$ || $\mathcal{P}n(F)$ | $n + 1$ | $1, t, \dots, t^n$ || $M{m \times n}(F)$ | $mn$ | matrices with one entry 1, the rest 0 || symmetric $2 \times 2$ | 3 | one basis matrix each for the (1,1), (2,2) and paired off-diagonal entries || $\mathcal{P}(F)$ | infinite | $1, t, t^2, \dots$ (no finite list spans) |

> Watch out: $\mathbb{C}^n$ has dimension $n$ over $\mathbb{C}$ but $2n$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (a basis over $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathbf{e}1, i\mathbf{e}1, \dots, \mathbf{e}n, i\mathbf{e}n$). Dimension depends on the field of scalars.

Growing and shrinking to a basis

Theorem. Let $V$ be finite-dimensional with $\dim V = n$.

1. Every spanning list can be shrunk to a basis by removing vectors.2. Every independent list can be extended to a basis by adding vectors.3. Any $n$ independent vectors form a basis; any $n$ spanning vectors form a basis.4. Every subspace $U$ is finite-dimensional with $\dim U \le n$, and $\dim U = n$ only if $U = V$.

Proof. (1) While the list is dependent, the dependence lemma lets you remove one vector without changing the span. The process stops at an independent spanning list. (2) Apply Steinitz with a basis as the spanning list: the independent list plus the remaining $n - m$ basis vectors spans $V$; it has length $n$, and by (1) some sublist is a basis, which must have length $n$, so it is the whole list. (3) An independent list of length $n$ extends to a basis of length $n$, so nothing is added; a spanning list of length $n$ shrinks to a basis of length $n$, so nothing is removed. (4) Any independent list in $U$ is independent in $V$, so it has length at most $n$ by Steinitz. Keep adding vectors of $U$ not yet in the span of an independent list (independence is kept, by Exercise 7); this stops after at most $n$ steps with a basis of $U$. If $\dim U = n$, that basis is $n$ independent vectors of $V$, so by (3) it spans $V$, hence $U = V$. $\blacksquare$

Chapter 5 — Coordinates

If $B = (\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}n)$ is a basis of $V$, each $\mathbf{v}$ has unique weights with $\mathbf{v} = a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + an\mathbf{v}n$. The column $[\mathbf{v}]B = (a1, \dots, an) \in F^n$ is the coordinate vector of $\mathbf{v}$ relative to $B$.

The map $\mathbf{v} \mapsto [\mathbf{v}]B$ is one-to-one, onto $F^n$, and respects linear combinations: $[a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{w}]B = a[\mathbf{u}]B + b[\mathbf{w}]B$. So every $n$-dimensional space over $F$ behaves exactly like $F^n$, and computations in it can be done with columns and matrices. Linear Algebra 03 makes this precise (isomorphism) and studies how coordinates change when the basis changes.

Example in $\mathbb{R}^2$. With $B = ((1, 1), (1, -1))$ and $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1)$: solve $a(1,1) + b(1,-1) = (3, 1)$, so $a + b = 3$, $a - b = 1$, giving $a = 2$, $b = 1$. So $[\mathbf{x}]B = (2, 1)$, while the standard coordinates are $(3, 1)$ — same vector, different description.

Example in $\mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R})$. With $B = (1,\ 1 + t,\ (1 + t)^2)$, find $[t^2]B$. Put $s = 1 + t$; then $t = s - 1$ and $t^2 = s^2 - 2s + 1$. So

$$t^2 = 1 \cdot (1 + t)^2 - 2(1 + t) + 1 \cdot 1, \qquad [t^2]B = (1, -2, 1)$$

(the weights on $1$, $1 + t$ and $(1 + t)^2$, in that order). Check: $(1 + 2t + t^2) - 2 - 2t + 1 = t^2$.

Chapter 6 — The four fundamental subspaces

Terminology

For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ of rank $r$ (the number of pivots):

| Subspace | Symbol | Lives in | Definition ||---|---|---|---|| Column space | $C(A)$ | $F^m$ | all $A\mathbf{x}$; span of the columns || Null space | $N(A)$ | $F^n$ | all $\mathbf{x}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ || Row space | $C(A^T)$ | $F^n$ | span of the rows || Left null space | $N(A^T)$ | $F^m$ | all $\mathbf{y}$ with $A^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$, that is $\mathbf{y}^TA = \mathbf{0}^T$ |

Two facts about row operations

Let $EA = R$ with $E$ invertible (a product of elementary matrices) and $R$ the RREF.

Fact 1 — the row space does not change. Every row of $R$ is a combination of rows of $A$, and since $A = E^{-1}R$, every row of $A$ is a combination of rows of $R$. So $C(A^T) = C(R^T)$.

Fact 2 — linear relations among columns do not change. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff EA\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \iff R\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, because $E$ is invertible. So a combination of columns of $A$ is zero exactly when the same combination of columns of $R$ is zero, and $N(A) = N(R)$. (The column spaces of $A$ and $R$ usually differ.)

Bases from the RREF

- $C(A)$ — the pivot columns of $A$ (not of $R$). In $R$ the pivot columns are distinct standard basis vectors, so they are independent, and every other column is a combination of them. By Fact 2 the same holds for the corresponding columns of $A$.- $C(A^T)$ — the nonzero rows of $R$. They span the row space by Fact 1, and they are independent because each has a leading 1 in a column where the other rows have 0.- $N(A)$ — the $n - r$ special solutions (one free variable 1, the others 0), as in Linear Algebra 00.- $N(A^T)$ — the last $m - r$ rows of $E$: those rows multiply $A$ to give the zero rows of $R$.

Worked example

Use the matrix from Linear Algebra 00, Chapter 6:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadR = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad m = 3,\ n = 4,\ r = 2.$$

Pivot columns 1 and 3; free variables $x2, x4$.

- Column space (dimension $r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^3$): basis $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(2, 6, 8)$ — columns 1 and 3 of $A$. The other columns satisfy the same relations as in $R$: column 2 $= 2 \times$ column 1, and column 4 $= -2 \times$ column 1 $+ 2 \times$ column 3. Check: $-2(1,2,3) + 2(2,6,8) = (2, 8, 10)$.- Row space (dimension $r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^4$): basis $(1, 2, 0, -2)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$.- Null space (dimension $n - r = 2$, in $\mathbb{R}^4$): basis $\mathbf{s}1 = (-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $\mathbf{s}2 = (2, 0, -2, 1)$.- Left null space (dimension $m - r = 1$, in $\mathbb{R}^3$): the elimination steps $R2 - 2R1$, $R3 - 3R1$, $R3 - R2$ make row 3 into $-(\text{row } 1) - (\text{row } 2) + (\text{row } 3) = \mathbf{0}$. So $\mathbf{y} = (-1, -1, 1)$ is a basis: $\mathbf{y}^TA = \mathbf{0}^T$.

Orthogonality — a preview

Each row of $A$ dotted with a null-space vector gives 0 — that is what $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ says, one row at a time. So every vector of $N(A)$ is orthogonal to every vector of $C(A^T)$. In the example, $(1, 2, 0, -2)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$, $(1, 2, 0, -2)\cdot(2, 0, -2, 1) = 0$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(2, 0, -2, 1) = 0$. In the same way $N(A^T)$ is orthogonal to $C(A)$: $(-1, -1, 1)\cdot(1, 2, 3) = 0$ and $(-1, -1, 1)\cdot(2, 6, 8) = 0$. Over $\mathbb{R}$ the dimensions add up ($2 + 2 = 4$, $2 + 1 = 3$), which makes each pair orthogonal complements — proved in Linear Algebra 05.

Row rank equals column rank

Theorem. For every matrix, $\dim C(A) = \dim C(A^T) = r$, the number of pivots.

Proof. The pivot columns of $A$ are a basis of $C(A)$, and there are $r$ of them. The nonzero rows of $R$ are a basis of $C(A^T)$, and there are $r$ of them (one per pivot). $\blacksquare$

This is not obvious: for a $3 \times 100$ matrix it says that the 100 columns span a space of the same dimension as the 3 rows. It also shows $\operatorname{rank} A = \operatorname{rank} A^T$, and that $r \le \min(m, n)$.

Rank-one matrices. A matrix has rank 1 exactly when it is a nonzero column times a nonzero row, $A = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^T$: every column is a multiple of $\mathbf{u}$, every row a multiple of $\mathbf{v}^T$. The outer-product view of Linear Algebra 01 writes any $AB$ as a sum of such pieces.

Chapter 7 — Rank–nullity (matrix form)

Theorem (rank–nullity). For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$,

$$\dim C(A) + \dim N(A) = n.$$

Proof. Let $r$ be the number of pivots. $\dim C(A) = r$ by Chapter 6. It remains to show that the $n - r$ special solutions $\mathbf{s}1, \dots, \mathbf{s}{n-r}$ are a basis of $N(A)$.

Independent: look only at the free-variable entries. In $\mathbf{s}k$ the $k$-th free variable is 1 and the others are 0. So in $\sum ck\mathbf{s}k$ the $k$-th free entry is $ck$; if the sum is $\mathbf{0}$, every $ck = 0$.

Spanning: let $\mathbf{x} \in N(A)$ with free-variable values $f1, \dots, f{n-r}$. Then $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x} - \sum fk\mathbf{s}k$ is in $N(A)$ and has every free variable equal to 0. The RREF expresses each basic variable in terms of the free ones, so all basic variables of $\mathbf{z}$ are 0 too; thus $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \sum fk\mathbf{s}k$.

So $\dim N(A) = n - r$, and $r + (n - r) = n$. $\blacksquare$

In words: each of the $n$ columns either contributes a pivot (a new direction in the column space) or a free variable (a new direction in the null space). For the example, $2 + 2 = 4$. Applied to $A^T$ (which has $m$ columns and the same rank), it gives $\dim N(A^T) = m - r$, here $3 - 2 = 1$.

The general version — for a linear map $T : V \to W$, $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{im} T + \dim \ker T$ — is proved in Linear Algebra 03.

Consequences

| Condition on the rank | Meaning for $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ ||---|---|| $r = m$ (full row rank) | solvable for every $\mathbf{b}$; $C(A) = F^m$ || $r = n$ (full column rank) | at most one solution; $N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ || $r = m = n$ | exactly one solution; $A$ invertible || $r < m$ and $r < n$ | either no solution or infinitely many |

For a square matrix, "rank $n$", "$N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$" and "$C(A) = F^n$" join the invertible matrix theorem from Linear Algebra 01.

> Trick: rank–nullity is a quick sanity check. A $5 \times 7$ matrix has rank at most 5, so its null space has dimension at least $7 - 5 = 2$ — there are nonzero solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ before any computation.

Chapter 8 — Sums and direct sums

Terminology

- Sum of subspaces $U, W \subseteq V$: $U + W = \{\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} : \mathbf{u} \in U, \mathbf{w} \in W\}$. It is the smallest subspace containing both (a subspace containing $U$ and $W$ contains all such sums).- Direct sum — the sum is direct, written $U \oplus W$, if every vector of $U + W$ can be written as $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}$ in only one way.

Proposition. $U + W$ is direct if and only if $U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

Proof. Suppose the sum is direct and $\mathbf{v} \in U \cap W$. Then $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v})$ with $\mathbf{v} \in U$, $-\mathbf{v} \in W$, and also $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}$; uniqueness gives $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. Conversely, if $U \cap W = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u}' + \mathbf{w}'$, then $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}' = \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w}$ lies in both $U$ and $W$, so it is $\mathbf{0}$. $\blacksquare$

> Watch out: for three or more subspaces, pairwise zero intersections are not enough. Three distinct lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ meet pairwise only at $\mathbf{0}$, but their sum is not direct (three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are dependent).

The dimension formula

Theorem. If $U$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional subspaces of $V$, then

$$\dim(U + W) = \dim U + \dim W - \dim(U \cap W).$$

Proof. Let $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k$ be a basis of $U \cap W$. Extend it to a basis $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}p$ of $U$ and to a basis $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}q$ of $W$ (Chapter 4). The combined list $\mathbf{z}1, \dots, \mathbf{z}k, \mathbf{u}1, \dots, \mathbf{u}p, \mathbf{w}1, \dots, \mathbf{w}q$ clearly spans $U + W$. For independence, suppose

$$\sum ai\mathbf{z}i + \sum bj\mathbf{u}j + \sum cl\mathbf{w}l = \mathbf{0}.$$

Then $\sum cl\mathbf{w}l = -\sum ai\mathbf{z}i - \sum bj\mathbf{u}j$ lies in $W$ and in $U$, hence in $U \cap W$, so $\sum cl\mathbf{w}l = \sum di\mathbf{z}i$ for some $di$. The $\mathbf{z}$'s and $\mathbf{w}$'s together are a basis of $W$, so all $cl = 0$ (and all $di = 0$). Now $\sum ai\mathbf{z}i + \sum bj\mathbf{u}j = \mathbf{0}$ is a relation in a basis of $U$, so all $ai, bj = 0$. Hence the combined list is a basis, and

$$\dim(U + W) = k + p + q = (k + p) + (k + q) - k. \qquad \blacksquare$$

This is the same shape as $\lvert A \cup B\rvert = \lvert A\rvert + \lvert B\rvert - \lvert A \cap B\rvert$ for finite sets.

Examples

- In $\mathbb{R}^3$, let $U = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}1, \mathbf{e}2)$ (the $xy$-plane) and $W = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}2, \mathbf{e}3)$ (the $yz$-plane). Then $U \cap W = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{e}2)$ and $\dim(U + W) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$, so $U + W = \mathbb{R}^3$, but the sum is not direct.- The plane $P = \{\mathbf{x} : x1 + x2 + x3 = 0\}$ and the line $L = \operatorname{span}(1, 1, 1)$ satisfy $P \cap L = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (a point $(c, c, c)$ with $3c = 0$), so $\dim(P + L) = 2 + 1 = 3$ and $\mathbb{R}^3 = P \oplus L$. For example $(1, 2, 3) = (-1, 0, 1) + (2, 2, 2)$.- Every square matrix splits uniquely into symmetric plus skew-symmetric parts, $M = \tfrac12(M + M^T) + \tfrac12(M - M^T)$, so over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ (any field where $2 \neq 0$), $M{n \times n} = \text{Sym} \oplus \text{Skew}$. For $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ the parts are $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.- For a real $m \times n$ matrix, $\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A)$. The intersection is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, because a vector in both is orthogonal to itself, and $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x} = 0$ forces $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$; the dimensions add to $r + (n - r) = n$. So every $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ splits uniquely as $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}r + \mathbf{x}n$, a row-space part plus a null-space part — the left half of the four-subspace picture. Since $A\mathbf{x}n = \mathbf{0}$, $A\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{x}r$: every $\mathbf{b}$ in $C(A)$ is $A\mathbf{x}r$ for a row-space vector $\mathbf{x}r$, which is the arrow from row space to column space in the figure of Chapter 6.

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1 — A polynomial subspace

Show that $U = \{p \in \mathcal{P}2(\mathbb{R}) : p(1) = 0\}$ is a subspace, and find a basis and its dimension.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The zero polynomial has $p(1) = 0$, and $(ap + bq)(1) = ap(1) + bq(1) = 0$, so $U$ is a subspace. $p(t) = c0 + c1t + c2t^2$ is in $U$ iff $c0 + c1 + c2 = 0$, i.e. $c0 = -c1 - c2$, so $p = c1(t - 1) + c2(t^2 - 1)$. The list $t - 1$, $t^2 - 1$ spans $U$ and is independent (only the second has a $t^2$ term), so $\dim U = 2$. This matches rank–nullity: $U$ is the null space of the map $p \mapsto p(1)$, and $3 - 1 = 2$.

</details>

Exercise 2 — Not a subspace

Is $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : xy \ge 0\}$ a subspace?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

No. It contains $\mathbf{0}$ and is closed under scalar multiplication ($(cx)(cy) = c^2xy \ge 0$), but not under addition: $(1, 0) + (0, -1) = (1, -1)$ has $xy = -1 < 0$. It is the union of the first and third quadrants.

</details>

Exercise 3 — Shrink to a basis

Find a basis for $\operatorname{span}\{(1, 2, 1), (2, 4, 2), (1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 0)\}$ taken from the given vectors, and its dimension.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Put them as columns and row reduce: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Pivot columns 1 and 3, so $(1, 2, 1)$ and $(1, 0, 1)$ are a basis and the dimension is 2. (All four vectors satisfy $x1 = x3$, a plane.)

</details>

Exercise 4 — Four subspaces of a rank-one matrix

For $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, give bases and dimensions of all four subspaces.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$m = 3$, $n = 2$, $r = 1$ ($B = (1, 2, 3)^T(1, 2)$). $C(B)$: $(1, 2, 3)$, dimension 1. $C(B^T)$: $(1, 2)$, dimension 1. $N(B)$: $(-2, 1)$, dimension $n - r = 1$. $N(B^T)$: solutions of $y1 + 2y2 + 3y3 = 0$, basis $(-2, 1, 0)$, $(-3, 0, 1)$, dimension $m - r = 2$. Check orthogonality: $(1, 2)\cdot(-2, 1) = 0$.

</details>

Exercise 5 — Coordinates

Find the coordinates of $p(t) = 2 + 3t + t^2$ relative to $B = (1,\ 1 + t,\ (1 + t)^2)$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$(1 + t)^2 = 1 + 2t + t^2$, so $p - (1 + t)^2 = 1 + t$. Hence $p = 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (1 + t) + 1 \cdot (1 + t)^2$, and $[p]B = (0, 1, 1)$. Check: $(1 + t) + (1 + 2t + t^2) = 2 + 3t + t^2$.

</details>

Exercise 6 — Forced intersection

Let $U$ and $W$ be 3-dimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^5$. Prove that $U \cap W \neq \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$U + W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^5$, so $\dim(U + W) \le 5$. The dimension formula gives $\dim(U \cap W) = 3 + 3 - \dim(U + W) \ge 6 - 5 = 1$.

</details>

Exercise 7 — Proof practice

Prove that if $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}m$ is independent and $\mathbf{v} \notin \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}m)$, then $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}m, \mathbf{v}$ is independent.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Suppose $a1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + am\mathbf{v}m + a\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. If $a \neq 0$, then $\mathbf{v} = -\frac1a\sum ai\mathbf{v}i$ is in the span — a contradiction. So $a = 0$, and then all $ai = 0$ by independence of the $\mathbf{v}i$. (This is the step used in Chapter 4, part 4.)

</details>

Chapter 4 — Document-level locking + MVCC

WiredTiger uses multi-version concurrency control (MVCC):

- Readers see a consistent snapshot at their start time.- Writers create new versions; old versions linger until no reader needs them.- Locking is at the document level — different documents in the same collection don't block each other.

Practical implications:

- High concurrency on different documents = fine.- High concurrency on the SAME document = serialised (hot-key problem, next).- Long readers force old versions to linger → memory pressure.

No collection-level lock during ordinary CRUD. DDL (createIndex, drop, collMod) still locks the collection briefly.

This post treats matrices as objects with their own algebra: they can be added, multiplied, transposed and — sometimes — inverted. Matrix multiplication is the operation that makes all of this interesting, so it gets four separate readings, each useful in a different situation. Then row operations become matrices themselves (elementary matrices), which turns Gaussian elimination into an algebraic statement: $A = LU$, a lower triangular matrix times an upper triangular one, or $PA = LU$ when rows must be exchanged. This factorization is how real software solves $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, and it explains a rule every numerical programmer learns early: do not compute $A^{-1}$ to solve a linear system.

Chapter 1 — Matrix operations

Terminology

- $m \times n$ matrix — a rectangular array of numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. $a{ij}$ is the entry in row $i$, column $j$.- Square matrix — $m = n$. Its main diagonal is $a{11}, a{22}, \dots, a{nn}$.- Identity matrix $In$ — square, 1 on the main diagonal, 0 elsewhere. $In\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}$ for all $\mathbf{x}$.- Zero matrix $0$ — all entries 0.- Equal matrices — same size and the same entry in every position.

Addition and scalar multiplication work entry by entry, exactly as for vectors, and obey the same eight rules. Only matrices of the same size can be added.

The matrix product

If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, the product $AB$ is the $m \times p$ matrix with entries

$$(AB){ij} = \sum{k=1}^{n} a{ik} b{kj} = a{i1}b{1j} + a{i2}b{2j} + \dots + a{in}b{nj}.$$

The inner sizes must match: $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$. The example used throughout this chapter:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadB = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadAB = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Four ways to read AB

1. Entry by entry (row times column). $(AB){ij}$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. For example $(AB){12} = 1\cdot 6 + 2 \cdot 8 = 22$.

2. By columns. Column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $B$:

$$AB = A\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{b}1 & \cdots & \mathbf{b}p \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A\mathbf{b}1 & \cdots & A\mathbf{b}p \end{pmatrix}.$$

So every column of $AB$ is a combination of the columns of $A$. Here $A\mathbf{b}1 = 5(1,3) + 7(2,4) = (19, 43)$.

3. By rows. Row $i$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ times $B$, so every row of $AB$ is a combination of the rows of $B$. Here row 1 is $1\cdot(5,6) + 2\cdot(7,8) = (19, 22)$. This is the view that explains elimination: row operations combine rows.

4. Columns times rows (sum of outer products). Multiply column $k$ of $A$ (an $m \times 1$ matrix) by row $k$ of $B$ (a $1 \times p$ matrix). Each such product is a full $m \times p$ matrix of rank at most one (Linear Algebra 02), and $AB$ is their sum:

$$AB = \sum{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{a}k \mathbf{b}^{}k= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 15 & 18 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 16 \\ 28 & 32 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Here $\mathbf{b}^{}k$ denotes row $k$ of $B$. This view reappears in the SVD (Linear Algebra 05), which writes a matrix as a sum of rank-one pieces ordered by importance.

All four give the same numbers because each one computes $\sumk a{ik}b{kj}$ in position $(i,j)$; they only group the work differently.

> Trick: pick the view that matches the question. "Is $\mathbf{b}$ in the column space?" — columns. "What does a row operation do?" — rows. "What is the rank of $AB$?" — outer products. "Just give me a number" — entries.

Algebraic rules — and what fails

For matrices of compatible sizes:

- $A(BC) = (AB)C$ (associative), $A(B + C) = AB + AC$ and $(A + B)C = AC + BC$ (distributive), $c(AB) = (cA)B = A(cB)$, and $ImA = A = AIn$.

Proof of associativity. For a vector $\mathbf{x}$, $B\mathbf{x} = \sumk xk\mathbf{b}k$, so $A(B\mathbf{x}) = \sumk xk A\mathbf{b}k = (AB)\mathbf{x}$ by the column view. Apply this to each column $\mathbf{c}j$ of $C$: column $j$ of $A(BC)$ is $A(B\mathbf{c}j) = (AB)\mathbf{c}j$, which is column $j$ of $(AB)C$. $\blacksquare$

Three rules from ordinary algebra fail:

1. Not commutative. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{pmatrix} \neq AB$. (Often only one of $AB$, $BA$ is even defined.)2. Zero divisors. With $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $N \neq 0$ but $N^2 = 0$.3. No cancellation. $AB = AC$ does not imply $B = C$, even when $A \neq 0$ (take $A = N$, $B = N$, $C = 0$).

> Common pitfall: $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$, not $A^2 + 2AB + B^2$. The two agree only when $AB = BA$.

Chapter 2 — Transpose and special matrices

Transpose

The transpose $A^T$ of an $m \times n$ matrix is the $n \times m$ matrix with $(A^T){ij} = a{ji}$: rows become columns. Rules:

$$(A^T)^T = A, \qquad (A + B)^T = A^T + B^T, \qquad (cA)^T = cA^T, \qquad (AB)^T = B^TA^T.$$

Proof of the last rule. $((AB)^T){ij} = (AB){ji} = \sumk a{jk}b{ki} = \sumk (B^T){ik}(A^T){kj} = (B^TA^T){ij}$. $\blacksquare$

The order reverses, just like taking off shoes and socks. The dot product is a matrix product: $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}$ (a $1 \times 1$ matrix).

Special matrices

| Type | Definition | Useful facts ||---|---|---|| Diagonal | $a{ij} = 0$ for $i \neq j$ | products and powers act entry by entry on the diagonal || Upper triangular | $a{ij} = 0$ for $i > j$ | product of two upper triangular is upper triangular || Lower triangular | $a{ij} = 0$ for $i < j$ | same for lower; unit lower triangular has 1s on the diagonal || Symmetric | $A^T = A$ | $A^TA$ and $AA^T$ are always symmetric || Permutation | the rows of $I$ in some order | $P\!A$ reorders the rows of $A$; $P^{-1} = P^T$ |

$A^TA$ is symmetric: $(A^TA)^T = A^T(A^T)^T = A^TA$. For example, with $X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, $X^TX = \begin{pmatrix} 35 & 44 \\ 44 & 56 \end{pmatrix}$. This matrix is the heart of least squares (Linear Algebra 05).

Product of lower triangular matrices is lower triangular. If $a{ik} = 0$ for $k > i$ and $b{kj} = 0$ for $j > k$, then in $(AB){ij} = \sumk a{ik}b{kj}$ a term can be nonzero only if $j \le k \le i$. When $i < j$ there is no such $k$, so $(AB){ij} = 0$. If both have unit diagonal, $(AB){ii} = a{ii}b{ii} = 1$. $\blacksquare$

Permutation matrices. Each row and each column of $P$ has exactly one 1. The rows of $P$ are orthonormal, so $PP^T = I$, which means $P^{-1} = P^T$.

Chapter 3 — Block matrices

A matrix can be cut by horizontal and vertical lines into blocks (submatrices). If the column cuts of $A$ match the row cuts of $B$, then $AB$ can be computed block by block, using the same formula as for numbers — but keeping the order of each product:

$$\begin{pmatrix} A{11} & A{12} \\ A{21} & A{22} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} B{11} & B{12} \\ B{21} & B{22} \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} A{11}B{11} + A{12}B{21} & A{11}B{12} + A{12}B{22} \\ A{21}B{11} + A{22}B{21} & A{21}B{12} + A{22}B{22} \end{pmatrix}.$$

The four views of Chapter 1 are all special cases: the column view cuts $B$ into columns, the row view cuts $A$ into rows, and the outer-product view cuts $A$ into columns and $B$ into rows.

Block elimination and the Schur complement

Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}$ with $A$ square and invertible. Eliminating the block $C$ is one block row operation — "block row 2 minus $CA^{-1}$ times block row 1":

$$\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -CA^{-1} & I \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D - CA^{-1}B \end{pmatrix}.$$

The block $S = D - CA^{-1}B$ is the Schur complement of $A$ in $M$. When every block is $1 \times 1$ this is exactly one step of ordinary elimination: $d - (c/a)b$. Schur complements appear in statistics (conditional covariance), in optimization and in domain-decomposition solvers.

> Watch out: in block formulas every product must keep its order. $CA^{-1}B$ cannot be rewritten as $A^{-1}BC$ or $BCA^{-1}$.

Chapter 4 — Elementary matrices

An elementary matrix is the result of one row operation on the identity. Multiplying $A$ on the left by $E$ performs that row operation on $A$ (by the row view: row $i$ of $EA$ is row $i$ of $E$ times $A$).

Using the $3 \times 3$ system from Linear Algebra 00,

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadE{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadE{32} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

$E{21}$ subtracts 3 times row 1 from row 2; $E{32}$ subtracts 2 times row 2 from row 3. (No $E{31}$ is needed because $a{31}$ is already 0.) Then

$$E{32}E{21}A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = U.$$

Every elementary matrix is invertible, and its inverse is elementary of the same type: undo "subtract $\ell$ times row $j$" by "add $\ell$ times row $j$"; undo a swap by the same swap; undo scaling by $c$ by scaling by $1/c$. For example

$$E{21}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The product in the "wrong" order is messy

$$E{32}E{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 6 & -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\qquadE{21}^{-1}E{32}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The entry 6 in the first product appears because row 3 is changed using the new row 2, which already contains $-3$ times row 1; indeed $6 = (-2)(-3)$. The inverses, multiplied in the opposite order, do not interact at all: the multipliers $3$ and $2$ simply drop into place. That clean matrix is $L$, and Chapter 7 is built on it. (The messy product $E{32}E{21}$ is $L^{-1}$.)

Chapter 5 — The inverse of a matrix

Definition and uniqueness

A square $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible (or nonsingular) if there is a matrix $C$ with

$$CA = I \quad \text{and} \quad AC = I.$$

Then $C$ is called the inverse and written $A^{-1}$. A square matrix without an inverse is singular.

The inverse is unique. If $CA = I$ and $AD = I$, then $C = CI = C(AD) = (CA)D = ID = D$. $\blacksquare$ (This short argument even shows a left inverse and a right inverse must agree.)

Properties. If $A$ and $B$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices, then

$$(A^{-1})^{-1} = A, \qquad (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}, \qquad (A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T.$$

Proof. $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$, and similarly in the other order. For the transpose, take the transpose of $AA^{-1} = I$: $(A^{-1})^TA^T = I$, and similarly $A^T(A^{-1})^T = I$. $\blacksquare$

If $A$ is invertible, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$, namely $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$: it is a solution because $A(A^{-1}\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b}$, and it is the only one because $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ implies $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}A\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.

The 2 × 2 formula

$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{provided } ad - bc \neq 0.$$

Proof. Multiply: $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc \end{pmatrix}$, and the same holds in the other order. Divide by $ad - bc$. If $ad - bc = 0$, the same product shows $A$ times a matrix gives $0$; Exercise 6 shows $A$ is then singular. $\blacksquare$

The number $ad - bc$ is the determinant of the $2 \times 2$ matrix (Linear Algebra 03 defines it for all sizes). For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$: $ad - bc = 4 - 6 = -2$, so

$$A^{-1} = -\frac12 \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Gauss–Jordan on [ A | I ]

Why it works. Suppose row operations $E1, \dots, Ek$ turn $A$ into $I$: $Ek \cdots E1 A = I$. Then $Ek\cdots E1$ is a left inverse of $A$, and (by the invertible matrix theorem below) it is $A^{-1}$. Applying the same operations to $I$ produces $Ek \cdots E1 I = A^{-1}$. So: row reduce the wide matrix $[\,A \mid I\,]$; if the left half becomes $I$, the right half is $A^{-1}$. If the left half develops a zero row, $A$ is singular.

Worked example. Take the "second difference" matrix

$$K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Forward elimination: $R2 \leftarrow R2 + \tfrac12 R1$, then $R3 \leftarrow R3 + \tfrac23 R2$:

$$\left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 2 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3/2 & -1 & 1/2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4/3 & 1/3 & 2/3 & 1 \end{array}\right).$$

The pivots are $2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43$. Backward elimination: $R2 \leftarrow R2 + \tfrac34 R3$, then $R1 \leftarrow R1 + \tfrac23 R2$:

$$\left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 2 & 0 & 0 & 3/2 & 1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 3/2 & 0 & 3/4 & 3/2 & 3/4 \\ 0 & 0 & 4/3 & 1/3 & 2/3 & 1 \end{array}\right).$$

Divide each row by its pivot:

$$K^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3/4 & 1/2 & 1/4 \\ 1/2 & 1 & 1/2 \\ 1/4 & 1/2 & 3/4 \end{pmatrix} = \frac14\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Two observations that matter later. $K$ is symmetric and so is $K^{-1}$ (Exercise 7). And $K$ is tridiagonal — mostly zeros — but $K^{-1}$ is completely full. Keep that in mind for Chapter 8.

Chapter 6 — The invertible matrix theorem

Theorem. For a square $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the following are equivalent (all true or all false):

1. $A$ is invertible.2. $A$ is row equivalent to $In$.3. $A$ has $n$ pivot positions.4. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution.5. The columns of $A$ are linearly independent.6. $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has at least one solution for every $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.7. The columns of $A$ span $\mathbb{R}^n$.8. There is an $n \times n$ matrix $C$ with $CA = I$.9. There is an $n \times n$ matrix $D$ with $AD = I$.10. $A^T$ is invertible.11. $A$ is a product of elementary matrices.

Proof. (1) ⇒ (8) and (1) ⇒ (9): take $C = D = A^{-1}$.

(8) ⇒ (4): if $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ then $\mathbf{x} = CA\mathbf{x} = C\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.

(4) ⇔ (5): this is the definition of independence (Linear Algebra 00, Chapter 8).

(4) ⇒ (3): only the trivial solution means no free variables, so every one of the $n$ columns is a pivot column.

(3) ⇒ (2): $n$ pivots in an $n \times n$ matrix means one pivot in every row and every column, on the diagonal, so the RREF is $In$.

(2) ⇒ (11): $Ek\cdots E1A = I$ gives $A = E1^{-1}\cdots Ek^{-1}$, a product of elementary matrices.

(11) ⇒ (1): elementary matrices are invertible, and so is any product of invertible matrices.

(9) ⇒ (6): given $\mathbf{b}$, the vector $\mathbf{x} = D\mathbf{b}$ satisfies $A\mathbf{x} = AD\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$.

(6) ⇔ (7): $A\mathbf{x}$ ranges over all combinations of the columns (Linear Algebra 00).

(6) ⇒ (3): if $A$ had fewer than $n$ pivots, its echelon form $U$ would have a zero last row. Choose $\mathbf{c}$ with last entry 1; then $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$ is inconsistent, and undoing the row operations on $\mathbf{c}$ gives a $\mathbf{b}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ inconsistent, contradicting (6).

(1) ⇔ (10): if $A$ is invertible, $(A^{-1})^T$ inverts $A^T$ (Chapter 5); apply this to $A^T$ for the converse.

Every statement is now linked to every other. $\blacksquare$

Corollary (one-sided is enough). If $A$ and $B$ are square and $AB = I$, then $BA = I$ and $B = A^{-1}$. Proof: $AB = I$ is statement (9) for $A$, so $A$ is invertible; multiply $AB = I$ on the left by $A^{-1}$ to get $B = A^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

> Watch out: the corollary needs square matrices. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $AB = (1) = I1$ but $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq I2$.

Linear Algebra 02, 03 and 04 add more lines to this list: rank $n$, null space $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, $\det A \neq 0$, and $0$ is not an eigenvalue.

Chapter 7 — LU factorization

From elimination to A = LU

Suppose elimination on $A$ needs no row exchanges. Then only replacement operations "row $i$ minus $\ell{ij}$ times row $j$" (with $i > j$) are used, and

$$(E{32}E{31}E{21})\,A = U \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A = (E{21}^{-1}E{31}^{-1}E{32}^{-1})\,U = LU$$

(written for $n = 3$; larger $n$ is the same). $U$ is upper triangular with the pivots on its diagonal. $L$ is unit lower triangular, and as Chapter 4 showed, its entries below the diagonal are exactly the multipliers: $L{ij} = \ell{ij}$.

For the running example, the multipliers were $\ell{21} = 3$, $\ell{31} = 0$, $\ell{32} = 2$, so

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiply it out to check: row 2 of $LU$ is $3(1,2,1) + (0,2,-2) = (3, 8, 1)$, and row 3 is $2(0,2,-2) + (0,0,5) = (0, 4, 1)$.

Solving with L and U

$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ becomes $L(U\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b}$. Put $\mathbf{c} = U\mathbf{x}$ and solve two triangular systems:

1. Forward substitution $L\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{b}$ (top to bottom).2. Back substitution $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$ (bottom to top).

With $\mathbf{b} = (2, 12, 2)$: $c1 = 2$; $3c1 + c2 = 12$ gives $c2 = 6$; $2c2 + c3 = 2$ gives $c3 = -10$. So $\mathbf{c} = (2, 6, -10)$ — exactly the right-hand side that elimination produced in Linear Algebra 00. Then $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$ gives $z = -2$, $y = 1$, $x = 2$, as before.

The point: the factorization is done once; each new right-hand side then costs only two cheap triangular solves.

LDU and the symmetric case

Divide each row of $U$ by its pivot to get $A = LDU'$ with $D$ the diagonal matrix of pivots and $U'$ unit upper triangular. For the example, $D = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 5)$ and

$$U' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

When $A$ is symmetric and has an LU factorization, $U' = L^T$, so $A = LDL^T$. For a symmetric matrix with all pivots positive this becomes the Cholesky factorization $A = (LD^{1/2})(LD^{1/2})^T$, which costs about half as much as LU.

Uniqueness

Theorem. If $A$ is invertible and $A = L1U1 = L2U2$ with $L1, L2$ unit lower triangular and $U1, U2$ upper triangular, then $L1 = L2$ and $U1 = U2$.

Proof. Since $A$ is invertible, so are the $Ui$ (and the unit-diagonal $Li$ always are). From $L1U1 = L2U2$,

$$L2^{-1}L1 = U2U1^{-1}.$$

The left side is unit lower triangular (inverses and products of unit lower triangular matrices are unit lower triangular — Chapter 2 and Exercise 5), and the right side is upper triangular. A matrix that is both is diagonal, and its diagonal is all 1s from the left side. So both sides equal $I$, which gives $L1 = L2$ and $U1 = U2$. $\blacksquare$

Row exchanges: PA = LU

Elimination breaks down when a zero appears in a pivot position. Example:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The (1,1) entry is 0, so swap rows 1 and 2 first. If all the exchanges are done at the start, they form one permutation matrix $P$, and $PA$ factors without further trouble:

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadPA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The multipliers were $\ell{21} = 0$, $\ell{31} = 2$ (row 3 becomes $(0, 3, 7)$), then $\ell{32} = 3$ (row 3 becomes $(0, 0, 4)$).

Theorem. For every invertible $A$ there is a permutation matrix $P$ such that $PA = LU$ with $L$ unit lower triangular and $U$ upper triangular with nonzero pivots. An invertible $A$ has an LU factorization with no exchanges ($P = I$) exactly when every leading principal submatrix (the top-left $k \times k$ block, $k = 1, \dots, n$) is invertible.

To solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ from $PA = LU$: $LU\mathbf{x} = P\mathbf{b}$, so permute $\mathbf{b}$, then do the two triangular solves.

> Common pitfall: after factoring $PA = LU$, forgetting to apply $P$ to $\mathbf{b}$. The factorization is of $PA$, not of $A$.

Chapter 8 — Cost, and why not to compute the inverse

Counting flops

A flop is one floating-point add, subtract, multiply or divide. At elimination step $k$ of an $n \times n$ matrix, each of the $n - k$ rows below the pivot needs one division (its multiplier) and a multiply and a subtract for each of the remaining $n - k$ entries. So step $k$ costs $(n - k)\bigl(2(n - k) + 1\bigr)$ flops, and in total

$$\sum{k=1}^{n} \bigl( 2(n-k)^2 + (n-k) \bigr) = \frac{2}{3}n^3 - \frac12 n^2 - \frac16 n \approx \frac23 n^3.$$

Each triangular solve costs about $n^2$ flops. So for $n = 1000$: roughly $6.7 \times 10^8$ flops to factor, then only about $2 \times 10^6$ per right-hand side.

Why solving with the inverse is the wrong way

1. Speed. Forming $A^{-1}$ costs about $2n^3$ flops — roughly three times the LU factorization — and then $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ still costs $2n^2$, the same as the two triangular solves it was supposed to replace. There is nothing to gain even with many right-hand sides, because LU is reusable too.2. Accuracy. Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting is backward stable in practice: the computed $\mathbf{x}$ exactly solves a system with a very slightly perturbed $A$. Multiplying by a computed inverse does not have this guarantee in general, and its residual $\lVert \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} \rVert$ is typically larger (Higham, Chapters 9 and 14).3. Structure. A banded or sparse matrix has banded or sparse LU factors (without pivoting, a tridiagonal $K$ has bidiagonal $L$ and $U$), but its inverse is usually full — as $K^{-1}$ in Chapter 5 showed. For large sparse problems the inverse may not even fit in memory.

In code this means  (which calls LAPACK's LU-based ), or  once and  for each right-hand side — never .

> Trick: read $A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ in a formula as "the solution of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$", not as an instruction to invert. The same holds for expressions like $A^{-1}B$ (solve with many right-hand sides) and $\mathbf{c}^TA^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ (one solve, then one dot product).

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1 — Order matters

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $AB$ and $BA$. Describe $AB$ with the row view.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, which differ. Row view: $A$ is an elementary matrix ("add 2 times row 1 to row 2"), so $AB$ is $B$ with row 2 replaced by $(0,2) + 2(3,1) = (6, 4)$.

</details>

Exercise 2 — Transpose of a triple product

Prove $(ABC)^T = C^TB^TA^T$, and deduce that $B^TAB$ is symmetric whenever $A$ is symmetric.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$(ABC)^T = ((AB)C)^T = C^T(AB)^T = C^TB^TA^T$. If $A^T = A$, then $(B^TAB)^T = B^TA^T(B^T)^T = B^TAB$.

</details>

Exercise 3 — Inverses

Find the inverse of $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ with the $2 \times 2$ formula, and of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ by Gauss–Jordan.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$ad - bc = 6 - 5 = 1$, so the first inverse is $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

For the $3 \times 3$ matrix, row reducing $[\,A \mid I\,]$ gives

$$A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Check one entry of $AA^{-1}$: row 3 of $A$ times column 1 of $A^{-1}$ is $5(-24) + 6(20) + 0 = 0$, as it should be.

</details>

Exercise 4 — LU by hand

Factor $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 & 0 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ as $LU$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$\ell{21} = 4/2 = 2$ and $\ell{31} = -2/2 = -1$ give rows $(0, -8, -2)$ and $(0, 8, 3)$. Then $\ell{32} = 8/(-8) = -1$ gives row 3 $= (0, 0, 1)$. So

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquadU = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -8 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Pivots $2, -8, 1$.

</details>

Exercise 5 — Inverse of a unit lower triangular matrix

Show that the inverse of a unit lower triangular matrix is unit lower triangular (this completes the uniqueness proof in Chapter 7).

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

A unit lower triangular $L$ reduces to $I$ using only operations "row $i$ minus a multiple of row $j$" with $i > j$ (no swaps, no scaling, since the pivots are already 1). Each such elementary matrix is unit lower triangular, so $L^{-1}$, the product of these elementary matrices, is unit lower triangular by the Chapter 2 result.

</details>

Exercise 6 — A zero pivot that cannot be fixed

Show that if $ad - bc = 0$ then $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is singular. Also show that $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible but has no LU factorization without a row exchange.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

If $A = 0$ it is singular. Otherwise $M = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \neq 0$ and $AM = 0$. If $A$ were invertible, $M = A^{-1}AM = 0$, a contradiction.

For the second matrix, $ad - bc = -1 \neq 0$, so it is invertible. If $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \ell & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} u{11} & u{12} \\ 0 & u{22} \end{pmatrix}$, the (1,1) entry forces $u{11} = 0$, but then the (2,1) entry is $\ell u{11} = 0 \neq 1$. Its first leading principal submatrix $(0)$ is singular, as the theorem predicts. With the swap, $PA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I \cdot U$.

</details>

Exercise 7 — Symmetric inverse

Prove: if $A$ is symmetric and invertible, then $A^{-1}$ is symmetric.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$, using the transpose rule for inverses from Chapter 5 and $A^T = A$.

</details>

Chapter 1 — Vectors in n-space

Terminology

- Scalar — a single real number. Later in the series scalars may come from any field (see Linear Algebra 02), but for now they are real.- Vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ — an ordered list of $n$ real numbers, written as a column. The numbers are its entries or components.- Zero vector $\mathbf{0}$ — the vector whose entries are all 0.- Vector addition — add entry by entry. Only defined for two vectors of the same length.- Scalar multiplication — multiply every entry by the same scalar.

$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v1 \\ v2 \\ \vdots \\ vn \end{pmatrix}, \qquad\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} v1 + w1 \\ \vdots \\ vn + wn \end{pmatrix}, \qquadc\,\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} c v1 \\ \vdots \\ c vn \end{pmatrix}.$$

Algebraic rules

For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and all scalars $c, d$:

1. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$ and $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$.2. $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$.3. $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$ and $(c + d)\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{v} + d\mathbf{v}$.4. $c(d\mathbf{v}) = (cd)\mathbf{v}$ and $1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$.

Each rule follows from the matching rule for real numbers, applied one entry at a time. These eight rules are exactly the axioms of a vector space; Linear Algebra 02 turns them into a definition, and then polynomials, functions and matrices become "vectors" too.

The geometric picture

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ a vector is an arrow from the origin to the point with those coordinates.

- Addition is the parallelogram rule: place the tail of $\mathbf{w}$ at the head of $\mathbf{v}$; the sum ends where $\mathbf{w}$ ends.- Scaling stretches the arrow by $\lvert c\rvert$ and reverses it when $c < 0$.- $\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}$ is the arrow from the head of $\mathbf{w}$ to the head of $\mathbf{v}$.

For $n > 3$ there is no picture, but every algebraic fact still holds. The habit to build is: reason with the algebra, check the intuition in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$.

> Watch out: a point and a vector are written the same way in $\mathbb{R}^n$, but they play different roles. A point is a location; a vector is a displacement. The distinction matters in Chapter 7, where a solution set can be a plane that does not pass through the origin.

Chapter 2 — Linear combinations and span

Terminology

- Linear combination of $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k$ — any vector $c1\mathbf{v}1 + c2\mathbf{v}2 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k$ with scalars $c1, \dots, ck$ (the weights or coefficients).- Span — the set of all linear combinations:

$$\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k\} = \{\, c1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k : c1, \dots, ck \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.$$

The span always contains $\mathbf{0}$ (take every weight 0) and each $\mathbf{v}i$ itself (weight 1 on it, 0 elsewhere).

Geometric picture of a span in R³

| Vectors | Span ||---|---|| only $\mathbf{0}$ | the single point $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ || one nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ | the line through the origin in direction $\mathbf{v}$ || two vectors, neither a multiple of the other | the plane through the origin containing both || two vectors, one a multiple of the other | still only a line || three vectors not in one plane | all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ || three vectors in one plane | only that plane (or a line) |

The pattern "adding a vector enlarges the span only if the new vector is not already inside it" is the seed of linear independence (Chapter 8) and of dimension (Linear Algebra 02).

Worked example — is b in the span?

Is $\mathbf{b} = (1, 5)$ a combination of $\mathbf{a}1 = (2, 1)$ and $\mathbf{a}2 = (-1, 1)$? We need $x, y$ with

$$x \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{aligned} 2x - y &= 1 \\ x + y &= 5. \end{aligned}$$

Adding the two equations gives $3x = 6$, so $x = 2$ and $y = 3$. Check: $2(2,1) + 3(-1,1) = (4-3,\ 2+3) = (1, 5)$. So $\mathbf{b}$ is in the span. In fact, since $\mathbf{a}1$ and $\mathbf{a}2$ are not parallel, their span is the whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.

The key lesson: "is $\mathbf{b}$ in the span of these vectors?" is the same question as "does this linear system have a solution?" Everything in this post comes back to that sentence.

Chapter 3 — Dot product and length

This chapter is brief; inner products get their full treatment in Linear Algebra 05.

Definitions

- Dot product: $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u1 v1 + u2 v2 + \dots + un vn$. It takes two vectors and returns a scalar.- Length (norm): $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}} = \sqrt{v1^2 + \dots + vn^2}$.- Unit vector: a vector of length 1. For $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the vector $\mathbf{v} / \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$ is the unit vector in the same direction.- Orthogonal: $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal (perpendicular) when $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.

Properties

The dot product is symmetric ($\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}$), linear in each argument, and positive definite: $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} \ge 0$, with equality only for $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. Two inequalities hold for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (proofs in Linear Algebra 05):

$$\lvert \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} \rvert \le \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \quad \text{(Cauchy–Schwarz)}, \qquad\lVert \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \rVert \le \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert + \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \quad \text{(triangle inequality)}.$$

Cauchy–Schwarz is what makes the angle formula legitimate: for nonzero vectors the ratio below lies in $[-1, 1]$, so it is the cosine of exactly one angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$.

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{v}\rVert}.$$

Worked example

Let $\mathbf{u} = (1, 2, 2)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (2, -2, 1)$. Then $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$, so $\mathbf{u}/3 = (1/3, 2/3, 2/3)$ is a unit vector. And $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 2 - 4 + 2 = 0$, so $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal.

> Trick: the dot product gives a second reading of one equation. The equation $a1x1 + \dots + anxn = b$ says $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x} = b$. When $b = 0$, the solutions are exactly the vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{a}$ — a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$, a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, a "hyperplane" in general.

Chapter 4 — Systems of linear equations

Terminology

- Linear equation in $x1, \dots, xn$ — an equation $a1x1 + \dots + anxn = b$ with constant coefficients $ai$ and constant $b$. No products $xixj$, no powers, no $\sin x$.- System of linear equations — a finite list of linear equations in the same unknowns.- Solution — a vector $\mathbf{x}$ that satisfies every equation at once. The solution set is the set of all solutions.- Consistent — has at least one solution. Inconsistent — has none.- Equivalent systems — systems with the same solution set.- Coefficient matrix $A$ — the $m \times n$ array of the $a{ij}$. Augmented matrix $[\,A \mid \mathbf{b}\,]$ — $A$ with $\mathbf{b}$ attached as an extra column.

Matrix times vector

Write the columns of $A$ as $\mathbf{a}1, \dots, \mathbf{a}n$. The product $A\mathbf{x}$ is defined as a combination of the columns, with the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ as weights:

$$A\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{a}1 & \mathbf{a}2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}n \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x1 \\ \vdots \\ xn \end{pmatrix} = x1\mathbf{a}1 + x2\mathbf{a}2 + \dots + xn\mathbf{a}n.$$

Entry $i$ of that combination is $a{i1}x1 + \dots + a{in}xn$, which is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{x}$. So there are two ways to compute $A\mathbf{x}$ — by columns and by rows — and they always agree. A system of $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns is therefore one vector equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, with $A$ of size $m \times n$, $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

Two rules follow straight from the definition and are used constantly: $A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v}$ and $A(c\mathbf{u}) = c\,A\mathbf{u}$.

Row picture and column picture

Take the system from Chapter 2:

$$\begin{aligned} 2x - y &= 1 \\ x + y &= 5 \end{aligned}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

- Row picture. Each equation is a line in the $xy$-plane: $y = 2x - 1$ and $y = 5 - x$. A solution is a point on both lines. They cross at $(2, 3)$.- Column picture. The same system asks for weights on the columns $(2, 1)$ and $(-1, 1)$ that produce $(1, 5)$. The answer is $2$ of the first column plus $3$ of the second.

The same numbers $x = 2$, $y = 3$ appear in both pictures, but they mean different things: a point where lines cross, or weights in a combination.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the row picture has three planes, and a solution is a point common to all three. The column picture has three column vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and the question is whether $\mathbf{b}$ is a combination of them. The column picture scales to any size and becomes the main point of view from Linear Algebra 02 onwards (column space).

How many solutions can there be?

Theorem (zero, one or infinitely many). A linear system has either no solution, exactly one solution, or infinitely many solutions.

Proof. Suppose $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are two different solutions, so $A\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$. For every real $t$, put $\mathbf{z}t = \mathbf{x} + t(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x})$. Then

$$A\mathbf{z}t = A\mathbf{x} + t(A\mathbf{y} - A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b} + t(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{b}.$$

Since $\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$, different values of $t$ give different vectors $\mathbf{z}t$, so there are infinitely many solutions. $\blacksquare$

Geometrically: the whole line through two solutions consists of solutions. Two lines in the plane cannot meet in exactly two points.

Chapter 5 — Elimination

Elementary row operations

Three operations on the rows of an augmented matrix:

1. Replacement — add a multiple of one row to another row: $Ri \leftarrow Ri + cRj$ (with $i \neq j$).2. Interchange — swap two rows: $Ri \leftrightarrow Rj$.3. Scaling — multiply a row by a nonzero constant: $Ri \leftarrow cRi$, $c \neq 0$.

Two matrices are row equivalent if one can be turned into the other by a finite sequence of these operations.

Theorem. If two augmented matrices are row equivalent, the corresponding systems have the same solution set.

Proof. It is enough to check one operation at a time. Any $\mathbf{x}$ that satisfies the old equations also satisfies the new ones: a swap or a scaling changes nothing essential, and if $\mathbf{x}$ satisfies equations $i$ and $j$ it satisfies their sum "$i + c\cdot j$". Conversely, each operation can be undone by an operation of the same type — replacement by $Ri \leftarrow Ri - cRj$, interchange by the same swap, scaling by $1/c$ (this is why $c \neq 0$ is required). So any solution of the new system also solves the old one. $\blacksquare$

> Common pitfall: multiplying a row by $0$ is not allowed. It destroys an equation and can create new "solutions". Scaling needs $c \neq 0$ precisely so the step is reversible.

Gaussian elimination on a 3 × 3 system

Solve

$$\begin{aligned}x + 2y + z &= 2 \\3x + 8y + z &= 12 \\4y + z &= 2\end{aligned}\qquad[\,A \mid \mathbf{b}\,] = \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 & 1 & 12 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right).$$

Step 1 — clear column 1 below the first pivot. The first pivot is the 1 in position (1,1). Row 2 needs multiplier $\ell{21} = 3/1 = 3$; row 3 already has a 0, so $\ell{31} = 0$.

$$R2 \leftarrow R2 - 3R1: \qquad\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right).$$

Step 2 — clear column 2 below the second pivot. The second pivot is 2 in position (2,2). Multiplier $\ell{32} = 4/2 = 2$.

$$R3 \leftarrow R3 - 2R2: \qquad\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 & -10 \end{array}\right).$$

The coefficient part is now upper triangular, with pivots $1, 2, 5$.

Step 3 — back substitution. From the bottom up: $5z = -10$ gives $z = -2$. Then $2y - 2z = 6$ gives $2y = 2$, so $y = 1$. Then $x + 2y + z = 2$ gives $x = 2 - 2 + 2 = 2$.

Check in the original equations: $2 + 2 - 2 = 2$, $6 + 8 - 2 = 12$, $4 - 2 = 2$. The unique solution is $(x, y, z) = (2, 1, -2)$.

The multipliers $3, 0, 2$ are not thrown away in practice — Linear Algebra 01 stores them in a lower triangular matrix $L$ and gets the factorization $A = LU$.

When elimination breaks down

- Zero in a pivot position, nonzero below it — swap rows and continue. This is a temporary breakdown.- Zero in a pivot position and zeros all the way down — there is no pivot in that column. The matrix is singular (Linear Algebra 01) and the system has either no solution or infinitely many. Chapter 6 handles this case completely.

> Watch out: on a computer, a pivot that is merely small is as dangerous as a zero one, because dividing by it magnifies rounding error. Software therefore swaps in the largest available entry of the column (partial pivoting). By hand, with exact numbers, any nonzero pivot is fine.

Chapter 6 — Echelon forms, pivots and free variables

Terminology

- Leading entry of a row — its leftmost nonzero entry.- Row echelon form (REF) — (i) all zero rows are at the bottom; (ii) each leading entry is in a column to the right of the leading entry of the row above; (iii) all entries below a leading entry are zero.- Reduced row echelon form (RREF) — REF, plus (iv) every leading entry is 1, and (v) each leading 1 is the only nonzero entry in its column.- Pivot position — a position that holds a leading entry in an echelon form of the matrix. Pivot column — a column containing a pivot position.- Basic variable — a variable whose column is a pivot column. Free variable — any other variable.

Gauss–Jordan elimination continues past REF: scale each pivot to 1, then clear the entries above each pivot, working from the bottom pivot up.

Uniqueness of the RREF

Theorem. Each matrix is row equivalent to one and only one matrix in reduced row echelon form.

Existence is the Gauss–Jordan algorithm. Uniqueness takes a careful induction on the number of columns; see Lay, Appendix A, or Hoffman–Kunze, Chapter 1. It has an important consequence: the pivot positions — and so the pivot columns, the number of pivots and the split into basic and free variables — do not depend on which row operations you chose. An REF is not unique (you can always scale a row by 2), but its pivot positions are.

A worked 3 × 4 example

Solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 6 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Forward elimination, $R2 \leftarrow R2 - 2R1$ and $R3 \leftarrow R3 - 3R1$:

$$\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 4 \end{array}\right).$$

Column 2 has no pivot (zeros from row 2 down), so move to column 3; its pivot is the 2 in row 2. Then $R3 \leftarrow R3 - R2$:

$$\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right)\quad \text{(REF)}.$$

Gauss–Jordan: $R2 \leftarrow \tfrac12 R2$, then $R1 \leftarrow R1 - 2R2$:

$$\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & -2 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right)\quad \text{(RREF)}.$$

Pivot columns are 1 and 3, so $x1, x3$ are basic and $x2, x4$ are free. The RREF reads

$$x1 = -3 - 2x2 + 2x4, \qquad x3 = 2 - 2x4, \qquad x2, x4 \text{ free}.$$

Parametric vector form

Write the free variables as parameters $x2 = s$, $x4 = t$ and collect terms:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x1 \\ x2 \\ x3 \\ x4 \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}+ s \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}+ t \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},\qquad s, t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

The solution set is a 2-dimensional plane inside $\mathbb{R}^4$, one parameter per free variable. Check the first vector: $A(-3, 0, 2, 0) = (-3 + 4,\ -6 + 12,\ -9 + 16) = (1, 6, 7) = \mathbf{b}$.

Consistency

The zero row of the augmented RREF above ended in 0, so it said $0 = 0$ — harmless. Change $\mathbf{b}$ to $(1, 6, 8)$ and the same steps end with the row $(0\ 0\ 0\ 0 \mid 1)$, which says $0 = 1$. That system is inconsistent. In general:

Theorem (existence and uniqueness). A linear system is consistent if and only if the last column of its augmented matrix is not a pivot column — that is, no echelon form has a row $(0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid c)$ with $c \neq 0$. If it is consistent, then it has exactly one solution when there are no free variables, and infinitely many when there is at least one free variable.

Proof. Row operations do not change the solution set, so study the RREF. A row $(0 \cdots 0 \mid c)$ with $c \neq 0$ is the equation $0 = c$, which nothing satisfies. If there is no such row, every nonzero row of the RREF has a leading 1 in a coefficient column and expresses one basic variable in terms of the free ones; choosing any values for the free variables produces a solution. With no free variables that solution is forced and unique; with at least one free variable each choice of its value gives a different solution. $\blacksquare$

For $\mathbf{b} = (b1, b2, b3)$ and our $A$, the condition works out to $b3 = b1 + b2$: row 3 of $A$ is row 1 plus row 2, so the right-hand sides must obey the same relation.

> Trick: count before you compute. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and has $r$ pivots, then $r \le m$ and $r \le n$, and there are $n - r$ free variables. Here $m = 3$, $n = 4$, $r = 2$: two free variables, and a solution for $\mathbf{b}$ only when $\mathbf{b}$ passes one test (one zero row).

Chapter 7 — Homogeneous systems and the shape of the solution set

Terminology

- Homogeneous system — $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. It always has the trivial solution $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.- Nontrivial solution — a solution $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

Theorem. A homogeneous system with more unknowns than equations ($n > m$) has a nontrivial solution.

Proof. There are at most $m$ pivots, one per row, so at least $n - m > 0$ variables are free. The system is consistent (it has $\mathbf{0}$), so by the existence-and-uniqueness theorem it has infinitely many solutions, and in particular a nonzero one. $\blacksquare$

For the $3 \times 4$ matrix above, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ has the same RREF with a zero last column, and its solutions are

$$\mathbf{x} = s \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The two vectors are the special solutions: set one free variable to 1, the others to 0, and solve. This set is a plane through the origin — the null space of $A$, studied in Linear Algebra 02.

Particular plus homogeneous

Theorem. Suppose $A\mathbf{x}p = \mathbf{b}$. Then the solution set of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is exactly

$$\{\, \mathbf{x}p + \mathbf{x}h : A\mathbf{x}h = \mathbf{0} \,\}.$$

Proof. If $A\mathbf{x}h = \mathbf{0}$ then $A(\mathbf{x}p + \mathbf{x}h) = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{b}$. Conversely, if $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, put $\mathbf{x}h = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}p$; then $A\mathbf{x}h = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}p + \mathbf{x}h$. $\blacksquare$

Compare with the parametric form in Chapter 6: $(-3, 0, 2, 0)$ is a particular solution, and the $s$ and $t$ terms are exactly the homogeneous solutions. Geometrically, the solution set of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is the null-space plane shifted by $\mathbf{x}p$ — parallel to it, but not through the origin (unless $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$).

> Common pitfall: the solution set of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ is not closed under addition: if $A\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$ then $A(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) = 2\mathbf{b}$. Only the homogeneous solution set is a "subspace". The particular solution is not unique either — any solution can play that role.

This is the same structure as linear differential equations ("general solution = particular solution + solution of the homogeneous equation"), and for the same reason: the operator is linear.

Chapter 8 — Linear independence, a first look

Terminology

- Linearly independent — vectors $\mathbf{v}1, \dots, \mathbf{v}k$ such that the only solution of $c1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k = \mathbf{0}$ is $c1 = \dots = ck = 0$.- Linearly dependent — not independent: some combination with not all weights zero gives $\mathbf{0}$. Such an equation is a linear dependence relation.

Put the vectors as the columns of $A$. Then $c1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k = A\mathbf{c}$, so:

> The columns of $A$ are linearly independent $\iff$ $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has only the trivial solution $\iff$ every column of $A$ is a pivot column (no free variables).

Worked example

Are $\mathbf{v}1 = (1, 2, 3)$, $\mathbf{v}2 = (4, 5, 6)$, $\mathbf{v}3 = (7, 8, 9)$ independent? Row reduce

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 & 8 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & -6 & -12 \end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Column 3 is not a pivot column, so the vectors are dependent. The free variable $c3 = 1$ gives $-3c2 - 6 = 0$, so $c2 = -2$, and $c1 + 4(-2) + 7 = 0$, so $c1 = 1$. The dependence relation is

$$\mathbf{v}1 - 2\mathbf{v}2 + \mathbf{v}3 = \mathbf{0}.$$

Check: $(1 - 8 + 7,\ 2 - 10 + 8,\ 3 - 12 + 9) = (0, 0, 0)$. Geometrically, the three vectors lie in one plane through the origin, so their span is that plane, not all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Quick facts

Theorem.

1. Any set containing $\mathbf{0}$ is dependent ($1\cdot\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ is a nontrivial relation).2. Two vectors are dependent if and only if one is a scalar multiple of the other.3. A set of vectors is dependent if and only if at least one of them is a linear combination of the others.4. More than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are always dependent.

Proof of 3. If $c1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k = \mathbf{0}$ with some $cj \neq 0$, divide by $cj$ and solve for $\mathbf{v}j$ in terms of the others. Conversely, if $\mathbf{v}j = \sum{i \neq j} di\mathbf{v}i$, then $\sum{i \neq j} di\mathbf{v}i - \mathbf{v}j = \mathbf{0}$ is a relation with weight $-1 \neq 0$ on $\mathbf{v}j$. Statement 2 is the case $k = 2$.

Proof of 4. With $k > n$ vectors as columns, $A$ is $n \times k$ with more unknowns than equations, so by the Chapter 7 theorem $A\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ has a nontrivial solution. $\blacksquare$

> Watch out: statement 3 says some vector is a combination of the others, not every vector. In $\{(1,0), (2,0), (0,1)\}$ the first two are dependent, but $(0,1)$ is not a combination of them.

Linear Algebra 02 makes independence and span precise in any vector space and combines them into the idea of a basis.

Exercise 1 — Span

Is $\mathbf{b} = (3, 1, 2)$ in the span of $\mathbf{v}1 = (1, 0, 1)$ and $\mathbf{v}2 = (1, 1, 0)$? If yes, give the weights.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Solve $x(1,0,1) + y(1,1,0) = (3,1,2)$: the equations are $x + y = 3$, $y = 1$, $x = 2$. They agree: $x = 2$, $y = 1$. So $\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{v}1 + \mathbf{v}2$, and $\mathbf{b}$ is in the span. (Three equations, two unknowns: consistency was not guaranteed; with $\mathbf{b} = (3, 1, 3)$ it would fail.)

Find a unit vector in the direction of $(2, -1, 2)$ and a nonzero vector orthogonal to it.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

The length is $\sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$, so the unit vector is $(2/3, -1/3, 2/3)$. Any $(a, b, c)$ with $2a - b + 2c = 0$ is orthogonal, for example $(1, 2, 0)$: $2 - 2 + 0 = 0$.

</details>

Exercise 3 — Elimination

Solve by Gaussian elimination and back substitution: $x + y + z = 6$, $2x + 3y + z = 11$, $x + 2y + 3z = 14$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$R2 \leftarrow R2 - 2R1$ gives $(0, 1, -1 \mid -1)$. $R3 \leftarrow R3 - R1$ gives $(0, 1, 2 \mid 8)$. $R3 \leftarrow R3 - R2$ gives $(0, 0, 3 \mid 9)$. Back substitution: $z = 3$; $y - 3 = -1$ so $y = 2$; $x + 2 + 3 = 6$ so $x = 1$. Pivots $1, 1, 3$. Check: $2 + 6 + 3 = 11$ and $1 + 4 + 9 = 14$.

</details>

Exercise 4 — RREF and parametric form

Bring $\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & -1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & -2 & 5 & 8 \end{array}\right)$ to RREF and write the solution set in parametric vector form.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$R2 \leftarrow R2 - 2R1$ gives $(0, 0, 1 \mid 2)$. Then $R1 \leftarrow R1 - 2R2$ gives $(1, -1, 0 \mid -1)$. Pivot columns 1 and 3; $x2 = s$ is free. So $x1 = -1 + s$, $x3 = 2$:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + s \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

A line in $\mathbb{R}^3$ that does not pass through the origin.

</details>

Exercise 5 — Consistency condition

For which $h$ is the system $x + 2y = 4$, $3x + 6y = h$ consistent? How many solutions does it have then?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$R2 \leftarrow R2 - 3R1$ gives $(0, 0 \mid h - 12)$. Consistent exactly when $h = 12$. Then $y$ is free and there are infinitely many solutions, $x = 4 - 2y$. In the row picture the two lines are parallel, and they coincide only when $h = 12$.

</details>

Exercise 6 — Independence

Are $(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$ linearly independent? What is their span?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

Row reduce the matrix with these columns: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Three pivots, so the columns are independent. Every row has a pivot too, so $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable for every $\mathbf{b}$: the span is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

</details>

Exercise 7 — Proof

Prove: if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ both solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then so does every linear combination $c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}$. Does the same hold for $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

$A(c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}) = cA\mathbf{u} + dA\mathbf{v} = c\mathbf{0} + d\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$. For $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ it fails: $A(c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{v}) = (c + d)\mathbf{b}$, which equals $\mathbf{b}$ only when $c + d = 1$. (Combinations with weights summing to 1 are called affine combinations; they are exactly what the Chapter 4 proof used.)

</details>

Cheat-sheet

| Idea | Key fact ||---|---|| Linear combination | $c1\mathbf{v}1 + \dots + ck\mathbf{v}k$ || Span | all linear combinations; always contains $\mathbf{0}$ || $A\mathbf{x}$ | $x1\mathbf{a}1 + \dots + xn\mathbf{a}n$ (columns), or row $i$ dot $\mathbf{x}$ (rows) || $\mathbf{b}$ in span of columns | $\iff$ $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is consistent || Length, angle | $\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}$; $\cos\theta = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} / (\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert)$ || Row operations | replace, swap, scale by $c \neq 0$ — all reversible || Number of solutions | 0, 1 or infinitely many || REF vs RREF | REF not unique; RREF unique; pivot positions unique || Inconsistent | a row $(0 \cdots 0 \mid c)$ with $c \neq 0$ || Free variables | $n - r$ for $r$ pivots; each gives one parameter || Homogeneous, $n > m$ | always a nontrivial solution || Solution set | $\mathbf{x}p$ + (all solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$) || Columns independent | $\iff$ a pivot in every column || More than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ | always dependent |

Chapter 9 — Capacity planning

Plan four dimensions:

1. Working set vs RAM

The working set = hot indexes + frequently-accessed docs. Must fit in WiredTiger cache (default 50 % of RAM - 1 GB) for fast reads. If it doesn't, every query touches disk → latency 10-100× worse.

Estimate working set: cumulative size of indexes used in hot queries + 5-10 % of frequently-accessed documents. If RAM < working set, scale up.

2. Storage IOPS

Random read IOPS = how many "miss the cache" queries per second you can serve.

- Atlas tiers have provisioned IOPS per disk type.- AWS gp3: configurable up to 16k IOPS per volume.- NVMe instance-store: 100k+ IOPS but ephemeral.

If IOPS is the bottleneck (visible as high disk queue depth + slow queries despite indexes), scale storage tier.

3. CPU

For pipelines + aggregations + many concurrent queries, CPU matters. Aggregation-heavy workloads peg CPU before disk.

4. Connections

App fleet ×  ≤ tier connection limit (Post 08). Plan headroom for failover bursts.
