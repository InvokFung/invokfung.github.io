All major and minor scales in three or four octaves, double stops in 3rds / 6ths / 8ves / 10ths, the full vocabulary of bow techniques (martelé, ricochet, sautillé, flying staccato, col legno, jeté), and concerto / sonata repertoire approaching professional standard. Learning Outcome

- Play all major and minor three-octave scales + arpeggios fluently.- Play scales in 3rds, 6ths, octaves, and 10ths in all common keys.- Use every bow technique in repertoire.- Apply expressive vibrato (variable speed + width) for musical effect.- Perform a complete concerto movement of significant length (8–15 minutes) from memory.- Read and interpret 20th-century notation (extended techniques, irregular metres).- Demonstrate stylistic understanding: Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern.

Chapter 1 — The full scale + double-stop arsenal

Three-octave scales: every key

By Grade 8 you must own all 12 major and all 12 minor (× 2 = harmonic + melodic) scales — three octaves each.

Practice rotation (4-week cycle)

| Week | Major keys | Minor keys ||------|------------|------------|| 1 | C, G, D, A, E | A, E, B, F♯, C♯ || 2 | B, F♯, C♯, D♭, A♭ | G♯, D♯, A♯, B♭, F || 3 | E♭, B♭, F | C, G, D || 4 | Review weakest 4 keys | Review weakest 4 keys |

15 minutes scales per day, rotating focus across the cycle.

Double stops at Grade 8

| Interval | Difficulty | Key example ||----------|------------|--------------|| 3rds | Moderate (Grade 6+) | G, D, A major || 6ths | Moderate | G, D, A major || Octaves | Hard | G, D major || 10ths | Very hard | Selected keys, single octave || Fingered octaves | Very hard | Heifetz scale system |

10ths require an enormous left-hand stretch (1st + 4th finger on adjacent strings, two octaves and a third apart). Most adult learners take 1–2 years to play 10ths reliably.

> Trick: don't force 10ths into pieces if they're not in the syllabus. Practise as a stretch exercise (open-palm yoga for the violin hand). When the syllabus demands them, you'll already have the reach.

Chapter 2 — Virtuoso bowing toolkit

By Grade 8 every technique is in active use. Quick reference:

| Bowing | Description | Typical use ||--------|-------------|--------------|| Détaché | Smooth separate strokes | Most Classical/Romantic passages || Legato / slur | Connected, one bow | Lyrical lines || Staccato | Sharp short, on string | Articulated passages || Slurred staccato | Multiple stops, one direction | Long lines with articulation || Spiccato | Bow lifts between notes | Light, dancing passages || Sautillé | Very fast spiccato | Fast 16th-note passages || Ricochet | Multiple bounces per drop | Showpieces, cadenzas || Martelé | Hammered, percussive | Accents, dramatic openings || Flying staccato | Lifts each note, one direction | Showpieces || Col legno battuto | Tap with the bow stick | Modern repertoire, Mars (Holst) || Col legno tratto | Draw with the wood | Eerie effects, rare || Sul ponticello | Near the bridge | Glassy colour || Sul tasto | Over the fingerboard | Soft, dreamy colour || Pizzicato | Plucked | Common; Bartók pizz snaps string against fingerboard || Jeté | "Thrown" — multiple notes from a single bounce-then-draw | Showpieces || Tremolo | Rapid bow oscillation on one note | Suspense, orchestral effect |

A piece using nearly all of them

Sarasate Zigeunerweisen uses détaché, legato, spiccato, sautillé, ricochet, harmonics, pizzicato, and flying staccato in one 9-minute work. Reference repertoire for technique synthesis.

Chapter 3 — Vibrato as expression

By Grade 8, vibrato is not "on" or "off" — it's a continuous parameter.

The four vibrato variables

1. Width — narrow (subtle warmth) vs wide (passionate Romantic).2. Speed — slow (sustained nobility) vs fast (intense, virtuoso fire).3. Continuous vs delayed — start the vibrato instantly, or let the note begin straight then add vibrato.4. Pulse — strict steady vibrato vs phrased vibrato (slows and speeds with the phrase).

Vibrato style per period

| Period | Vibrato preference ||--------|---------------------|| Baroque | Sparse, narrow, often as ornament (varies by historical performance practice) || Classical | Selective; on long notes mostly || Romantic | Continuous, wide, warm || 20th-c / Modern | Variable; sometimes deliberately senza vibrato || Folk / world | Narrow, fast, intermittent |

> Trick: listen to one violinist play the same piece from different periods (e.g., Hilary Hahn plays both Bach and Tchaikovsky). Notice how her vibrato choice changes. Internalise the period-appropriate sound by daily listening.

Chapter 4 — 20th-century notation

Grade 8 introduces:

Irregular metres

5/4, 7/8, 11/16. Count subdivisions:

Listen: Holst Mars (5/4), Stravinsky Rite of Spring (constantly shifting metres).

Extended techniques (notation symbols)

| Symbol | Technique ||--------|-----------|| Diamond note | Natural or artificial harmonic || Open circle above note | Natural harmonic || "+" above note | Left-hand pizzicato (pluck with left finger) || "Bartók pizz" symbol | Snap pluck (string against fingerboard) || Wavy line above note | Trill || ↑ ↓ arrows | Glissando direction || Box around staff | Aleatoric (player chooses) |

Atonal pitch language

Some Grade 8 repertoire abandons traditional key signatures. Read by intervals, not by key. Practise by sight-reading short atonal exercises (Bartók Mikrokosmos arranged for violin, etc.).

Chapter 6 — Performance polish

Grade 8 exams test interpretation, not just technique.

Programme planning (typical Grade 8 recital)

| Slot | Choice | Length ||------|--------|--------|| 1 | Baroque (Bach, Handel) | 5–8 min || 2 | Classical / early Romantic (Mozart, Beethoven) | 8–12 min || 3 | Late Romantic / Modern (Brahms, Ravel, Bartók) | 5–10 min || 4 | Virtuoso showpiece | 4–8 min |

Variety across periods is graded.

Stage presentation

- Tuning ritual: walk onstage, tune visibly, take a breath, begin.- Eye contact with pianist (if accompanied) before nodding to start.- Bow direction visible to audience.- Recovery: if you make a mistake, don't react. Continue. Audience usually misses small errors.

Stage anxiety management

- Daily mock performance for 30 days before exam. Play full programme top-to-bottom every day.- Performance-day routine: same warm-up, same meal timing, same clothing dress rehearsal.- Breathing: 4 seconds in, 6 seconds out, before walking onstage. Lowers heart rate.- Visualisation: mentally walk through the recital from arrival to bow, daily for 2 weeks before exam.

> Where I got stuck: I prepared technically but didn't rehearse the experience of performing. First exam: hands shook on the bow. Fix: 4 weeks of daily mock recitals — in front of family, friends, even an empty room with a recording running. By exam day, the room was less intimidating because I'd "performed" 30 times already.

Chapter 7 — Stylistic understanding

Grade 8 exams ask: Why is this Baroque ornament played this way? You should know:

Baroque (1600–1750)

- Trills start on upper note, on the beat.- Less vibrato; vibrato as occasional ornament.- Strong terraced dynamics (sudden f to p, not gradual).- Rubato sparingly; mostly steady tempo.- Détaché with subtle weight changes for phrase shape.

Classical (1750–1820)

- Balanced phrases, clear structure.- Vibrato on long notes only.- Dynamic gradients (cresc, dim) more frequent.- Détaché with elegance; spiccato light.

Romantic (1820–1900)

- Vibrato continuous.- Wide dynamic range; rubato substantial.- Long lyrical lines.- Full use of register; expressive shifts welcomed.

Modern (1900+)

- Technique-heavy.- May use extended techniques.- Senza vibrato on certain passages.- Irregular metres + atonal pitch language possible.

How to internalise style

Listen to one piece per period weekly, with score in hand. Mark performance choices (vibrato, rubato, bowing). Compare across recordings. Over a year, style sensitivity becomes second nature.

Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills

Grade 8 aural is extensive:

- Sing/play 8-bar phrases with modulations and chromatic notes after 1–2 hearings.- Identify period + style at sight/sound.- Identify formal structure (sonata-allegro, rondo, theme + variations).- Identify modulations to distant + enharmonic keys.- Identify orchestral textures (homophonic, polyphonic, monophonic).- Sight-sing complex 8-bar phrases with modulation + chromaticism.

Daily preparation

15 minutes ear training daily for 3 months before exam. Use Tonal Energy, Functional Ear Trainer, or Teoria.com drills. Mix:- Intervals (target: 1 sec recognition)- Chord qualities (target: 2 sec recognition)- Modulation type (target: 5 sec recognition)- Period style (target: 10 sec from short clip)

After Grade 8, the ABRSM diploma path:

| Diploma | Level | Requirements ||---------|-------|--------------|| ARSM | Performance diploma | 30-min recital, professional standard || DipABRSM | Performance / Teaching | 35-min recital + viva / pedagogy paper || LRSM | Licentiate | 50-min recital + extensive theory || FRSM | Fellowship | Concert-artist level |

Closing — the journey from Grade 1

Looking back across this series:

- Grades 1–2: getting the hands to work at all.- Grades 3–4: first shift, first concerto, first real expression.- Grade 5: the bottleneck (vibrato + theory paper).- Grades 6–7: double stops, advanced bowings, full repertoire.- Grade 8: synthesis — every technique in service of musical interpretation.

> Final trick: keep listening. Daily, lifelong. Every great violinist I admire spends more time listening than playing. Hearing shapes the hand. Hearing shapes the bow. Hearing makes you a musician — not just a violinist.

Chapter 1 — Harmonics: the bell-like tones

A harmonic is a high, pure, bell-like tone produced by lightly touching the string at specific nodes (rather than pressing down).

Natural harmonics

Found at division points of the open string:

| Touch point on string | Resulting harmonic | Pitch ||-----------------------|--------------------|-------|| 1/2 (octave node) | 1st overtone | Open string + octave || 1/3 node | 2nd overtone | Octave + 5th || 1/4 node | 3rd overtone | Two octaves above open || 1/5 node | 4th overtone | Two octaves + maj 3rd |

The 1/2 node on the A string is found by lightly touching with your 4th finger at the position where the 4th finger would normally play A5 (one octave above open A). The sound: a soft, ringing high A — an octave higher than expected.

> Trick: harmonics need almost no finger pressure — just a feather touch. If you press, you get a muted scratchy note. If you brush, you get the pure overtone. Practise the touch on open A: bow long stroke, feather-touch where the 4th finger sits in 1st position. The ringing high A appears almost magically.

Artificial harmonics

Press one finger firmly (the "stopping finger") and touch with another finger 1/4 above (the "harmonic finger"). The pitch produced is two octaves above the stopped pitch.

Notation: diamond-shape note head for the touched note, plus regular note for the stopped note.

Used famously in: Tchaikovsky Violin Concerto, Paganini Caprices, Ravel Tzigane.

Chapter 2 — All positions: fluency target

By Grade 7, you've learned half, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th positions. Now: 8th position and fluency across all of them.

8th position

On the A string: 1=B5, 2=C♯6, 3=D6, 4=E6 (right at the top of the violin's standard range for most pieces).

The left hand pivots: thumb shifts to a different angle, hand approaches the bridge.

The fluency goal

You should be able to:1. Shift to any position without looking.2. Find the correct first finger pitch within a quarter-tone, on the first try.3. Choose appropriate position for expression (tone colour) — not just to reach a note.

> Trick: practise a single piece entirely in 1st position, then again entirely in 3rd, then entirely in 5th (skipping notes you can't reach). The same melody in different positions sounds different — different string vibrations, different timbre. Pick the position that suits the music.

Position landmarks (physical)

| Position | Hand landmark ||----------|---------------|| 1st | Index touches the neck near the nut || 3rd | Thumb at the curve of the neck || 5th | Hand at the neck-body joint || 7th | Hand over the body curve || 8th+ | Thumb leaves the neck, sits on the shoulder of the violin |

Chapter 3 — Advanced bowings

Martelé ("hammered")

A short, percussive stroke with a strong attack and a dead stop. Bow pressure increased before the stroke begins, released through the stroke, stops abruptly.

Used for: marching feel, accented passages, opening of dramatic phrases.

Ricochet ("rebound, thrown")

Multiple notes from a single thrown bow. The bow is dropped from above, bounces 2–4 times naturally, lifted before it stops.

Used in: Mendelssohn Concerto last movement, Paganini Caprices, Sarasate showpieces.

Sautillé ("springing")

Very fast spiccato — so fast that the bow no longer fully lifts off; the wood "springs" rapidly. Played in the middle of the bow with a loose grip.

Used in: Vivaldi Four Seasons (Summer storm), Beethoven Concerto 1st mvt, Mendelssohn Concerto 1st mvt.

> Where I got stuck: I couldn't transition from spiccato to sautillé. The hand wanted to "control" each bounce. Fix: extreme tempo experiment. Start a tremolo (very fast back-and-forth on one note). Gradually add finger changes. The wrist learns the bouncing speed first, then the fingers add pitches.

Chapter 4 — Octave double stops

Two notes an octave apart, played simultaneously. Fingering: 1st finger on lower string + 4th finger on upper string, 3 strings apart (e.g., 1 on G + 4 on D for an octave).

Octaves require:- 1st and 4th fingers to be exactly in tune with each other.- 3rd and 4th fingers stretched apart maximally.

Practice

Slow tuner work. Tune 1st finger first; freeze it. Add 4th finger; adjust until the octave rings (no beats). Then bow the double stop.

Practise on every string pair: G+D, D+A, A+E.

> Trick: octaves are the second-hardest interval to tune (after major 7ths). Beats are very audible; an octave that's even slightly off sounds painful. Practise daily for 5 minutes. Octave fluency takes 6 months minimum.

Chapter 7 — Memorisation strategy

By Grade 7, you should perform from memory. Strategy:

1. Analyse the structure first. Break the piece into sections (A-B-A, exposition-development-recap, theme + variations, etc.). Label each section.2. Memorise section by section — not start-to-finish.3. Memorise the hardest section first. Mental energy is highest at the start of practice; spend it on the toughest bars.4. Practise from any starting point. Open the score randomly; you should be able to start there from memory. This builds resilience for performance anxiety.5. Mental practice. Without the violin, mentally walk through the piece — every shift, every bow direction, every fingering. 10 minutes a day. Increases memory reliability dramatically.6. Slow performance practice. Once memorised, play the whole piece at 60% tempo from memory. Catches weak spots.

> Where I got stuck: I memorised by repetition only — fingers learned the piece, but my brain didn't. Under stress (an audition), my fingers blanked. Fix: 10 minutes of mental practice daily. It feels useless at first, but after a month, memory failure rate dropped 80%.

Chapter 1 — Double stops: the violin singing in two voices

A double stop is two strings bowed simultaneously, each producing a different pitch. The bow must contact both strings at once.

Bow angle for double stops

The bow normally tilts to one side to contact one string. For double stops, the bow lies flat on both strings at the same angle. Bow arm height adjusts to suit.

Easy double stops: open string + stopped string

Examples:- Open D + 3rd finger A on D = A4 + D5 (a 4th)- Open A + 1st finger B on E = B5 + open A (depends; usually combined notes)

These are the entry-level double stops; you've been hearing them in pieces since Grade 3 (as the drone effect).

Stopped double stops: 3rds

Two fingers on adjacent strings, both stopped. For a 3rd in 1st position:

To get a 3rd, you need adjacent fingers giving notes 3 letters apart. In 3rd position on A + E:

The Grade 6 syllabus typically asks for a one-octave scale in 3rds in keys like G major.

6ths

Two-finger spread; the two fingers are usually on different strings, one slightly behind the other.

> Where I got stuck: my fingers couldn't independently stop adjacent strings at different positions. Fix: very slow practice with a tuner on each note separately, then together. 5 minutes per day, 6 weeks, before any scale in 3rds was reliable.

Chapter 2 — Chromatic scale

12 semitones in one octave, played either with traditional fingering or sliding-finger chromatic.

Traditional fingering (G chromatic ascending, one octave on G + D string)

The same finger plays two consecutive semitones by sliding (1 plays G♯ then A; 2 plays A♯ then B). The finger slides up about ½ a finger-width.

Galamian's chromatic fingering

Uses a different finger pattern that avoids slides. Faster but harder to learn.

> Trick: the slide-finger chromatic is more idiomatic and what most pieces expect. Practise it very slowly with metronome. The "click" of the finger sliding between two semitones should be inaudible — the bow disguises it.

Chapter 3 — Flying staccato (staccato volante)

A series of bouncing staccato notes all on one bow direction, the bow lifting between each.

Distinct from slurred staccato (which stays on the string). Flying staccato lifts.

How to learn it

1. Solid up-bow staccato first (easier direction). 4 notes per bow at slow tempo.2. Each note: bow stops and lifts a millimetre, then drops for the next note.3. Build to 8 notes per bow.4. Reverse to down-bow staccato.

Used famously in: Sarasate Zigeunerweisen, Wieniawski concertos, encore showpieces.

> Trick: the wrist + fingers produce the "stop+lift" motion; the arm stays steady. If your arm is jerking, you're forcing it. Relax, slow down.

Chapter 4 — Higher positions: 6th, 7th, half position

Half position

The hand sits below 1st position — 1st finger plays where the open string normally sounds (or a semitone above). Used for low chromatic passages.

6th and 7th positions

6th pos on A: 1=G♯5.7th pos on A: 1=A5 (an octave above open A).

In 7th position, the hand sits over the body of the violin. Thumb adjusts to a different position; left hand has limited mobility. Used mostly for very high passages in concertos and showpieces.

> Where I got stuck: my left wrist collapsed in 7th position — the angle felt wrong. Fix: explicitly lift the left elbow forward (more underneath the violin) when shifting above 5th. The hand pivots more from underneath.

Chapter 5 — Ornaments executed idiomatically

Grade 6 demands playing, not just identifying, ornaments.

Trill (tr or ⟵⟶)

Rapid alternation between the written note and the note a step above. In Baroque: starts on the upper note. In Classical/Romantic: starts on the written note.

The 2nd finger trills (lift-drop) while bow sustains. Trill speed: 6–10 oscillations per second. Practise slowly first, build speed.

Mordent (𝆚)

Quick three-note ornament: written note → step below (or above) → written note.

Turn (∾)

Four-note ornament: above-note → written → below-note → written.

Appoggiatura (small note before main note)

Takes half the value of the following note in Classical music.

Acciaccatura (small note with slash through stem)

A "crush" note — played as fast as possible, then immediately to the main note.

Chapter 1 — Vibrato: the expression

Vibrato is the small, rapid pitch oscillation that adds warmth and life to sustained notes. Without it, classical violin tone sounds naked.

Three vibrato types

| Type | Source | Speed | When ||------|--------|-------|------|| Wrist vibrato | Wrist rocks back and forth | Medium | Most repertoire || Arm vibrato | Whole forearm rocks | Slow + wide | Romantic, big tone || Finger vibrato | Finger joint flexes | Fast + narrow | Rare; modern works |

Wrist is the standard first vibrato taught at Grade 5.

Learning wrist vibrato — step by step

1. Air vibrato — without violin. Hold left arm up as if holding violin. Wave hand at the wrist, palm-up to palm-down motion, for 30 sec. This is the motion.2. Vibrato on knee. Sit. Place finger on knee. Rock wrist gently, finger stays in contact, pad rolls. Build motor pattern.3. Vibrato on violin neck (no string). Finger on the neck, not string. Rock wrist.4. Vibrato on string with finger anchored. 3rd finger on A (= D5). Bow long open D. Rock wrist; finger should roll (not slide). Pitch should wobble below the pitch and back to it.5. Slow metronome vibrato. 4 wobbles per beat at 60 bpm = 4 Hz. Build to 6 Hz over 6 months.6. Apply to scales. Vibrate every sustained note in a slow scale.

> Where I got stuck: my wrist locked. Vibrato felt impossible for 3 months. Fix: spend 5 minutes per day on the "knee drill" — no violin, no string, just the wrist motion. The brain learns motion separately from instrument. Once the wrist could rock freely, transferring to the violin took one week.

Pitch direction

Vibrato should wobble below the in-tune pitch back up to it. Never above. The brain hears the upper limit of the oscillation as "the note".

Where to start using it

Don't vibrate every note. Start with:- Long notes (half-notes, whole-notes).- Phrase peak notes.- Final note of a phrase.

By Grade 6, you'll vibrate almost every note that's longer than a quaver. At Grade 5, just the obvious ones.

Chapter 2 — Shifting to 4th and 5th positions

4th position

On the A string: 1=E5, 2=F♯5, 3=G♯5, 4=A5.

Note: 4 in 4th position = A5 = open E next door. Useful for staying on one string.

5th position

1=F♯5, 2=G♯5, 3=A5, 4=B5.

5th position is where the violin's neck meets the body. The thumb position changes — you'll start using the "shoulder" of the violin as a reference.

The shift map (3-octave G major)

To play G major across 3 octaves, you traverse:- G string: 1st position G-A-B-C- D string: 1st pos D-E-F♯-G- A string: 1st pos A-B-C♯-D- E string: 1st pos E-F♯-G- Shift to 3rd pos on E: A-B-C- Shift to 5th pos on E: D-E- (or shift higher: 7th position D-E-F♯-G — depends on edition)

> Trick: learn the shift route as a fixed sequence. Mark "III" or "V" above the music. Practise just the route (no rhythm) until the shifts are automatic; then add timing.

Silent shift drill (essential at Grade 5+)

1. Bow open A.2. Slide 1st finger up to D5 (3rd pos).3. Continue sliding to F♯5 (5th pos).4. Back down to D5, then to 1st position B.5. Repeat 10×, very slowly. No glissando audible.

Daily. The shifts smoothen over weeks.

Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine

3rd position — the workhorse

3rd position is the second most used position after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd.

Finding 3rd position

On the A string:1. Play open A.2. Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).3. Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5.

Other strings analogous:- G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.- D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.- E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D.

The shift motion

Three components, in this order:1. Release finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).2. Thumb and hand travel together up the neck.3. Press the new finger at the target.

The finger that leads the shift is called the guide finger. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1.

> Trick: practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a "scrape", the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide.

2nd position

Less common than 3rd. The hand sits halfway between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F.

2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits just above 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches.

How to choose position

| Reason | Position to use ||--------|------------------|| Reach high notes | Higher position || Stay on same string (tone colour) | Whatever position keeps you there || Avoid awkward string crossing | The position that smooths the line || Score marks it | Composer chose; obey || Difficult fingering pattern | Try 2nd position |

D melodic minor (one or two octaves)

Ascending: D E F G A B C♯ D — raise 6 (B) and 7 (C♯).Descending: D C B♭ A G F E D — back to natural.

Fingering (one octave on D + A):

That B♭ on descent / B♮ on ascent on the same string is the tricky part. Same finger (1), different position.

> Where I got stuck: my hand could not switch B♮ ↔ B♭ in one scale. Fix: bow only the A string portion (B♭ down, B♮ up) until the finger shape change became automatic. Don't add the rest of the scale until that bar is reliable.

Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce

Spiccato is the foundation of all advanced bowing. The bow lifts off the string between notes — not because you lifted it, but because the bow's elasticity bounces it.

How spiccato works

The bow is flexible. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the "sweet spot" (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note.

Spiccato practice progression

1. Drop test. Hold the bow over the A string, let it fall from 1 cm above, watch it bounce. Find the bow position where bounce is most controlled (usually 4–6 inches above the middle, near the balance point).2. One bounce per drop. Drop, lift, drop. Slow.3. Two bounces per drop without re-lifting. The bow bounces itself.4. Continuous bouncing on open string at metronome 60.5. Add scale notes. D major scale, spiccato.6. Speed up gradually to 100, then 120.

> Trick: spiccato fails when you grip the bow. Relax thumb + pinky; let the bow do the work. If your wrist hurts after spiccato practice, you're forcing it. Stop, shake out, restart looser.

Détaché vs spiccato vs staccato — quick distinction

| Bowing | Bow contact | Sound ||--------|-------------|-------|| Détaché | On string | Smooth separation || Spiccato | Bounces off | Light, dancing || Staccato | On string, sharp stop | Short, dry || Slurred staccato | On string, multiple stops one direction | Pulsed long line |

Chapter 4 — New keys: E major, A♭ major

E major (4 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯)

Fingering: normal "high" positions everywhere. Coming after the flat keys, E major feels easy because the violin's open strings (E, A, D) are all natural to the key.

A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭)

The hardest playable key at Grade 4. Two "low" fingers on most strings; A♭ itself (instead of open A) creates an unusual hand position.

Most editions require A♭ for one octave only at Grade 4. Two-octave A♭ is Grade 5+.

> Trick: don't memorise A♭ note-by-note. Memorise it as a modified D major shape, with low 1 on the D string and a low everywhere else. Pattern recognition over note recall.

Chapter 5 — All four cadences (theory)

| Cadence | Chords | Sound | Example ||---------|--------|-------|---------|| Perfect (Authentic) | V → I | "Full stop" | End of Twinkle || Plagal ("Amen") | IV → I | "Settled, churchy" | "Amen" at hymn end || Imperfect (Half) | any → V | "Hanging question" | Mid-phrase pause || Interrupted | V → vi (instead of I) | "Surprise" | Romantic deception |

Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves)

B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering:

Wait — that's three "low" fingers across the scale. The hand shape changes per string.

> Trick: the rule for any key with flats: drop the finger that plays the flatted note half a finger-width toward the nut. For B♭ major, that's 2 on G string, 1 on D, 1 on A. Memorise the pattern of which finger is low, not the abstract scale.

Top note: B♭5 (4th finger on E in 1st position).

Why this key matters

B♭ is the first key violinists genuinely struggle with. The hand has to relearn its default spacing. Master B♭ by Grade 3, and every flat key after becomes intuitive.

Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave)

E♭ has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. One octave only at Grade 3.

Different editions fingerprint this differently. The point: 3 flats means 3 "low" finger placements. Get the pattern of lowness right and your fingers will land.

> Where I got stuck: I tried to memorise E♭ as "B♭, E♭, A♭" abstractly. Fix: I stopped naming the flats and just thought "low-low-low" in the right places. Faster, more reliable.

Chapter 3 — The three minor forms

Grade 2 introduced natural minor. Grade 3 adds harmonic and (sometimes) melodic.

Natural minor (review)

Same notes as the relative major. A natural minor:

Harmonic minor — raises the 7th

The leading-tone effect that classical music expects:

That raised 7th creates an augmented 2nd between F and G♯ (3 semitones — a full step + half-step). This gives harmonic minor its Middle-Eastern or Spanish flavour.

> Trick: harmonic minor sounds exotic compared to natural minor. Sing both ascending — natural feels "modal/folk", harmonic feels "classical/dramatic". After ten A/B comparisons, your ear locks the difference.

Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down

Two different note sets in one scale. Composers chose this in the Baroque to handle melodic lines without the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd "bump".

Grade 3 typically requires melodic minor only on paper; full playing comes Grade 4.

Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato

A new bowing. Notation: slur (curved line) + dot under each note within the slur.

The bow stays on the string; the wrist makes a small stop-start motion for each note while moving in one direction.

> Where I got stuck: I lifted the bow between notes — that's staccato volante (flying staccato), Grade 6 stuff. Fix: practise on open strings, bow stays on the string, only the wrist pulses. Picture a bow that "hesitates" 4 times in one stroke.

Hooked bowing

Two unequal notes (often dotted-quaver + semiquaver) on one bow with a tiny stop between them:

Used to keep the dotted figure on the same bow direction throughout a phrase. Saves bow-direction confusion.

Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview

True shifting (moving the hand to 2nd/3rd/etc. positions) is Grade 4 territory, but Grade 3 introduces the concept and may use one shift in a piece or as a "step out" of 1st position.

Why shift?

1. Reach higher notes the 1st position can't.2. Stay on one string for a phrase (changing tone colour).3. Make awkward fingerings easier.

What 3rd position feels like

In 3rd position, the 1st finger plays what the 3rd finger plays in 1st position. So on the A string:

- 1st position: 3rd finger = D5.- 3rd position: 1st finger = D5.

The hand moves up the neck by about 1 inch.

First shift exercise

On the A string:1. Open A, then 3rd finger D (1st position).2. Slide the 1st finger up the string until it sounds D.3. Now you're in 3rd position. Play 2 (E) and 3 (F♯).

Don't lift; slide smoothly. The thumb travels with the hand.

> Trick: the thumb moves at the same time as the fingers. If the thumb stays back, you get a stretched, painful hand. Practise the shift with eyes closed, only feeling the smooth slide. Listen for the slide pitch — at first it'll glissando, later it'll be silent.

Grade 3 may include one piece with a marked shift; Grade 4 requires the technique.

C major (one octave) — first piece with no sharps

Chapter 4 — Longer slurs and bow planning

Grade 1 slurs were 2–4 notes. Grade 2 introduces 6, 8, even a full scale (8 notes) on one bow.

The bow-budget rule

Total bow length = 25 inches (varies by player + bow).For 8 notes in one bow: 25 ÷ 8 ≈ 3 inches per note.

You cannot play loud + slow + long-slurred. Pick two. Grade 2 long slurs are usually mezzo-piano and steady — the bow conserves itself.

Slur with string crossing

Example: slur "open A → 1st finger B → open E" in one bow. Two string changes inside one stroke. The right wrist + elbow tilt to roll between strings without breaking the sound.

> Where I got stuck: every slur with a string crossing scratched on the crossing point. Fix: slow practice with the metronome at 40 bpm, paying attention to the angle of the bow hair at the crossing. The bow should tilt gradually, not snap. Drill: 5 minutes a day for 2 weeks → solved.

Practice progression

1. 2-note slur, same string.2. 2-note slur, crossing strings.3. 4-note slur, same string.4. 4-note slur, crossing strings.5. 8-note slur = full scale.

Don't jump steps. Each stage takes about a week.

Chapter 1 — 1st Position fingering

What "1st position" means

The position number tells you where the first finger sits on the fingerboard. In 1st position, the index finger sits just below the nut — about a whole tone above the open string. From there, fingers 2, 3, 4 extend up the fingerboard, each roughly a whole tone apart (with one half-step adjustment per scale).

The diagram shows the A/D major pattern: whole-step between every finger. In other keys, the half-step location shifts (Chapter 3).

Finger numbering

| Finger | Number ||--------|--------|| Index | 1 || Middle | 2 || Ring | 3 || Little (pinky) | 4 || (Thumb supports underneath, no number) | — |

This is opposite to piano — there, the thumb is 1. On the violin, the thumb has no number because it never stops a string; its job is to brace the neck.

> Trick: coming from piano, I kept calling my thumb "finger 1" and reaching with my index expecting it to be "2". The fix: every Grade 1 piece marks "1" above the index. Trust the printed number, not your old habit.

Placing fingers cleanly

1. Fingertip down, not the pad — the smaller contact area produces a cleaner stop.2. Curved finger — knuckles bent.3. Just enough pressure to stop the string vibrating above the finger. Too much pressure = sharp pitch + finger fatigue.4. Drop and lift — don't slide. Vertical motion only.

> Where I got stuck: I pressed every finger too hard for two months. My left hand cramped after 10 minutes. The fix: deliberately under-press until the note buzzes; then add the minimum extra pressure to stop the buzz. That's the right amount. Most students press 2-3× more than needed.

Chapter 2 — Tuning revisited (the violinist's ear)

You learnt to tune in Post 00. Grade 1 adds double-stop tuning — bow two adjacent open strings together and listen for the perfect 5th.

How a perfect 5th sounds

When two strings tuned a perfect 5th apart sound together, the combined tone is smooth and ringing. When slightly off, you hear beats — a wobbling, rough pulse like an out-of-sync washing machine.

To tune by 5ths:1. Tune A to A=440 with a tuner.2. Bow A and D together gently. Adjust D's fine tuner until beats vanish.3. Bow D and G together. Adjust G.4. Bow A and E together. Adjust E.

> Trick: sit in a quiet room. Beats are easier to hear without background noise. After 6 months of tuning by 5ths, you'll do it automatically — and you'll have built a perfect-5th template in your ear that helps every interval recognition exercise.

Chapter 3 — The three Grade 1 scales

G major (one octave, G string + D string)

Open G → 1st finger A → 2nd finger B → 3rd finger C → open D → 1st finger E → 2nd finger F♯ → 3rd finger G.

Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 on each string. (B→C and F♯→G are half-steps.)

D major (one octave, D string + A string)

Open D → 1 E → 2 F♯ → 3 G → open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D.

Half-step location: between finger 2 and finger 3 again.

A major (one octave, A string + E string)

Open A → 1 B → 2 C♯ → 3 D → open E → 1 F♯ → 2 G♯ → 3 A.

Same half-step pattern.

> Trick: G, D, A majors all use the same finger pattern in 1st position: 0-1-2-3 with the half-step between 2 and 3 on each string. Memorise one hand shape; transfer to all three keys. This is the "violin equivalent" of the piano's W-W-H-W-W-W-H — but expressed as finger placement.

Arpeggios (Grade 1)

The tonic triad spelled out: root, third, fifth, octave.

Practise scales and arpeggios in pairs: G major scale → G major arpeggio → G again. Builds the key-feel.

Chapter 4 — Bowing: détaché and slur

Détaché

"Detached" — each note gets its own bow stroke (down or up). The bow doesn't lift between notes; it changes direction on the string.

In Grade 1 you play almost everything détaché. Goal: smooth, even tone with no scratching at the bow direction change. The wrist makes a tiny "give" at each change, like a pendulum bottom.

Slur

A curved line over two or more notes means play them in one bow stroke (one direction, no change). Same hair, multiple finger changes.

> Where I got stuck: I scratched every bow change for the first 2 months. Fix: practise the bow change motion itself without a string — just air bow with the empty bow, smoothly reversing direction. The wrist learns to soften the change. Then add the string back.

Bow distribution

On a slur of 4 notes, you have to "ration" the bow length so you don't run out. Roughly: divide the bow into 4 equal parts; one part per note.

Practice: slur 2 notes per bow, then 4, then 8. By Grade 3 you'll be slurring entire scales in one bow.

Chapter 5 — Reading music for violin

Violin reads in treble clef only (unlike the piano, which uses both). Range covers from open G (below the staff, on a ledger line) up to many ledger lines above. By Grade 1 you read up to about A or B above the staff (3rd finger on E string).

Notes you'll meet in Grade 1

How to study a piece

1. Listen to a recording 3 times before touching the violin.2. Sight-read pizzicato (pluck the strings) — checks left-hand alone before the bow complicates it.3. > Trick: always say note names aloud as you play during slow practice. The voice + hand link locks the note-name-to-pitch connection. Later you'll do this silently, but the verbal pass is the cement.

Chapter 7 — Intonation: the violin's hardest problem

Unlike piano (perfect pitch every key), violin pitch depends entirely on where your finger lands. Even 1 millimetre off = audibly out of tune.

How to develop intonation

1. Listen to open strings constantly. Cross-check stopped notes against open strings of the same name.2. Drone practice. Bow an open string, then play a scale on the next string. The open string is your reference; stopped notes that match the harmonic ring are in tune.3. Use ear-training apps. "Tonal Energy", "Functional Ear Trainer".4. Sing the note first, then play it. Singing pre-tunes your hand.

> Where I got stuck: I couldn't tell if I was sharp or flat — everything sounded "kind of right". Fix: bow the open A while pressing 3rd finger on D (which should match A). If the two pitches fight — beats appear — I was off. If they fuse into one ringing tone — in tune. After a month of drone practice, my finger landed correctly without conscious thought.

Tape on the fingerboard (controversial but works)

Many teachers put small pieces of tape across the fingerboard at the 1st, 3rd, and 4th finger positions for absolute beginners. Provides a visual guide for finger placement.

Pros: speeds up first 3 months; reduces frustration.Cons: trains eyes-on-fingerboard habit; must be removed by Grade 2 or it becomes a crutch.

Use it if you're self-teaching and frustrated. Remove it as soon as you can hit the notes without looking. By Grade 1 exam, no tape.

Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills

ABRSM Grade 1 violin aural is identical to piano aural at Grade 1:

- Clap back a 2-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4.- Sing back a 3-4 note phrase.- Identify the time signature by listening to a tune.

Violin-specific aural exercise

- Match pitch — examiner plays a note on the piano; you play it on the violin. Tests pitch perception + finger placement under pressure.- Tuning judgment — examiner plays two notes; you say which is higher or whether they're the same.

> Trick: practise pitch matching daily for 60 seconds. Pick a random note on a piano (or use a tuning app), then find it on your violin. By Grade 1 exam you should hit within a quarter-tone first try.

Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now)

The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is unfretted — you place your finger somewhere and the precision of placement decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise.

In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match.

> Where I started: I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point.

Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument

Size matters first

Violins come in fractional sizes for children: 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 4/4 (full). Adults use 4/4 unless they have unusually short arms. Measure from neck-base to the centre of the palm with arm extended:

| Arm length (neck to palm) | Size ||---------------------------|------|| < 36 cm | 1/8 || 36–43 cm | 1/4 || 43–48 cm | 1/2 || 48–55 cm | 3/4 || ≥ 55 cm | 4/4 (full) |

What to buy as an adult beginner

A "beginner outfit" includes: violin, bow, case, rosin, shoulder rest. Brands in the $200-$500 range that don't disappoint:

- Yamaha V3, V5, V7 — reliable workhorse, well-set-up.- Stentor Student II, Conservatoire — UK-popular student line.- Cremona SV-175, SV-500 — entry-level, decent set-up.- Eastman VL80, VL100 — Chinese workshop, often resold by reputable shops.

Avoid $50-$100 Amazon "VSO" (Violin-Shaped Object) instruments — they're often un-tuneable, with cheap pegs that slip and bridges that are wrong.

Chapter 3 — The instrument anatomy

Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these):

- Scroll, pegs, peg box — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs- Nut — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard- Fingerboard — the long black wooden strip your fingers press- Bridge — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration- F-holes — the two carved openings; let sound out- Soundpost — small wooden dowel inside the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.- Bass bar — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.- Tailpiece — anchors the strings at the bottom- Fine tuners — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)- Chinrest — where your chin rests- Shoulder rest — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder

Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin

Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years.

Standing

1. Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.2. Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).3. Head facing forward, then turn it slightly left to look down the fingerboard.4. Place the violin on your left collarbone, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.5. The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — don't grip with your jaw. The shoulder rest does the work.

> Where I got stuck: I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest higher until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is supported, not gripped. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit.

Sitting

Same upper-body posture. Sit on the front half of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch.

Left-hand position

- Thumb rests on the side of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.- Fingers curve over the strings.- Wrist straight — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.- A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger.

Common mistakes:- Wrist collapsed under the neck (the "frying pan grip") — kills agility.- Thumb wrapped over the top of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.- Fingers flat on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down.

Chapter 5 — The bow

Anatomy of the bow

- Stick — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)- Hair — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings- Frog — black bottom piece where the screw lives- Screw / button — adjusts hair tension- Tip / point — top end- Grip / wrap — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog

Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life)

1. Pick the bow up at the frog with your right hand.2. Thumb bent, tip placed in the notch between the frog and the wrap.3. Middle finger opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.4. Ring finger rests on the side of the frog.5. Pinky curved on top of the stick, near the screw.6. Index finger wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight.

All fingers curved, never locked straight. The grip is light — too tight and the bow can't move.

> Trick: practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — then pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows.

Tightening the bow

Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a standard pencil fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings.

Always loosen after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber.

Rosin

Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times before every session. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone.

Chapter 6 — Tuning

The four strings, low to high: G — D — A — E. Each interval is a perfect 5th (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5.

With a clip-on tuner or app

1. Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.2. Pluck or bow each string one at a time.3. Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise.

Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments)

Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing into the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Push-and-turn together.

- Turn away from you (top of peg moves forward) = string winds up = pitch rises.- Turn toward you = pitch falls.

Small turns. Pegs are sensitive. Tune the G first, then D, A, E.

Fine tuning (small adjustments)

The little screws on the tailpiece. Turn clockwise to raise pitch, counter-clockwise to lower. Use these for daily small corrections (< quarter-tone).

> Where I got stuck: I terrified myself snapping a string in week one. Fix: tune slowly and small. If a string is way off, raise it gradually, not in one big peg crank. Snapped strings are normal — buy a backup E string ($5) and stop being afraid.

Tuning A first (orchestral convention)

In orchestral settings, tune A first (because the oboe gives an A). Then tune D, G, E by listening for perfect fifths with the open A. This is your first ear-training exercise — when the two strings are exactly a 5th apart, the combined sound is clean. When slightly off, you hear "beats" — a wobbling pulse.

Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing

Before fingers go on strings, master pulling a clean tone on each open string.

The straight bow

The bow must move perpendicular to the strings (parallel to the bridge). If it angles toward the fingerboard or the bridge, the contact point changes and the tone breaks up.

Practice exercise:1. Place the bow flat on the A string, about halfway between bridge and fingerboard.2. Slowly pull a downstroke (bow moving from frog toward tip). Count "1-2-3-4".3. Pause. Reset. Upstroke (tip toward frog). Same count.4. Watch the bow in a mirror — is it staying parallel to the bridge?

Contact point

Three knobs control your sound:- Weight — how much the bow presses down (mostly from the index finger via the stick)- Speed — how fast the bow moves- Contact point — distance from the bridge (closer to bridge = louder + sharper; closer to fingerboard = softer + warmer)

Balance these three. A faster bow needs more weight to maintain pressure. A bow close to the bridge needs slower speed to avoid scratching. The "sweet spot" for a clean, warm tone is about 1.5–2 cm from the bridge for a normal mezzo-forte dynamic.

> Analogy: think of the bow like painting. Speed is how fast you move the brush. Weight is how hard you press. Contact point is which part of the paper you're stroking. Change any one and the paint stroke changes. Beginners change all three accidentally and produce a mess. Master one knob at a time.

Three first exercises

Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string

Whole bow length, 8 beats per stroke. Down-up-down-up on each string in turn: G, D, A, E. Listen for a clean, sustained tone with no scratches or wobbles. Five minutes daily.

Exercise 2 — String crossings

Bow A for one beat, then D for one beat. The forearm rotates from elbow; wrist stays neutral. Do A-D-A-D-A-D, then D-A-D-A. Then add G-D and A-E. Tests bow-arm coordination without fingers complicating things.

Exercise 3 — "Hot cross buns" (open strings only)

The melody fits perfectly on three open strings if you treat it as A-D-A-D-G-D-A. Just bow each string in the rhythm of the tune. You'll feel embarrassed playing nursery rhymes — and then proud, because you sound musical.

Chapter 8 — How to practise

The single most important rule: slow and small reps beat fast big ones. Five minutes of focused open-string bowing teaches more than thirty minutes of trying to play melodies you can't yet.

Daily routine, 25 minutes

| Block | Time | What ||-------|------|------|| Tune | 2 min | Tuner first, then check by ear || Bow hold check | 2 min | Hold + release the bow on a pencil 5 times || Open-string long bows | 8 min | All 4 strings, full bow, 8 beats per stroke || String crossings | 5 min | A-D, D-A, G-D, A-E || Exercise piece | 5 min | One short pattern (open strings only this month) || Cool-down | 3 min | Play any open string slowly, listen to the tone, end on a clear sound |

Slow practice rules

1. No scratch, no rush. If the tone is bad, slow down.2. One thing at a time. Bow speed or bow weight or contact point — not all three at once.3. Mirror. Use a mirror or phone front-camera for 5 min per session for the first 3 months. The eye catches what the hand doesn't.4. Stop before fatigue. Tired = tense = bad habits. Better to stop at 20 minutes feeling fresh than 45 minutes feeling done.

> Trick: after every session, record 30 seconds on your phone. Open string, long bow, full attention. Listen back. Week 1 will sound rough. Week 8 will sound transformed. The recording is your honest progress meter when your ears in the moment lie.

Chapter 1 — Keys Up to ±4 Sharps/Flats

Adding E major (4 sharps) and A♭ major (4 flats) to the Grade 3 list.

| Key | Sharps/Flats | Relative minor ||-----|--------------|----------------|| C | — | A m || G | F♯ | E m || D | F♯ C♯ | B m || A | F♯ C♯ G♯ | F♯ m || E | F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ | C♯ m || F | B♭ | D m || B♭ | B♭ E♭ | G m || E♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ | C m || A♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ | F m |

That's 9 major keys + 9 minor keys = 18 to know fluently by Grade 4.

> Trick: the circle of fifths is your best friend. Memorise the order clockwise from C: G D A E (each adds a sharp), counter-clockwise: F B♭ E♭ A♭ (each adds a flat). The relative minor lives 3 semitones below — so the same circle holds the minors too, offset by a third.

A small mental tool: "key family"

Group the Grade 4 keys into three families by sharps/flats. When you can derive any scale within a family fluently, the family acts as a single unit:

- Sharp family: G, D, A, E.- Flat family: F, B♭, E♭, A♭.- Neutral: C.

Each family has the same major-key triad pattern (M m m M M m d°). Practise one family per week; rotate; by month four you own all 9 keys.

Chapter 2 — Melodic Minor Finalised

Reminder from Grade 3: three minor flavours sharing the same key signature.

Melodic minor is the one Grade 4 wants from you in writing and playing.

Ascending raises the 6 and 7. Descending reverts to natural (lowers the 6 and 7 back). It's the only scale in Western theory that's deliberately asymmetric between up and down.

> Where I got stuck: I kept playing the ascending shape on the way down too. Fix: in slow practice, name the sharps aloud as you ascend ("F-sharp, G-sharp"), and name the naturals aloud as you descend ("G-natural, F-natural"). The voice catches the descent change before the fingers do.

Writing a melodic minor in any key

1. Write the natural minor (the key signature's normal scale).2. Mark the 6th and 7th degrees.3. Ascending: raise both with an accidental (♯ or ♮ depending on whether the natural is flat).4. Descending: revert — write naturals/flats explicitly to cancel the ascending raises.

Chapter 3 — All Interval Qualities

Grade 4 expands beyond the tritone to every augmented and diminished interval.

The full table (above C, ascending)

| Interval | Quality + Number | Semitones ||----------|-------------------|-----------|| C–C | Perfect unison (P1) | 0 || C–D♭ | Minor 2nd (m2) | 1 || C–D | Major 2nd (M2) | 2 || C–D♯ | Augmented 2nd (A2) | 3 || C–E♭ | Minor 3rd (m3) | 3 || C–E | Major 3rd (M3) | 4 || C–F♭ | Diminished 4th (d4) | 4 || C–F | Perfect 4th (P4) | 5 || C–F♯ | Augmented 4th (A4) | 6 || C–G♭ | Diminished 5th (d5) | 6 || C–G | Perfect 5th (P5) | 7 || C–G♯ | Augmented 5th (A5) | 8 || C–A♭ | Minor 6th (m6) | 8 || C–A | Major 6th (M6) | 9 || C–A♯ | Augmented 6th (A6) | 10 || C–B♭ | Minor 7th (m7) | 10 || C–B | Major 7th (M7) | 11 || C–C (oct) | Perfect 8th (P8) | 12 |

Augmented vs Diminished — the rule

- Augmented = one semitone larger than the major or perfect.- Diminished = one semitone smaller than the minor or perfect.

| Direction | From major | From minor | From perfect ||-----------|------------|------------|--------------|| Larger | Augmented | Major | Augmented || Smaller | Minor | Diminished | Diminished |

> Trick: the qualities form a ladder:>>>> One step "up" the ladder = one semitone bigger. One step "down" = one semitone smaller. The 2-3-6-7 group has 4 rungs (because they have a major-minor distinction); the 1-4-5-8 group has 3 rungs (only perfect at the centre).

Counting strategy

1. Count staff positions including both endpoints → number (e.g. 4th, 5th).2. Count semitones on the keyboard → cross-reference the table → quality.

> Where I got stuck: I kept calling C–D♯ a minor 3rd. They sound identical (both 3 semitones), and on the piano they're the same key. But the staff number is different (C–D = 2nd, C–E♭ = 3rd). Always read the letter names first, count positions, then count semitones. Two questions, two answers, two checks.

Chapter 4 — Primary Triads, Reinforced

Now in all 9 major + 9 minor keys, written out:

Major-key triads pattern (always the same)

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° ||--------|---|----|-----|----|----|----|------|| Quality | M | m | m | M | M | m | dim |

Minor-key triads (harmonic minor)

| Degree | i | ii° | III+ | iv | V | VI | vii° ||--------|---|-----|------|----|----|----|------|| Quality | m | dim | Aug | m | M | M | dim |

(III+ = augmented; only applies in harmonic minor because the raised 7 alters that triad.)

> Trick: memorise the major pattern first (M m m M M m d°). Then commit just this fact about minor: the V in harmonic minor is major (because of the raised 7th). For Grade 4 written work, primary triads (i, iv, V) are what you'll be asked. i and iv are minor (because they include the natural 3rd and 5th). V is major.

Worked example: write i, iv, V in F♯ minor

F♯ minor's relative major is A (3 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯). Harmonic minor raises the 7th of natural F♯ minor → raises E to E♯.

Notice E♯ (not F natural). The spelling matters — F♯ minor uses the letters F-G-A-B-C-D-E in order, so the raised 7 is E♯ (not F natural), even though they're the same key on the piano.

Chapter 5 — Cadences

A cadence (終止式) is a two-chord progression that ends a musical phrase. The four Grade-4 cadences:

| Cadence | Roman | Sound | Where ||---------|-------|-------|-------|| Perfect (Authentic) | V → I | Full closure | End of major sections || Plagal | IV → I | "Amen" / gentler close | End of hymns, gospel || Imperfect (Half) | ? → V | Pause; more to come | Mid-phrase question || Interrupted (Deceptive) | V → vi | Surprise — expected V→I doesn't arrive | Romantic music loves this |

Writing a cadence

For Grade 4, you'll typically be asked to write two block-chord triads in root position in a given key. Voicings are open here — the syllabus doesn't yet require correct four-part voice leading.

Example — plagal cadence in G major:

> Trick: perfect cadences are the period at the end of a sentence. Plagal cadences are the "and we're done" exhale. Imperfect is the question mark mid-phrase. Interrupted is the comma-where-you-expected-a-period — the listener feels the rug pulled. Naming the emotional function makes them memorable.

Detection by ear

- Perfect ends on the tonic with a strong "thud" of finality.- Plagal ends on tonic but softer, "Amen"-shaped.- Imperfect doesn't end on tonic — it leaves you hanging on V.- Interrupted sets you up for tonic, then jolts to vi.

> Where I got stuck: I confused plagal and perfect by ear because both end on the tonic. The fix: listen to the chord before the tonic. If it's a bright, leading chord with a sharpened 7th → V → perfect. If it's a softer subdominant (IV) → plagal. The lead-in chord is the give-away.

Chapter 6 — Transposition at the Octave (with clef change)

Grade 4 asks: rewrite a short passage one octave higher or lower, possibly changing clef.

Procedure

1. Identify the target clef (if changing). Treble ↔ bass change requires repositioning every note.2. Shift every note by 7 staff positions (one octave) in the chosen direction.3. Key signature unchanged — transposing at the octave doesn't change key.4. Preserve all accidentals, dynamics, articulation, fingering.

Common confusion: clef change ≠ key change

Moving a treble passage into the bass clef changes how the same notes look, not what they sound like. Middle C is one ledger below the treble staff; the same note is one ledger above the bass staff. The actual pitch is identical; the notation rearranges.

> Where I got stuck: I miscounted octaves on bass clef constantly. The fix that stuck: sketch the grand staff at the top of every transposition exercise during practice, with Middle C clearly marked on both sides. After two weeks the eye snaps to the right ledger without thinking.

How to build a Grade 4 programme

Pick:- 1 Baroque (Bach invention)- 1 Classical (sonatina or Mozart minuet)- 1 Romantic (Schumann, Burgmüller, or Tchaikovsky)- 1 20th-century or wild card (Bartók, Heller, contemporary)

This mix is exactly what the exam syllabus expects, and it's a real musical diet — period variety is what makes a pianist, not just a key-pusher.

Chapter 9 — Voicing (the new technique)

By Grade 4 you're playing pieces where one voice (usually the soprano line, top of the RH) carries the melody while the other voices accompany. You need to make the melody louder than the inner voices, with the same hand.

Mechanics

- The melody-bearing finger uses slightly more weight and arm support.- The accompanying fingers play softer — about 1 dynamic level below.- Wrist relaxes between melodic strikes; the inner voices ride along.

Practice drill

1. Play the chord with full RH voicing at one steady volume.2. Replay, this time bringing out the top note (finger 5, usually) — exaggerate.3. Replay, this time bringing out an inner voice (finger 2 or 3).4. Replay, this time bringing out the bottom note (finger 1).5. Now play the actual piece with the top voice highlighted naturally.

The first four steps are "isolated voicing"; the fifth is "musical voicing".

> Where I got stuck: my finger 5 was too weak to project. The fix: finger 5 isolation exercises — Hanon-style 5-finger patterns where I deliberately over-strike with the pinky. Two weeks of this gave finger 5 the strength I needed. Don't skip this — finger 5 carries 80% of upper-register melodies in classical repertoire.

Chapter 10 — Aural at Grade 4

Grade 4 aural adds:

- Identify which note of a 4-note phrase has been changed in a played comparison.- Identify cadence type: perfect, imperfect, or plagal (interrupted is Grade 5).- Identify a stronger set of features: dynamics, articulation, rhythm change, key change (to relative minor only).

> Trick: for cadence ID, the question is "did it end on the tonic, and how did it get there?">> - Tonic, with a leading-7 chord before → perfect (V → I)> - Tonic, with a softer subdominant before → plagal (IV → I)> - Not tonic, hanging on the dominant → imperfect (something → V)>> Two binary tests in sequence: (a) tonic or not, (b) which chord before. Three answers cover all three cadences.

Chapter 11 — Common Grade 4 Pitfalls
