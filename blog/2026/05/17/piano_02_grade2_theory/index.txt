<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Use 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 simple time and <strong>6/8 compound time</strong> comfortably.</li>
<li>Read semiquavers and matching rests.</li>
<li>Build major scales for <strong>C, G, D, F, B♭</strong> and play them hands separate, two octaves.</li>
<li>Identify intervals <strong>by quality</strong> (major / minor / perfect) up to an octave.</li>
<li>Recognise the <strong>relative minor</strong> of those major keys.</li>
<li>Play 3–5 short pieces hands together at Grade 2 standard.</li>
<li>Understand basic articulation: legato, staccato, slurs, accents.</li>

<p><em>Trick:</em> the order of sharps you encounter is fixed: <strong>F♯ → C♯ → G♯ → D♯ → A♯ → E♯ → B♯</strong>. Order of flats reversed: <strong>B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → C♭ → F♭</strong>. Same letters, opposite ends. Mnemonics: sharps = &quot;Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle&quot;; flats = the same backwards, &quot;Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father&quot;.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Finding-the-key-from-the-signature"><a href="#Finding-the-key-from-the-signature" class="headerlink" title="Finding the key from the signature"></a>Finding the key from the signature</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sharps:</strong> the last sharp is the leading tone — go up one semitone to find the tonic.
<ul>
<li>One sharp (F♯) → up to G → <strong>G major</strong>.</li>
<li>Two sharps (F♯, C♯) → last is C♯ → up to D → <strong>D major</strong>.</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Flats:</strong> the <em>second-to-last</em> flat is the tonic.
<ul>
<li>Two flats (B♭, E♭) → second-to-last is B♭ → <strong>B♭ major</strong>.</li>
<li>(Special case: one flat alone = F major.)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> the flat-rule asks for the <strong>second-to-last</strong>, not the last. I missed this constantly. I wrote it on the cover of my notebook. After two weeks it became automatic.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Semiquavers-Sixteenth-Notes"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Semiquavers-Sixteenth-Notes" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Semiquavers (Sixteenth Notes)"></a>Chapter 2 — Semiquavers (Sixteenth Notes)</h1>
<p>A <strong>semiquaver / 十六分音符</strong> = half a quaver = quarter of a crotchet. In 4/4, four semiquavers fit in one crotchet's time.</p>

<h2 id="Beaming-groups"><a href="#Beaming-groups" class="headerlink" title="Beaming groups"></a>Beaming groups</h2>
<p>Semiquavers are usually beamed in groups of 4 (one full crotchet beat). Mixed quaver + semiquaver groupings appear too:</p>

<h2 id="Counting-semiquavers-out-loud"><a href="#Counting-semiquavers-out-loud" class="headerlink" title="Counting semiquavers out loud"></a>Counting semiquavers out loud</h2>
<p>Standard system: <strong>&quot;1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a&quot;</strong> — four syllables per crotchet beat. Each crotchet contains 4 sixteenths; each syllable maps to one sixteenth-note position.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> try saying &quot;<strong>ta-ka-di-mi</strong>&quot; instead — many drummers use it, it's faster on the tongue than &quot;1 e and a&quot;. As long as you commit to one consistent vocabulary, the brain locks onto the subdivision.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Dotted-quaver-semiquaver-pattern"><a href="#Dotted-quaver-semiquaver-pattern" class="headerlink" title="Dotted quaver + semiquaver pattern"></a>Dotted quaver + semiquaver pattern</h2>
<p>The most common Grade 2 rhythm:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">♪.  ♪       = <span class="hljs-number">3</span><span class="hljs-regexp">/16 + 1/</span><span class="hljs-number">16</span> = <span class="hljs-number">4</span>/<span class="hljs-number">16</span> = <span class="hljs-number">1</span> crotchet</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>In count words: &quot;<strong>1 . . a, 2 . . a</strong>&quot; — the dotted quaver takes 3 sixteenths, the semiquaver takes the last 1.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Compound-Time-68"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Compound-Time-68" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Compound Time (6/8)"></a>Chapter 3 — Compound Time (6/8)</h1>
<p>In <strong>6/8</strong> there are <strong>two beats per bar</strong>, and each beat is a <strong>dotted crotchet</strong> (= 3 quavers). Don't be fooled by the &quot;6&quot; — you do not count &quot;1-2-3-4-5-6&quot; evenly; you feel <strong>two macro-beats</strong>, each subdivided into three.</p>

<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">6/8 counting:   <span class="hljs-number"> 1 </span>-<span class="hljs-number"> 2 </span>-<span class="hljs-number"> 3 </span>  |  <span class="hljs-number"> 4 </span>-<span class="hljs-number"> 5 </span>- 6</span><br><span class="line">                 ^               ^</span><br><span class="line">                 strong          medium-strong</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>

</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Relative-Minor"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Relative-Minor" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Relative Minor"></a>Chapter 5 — Relative Minor</h1>
<p>Every major key has a <strong>relative minor</strong> that <em>shares its key signature</em>. The relative minor's tonic sits a <strong>minor 3rd below</strong> the major tonic (or equivalently, the 6th degree of the major scale).</p>

<p>The natural minor scale uses <em>only</em> those shared sharps/flats. (Harmonic and melodic minors add accidentals — Grade 3 material.)</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I kept confusing <strong>relative</strong> minor with <strong>parallel</strong> minor.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Relative:</strong> same <em>key signature</em>, different <em>tonic</em> (C major ↔ A minor).</li>
<li><strong>Parallel:</strong> same <em>tonic</em>, different <em>key signature</em> (C major ↔ C minor).</li>
</ul>
<p>The fix that stuck: &quot;<strong>Relative</strong>&quot; = the <em>related</em> key with the <em>same parents</em> (signature). &quot;<strong>Parallel</strong>&quot; = the <em>parallel</em> universe with the <em>same name</em> (tonic). Two distinct concepts; pin them down separately.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Touch-types-—-the-three-youll-use-most"><a href="#Touch-types-—-the-three-youll-use-most" class="headerlink" title="Touch types — the three you'll use most"></a>Touch types — the three you'll use most</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Legato</strong> — finger weight transfers from key to key without lifting. The previous finger releases as the next presses, like walking.</li>
<li><strong>Staccato</strong> — the finger bounces off the key. Wrist relaxed; energy from the fingertip, not the arm.</li>
<li><strong>Portato</strong> — between legato and staccato, marked with dots under a slur. Each note slightly separated but still &quot;singing&quot; through.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="How-to-choose"><a href="#How-to-choose" class="headerlink" title="How to choose"></a>How to choose</h2>
<p>Pick <strong>one</strong> piece that excites you (whatever the level) and <strong>one</strong> that's slightly easier than your current ability. The exciting piece pulls you forward; the easy piece is where you actually drill technique without frustration. Rotate every 4–6 weeks.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> finish every learning session by playing <strong>the easiest piece you know</strong> at performance tempo, all the way through. It costs 90 seconds and reinforces what &quot;finished&quot; feels like. The pieces you struggle with don't get this treatment — only the ones you've already mastered.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-Practice-Templates"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-Practice-Templates" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — Practice Templates"></a>Chapter 8 — Practice Templates</h1>
<h2 id="Difficult-bar-loop-10-minutes"><a href="#Difficult-bar-loop-10-minutes" class="headerlink" title="&quot;Difficult bar&quot; loop (10 minutes)"></a>&quot;Difficult bar&quot; loop (10 minutes)</h2>
<p>Pick the worst 4-bar passage in your current piece. Set metronome to <strong>half</strong> your target tempo. Play those 4 bars <strong>10 times in a row</strong> without stopping. If you fail, slow the metronome by 10 BPM and start the count again. After 10 clean reps, raise BPM by 5. Repeat until you hit target.</p>
<p>This is the single highest-leverage practice technique. Twenty minutes of this beats two hours of &quot;playing the whole piece from the top&quot;.</p>
<h2 id="Pulse-robot-warm-up-5-minutes"><a href="#Pulse-robot-warm-up-5-minutes" class="headerlink" title="&quot;Pulse robot&quot; warm-up (5 minutes)"></a>&quot;Pulse robot&quot; warm-up (5 minutes)</h2>
<p>Set the metronome to <strong>quarter = 60</strong>. Play any scale you know, <em>one note per click</em>, both hands separate. Then both hands together. Then <strong>two notes per click</strong>. Then <strong>four</strong>. The point isn't the scale — it's getting your hands locked to an external clock.</p>
<h2 id="Mock-exam-run-20-minutes-once-a-week"><a href="#Mock-exam-run-20-minutes-once-a-week" class="headerlink" title="&quot;Mock exam&quot; run (20 minutes, once a week)"></a>&quot;Mock exam&quot; run (20 minutes, once a week)</h2>
<p>Treat one full practice session per week as the exam itself. Set up your music, your phone-recorder, your metronome. Play your three current pieces back-to-back with <strong>no restarts</strong>. Then listen to the recording. Mistakes you didn't notice in the moment will jump out on playback. That's your homework for the next six days.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Aural-Skills-at-Grade-2"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Aural-Skills-at-Grade-2" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Aural Skills at Grade 2"></a>Chapter 9 — Aural Skills at Grade 2</h1>
<p>ABRSM Grade 2 aural adds:</p>
<ul>
<li>Identify whether a phrase is in 2/4, 3/4, <strong>or now 6/8</strong>.</li>
<li>Sing back a slightly longer phrase (4 notes).</li>
<li>Clap back a 4-bar rhythm.</li>
<li>Identify a feature: &quot;loud&quot;, &quot;soft&quot;, &quot;smooth&quot;, &quot;detached&quot;, &quot;ending on a different note than it started&quot; — broader description.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> to tell 6/8 from 3/4, listen for <strong>the gallop</strong>. 3/4 walks evenly in threes — ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three. 6/8 lopes — DA-da-da DA-da-da. Tap your foot. If the foot lands twice per bar, it's 6/8. If three times, it's 3/4. The foot doesn't lie.</p>
</blockquote>
