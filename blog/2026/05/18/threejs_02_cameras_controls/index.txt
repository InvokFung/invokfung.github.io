<h2 id="The-nearfar-depth-problem"><a href="#The-nearfar-depth-problem" class="headerlink" title="The near/far depth problem"></a>The near/far depth problem</h2>
<p>Depth buffer precision is <strong>non-linear</strong> — most precision is packed near the camera. A huge <code>far / near</code> ratio (e.g., 0.001 / 10000 = 10⁷) causes <strong>z-fighting</strong> (flickering coplanar surfaces).</p>
<p>Rule: keep <code>far / near &lt; ~10⁴</code>. If your scene needs both centimetre detail and kilometres of distance, use a <strong>logarithmic depth buffer</strong>: <code>new THREE.WebGLRenderer({ logarithmicDepthBuffer: true })</code>.</p>

<h2 id="Performance-tips-for-raycasting"><a href="#Performance-tips-for-raycasting" class="headerlink" title="Performance tips for raycasting"></a>Performance tips for raycasting</h2>
<ul>
<li><code>intersectObjects(...)</code> walks every triangle by default — slow with &gt; 10k tris.</li>
<li>For large scenes, use a <strong>BVH</strong> (Bounding Volume Hierarchy): the <strong>three-mesh-bvh</strong> library accelerates raycasts up to 100× and reuses the BVH per geometry.</li>
<li>For purely UI hit-tests over flat icons, raycast against a low-poly invisible proxy mesh, not the full visual model.</li>
</ul>
