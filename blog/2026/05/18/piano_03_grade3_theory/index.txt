<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Use simple time (2/4, 3/4, 4/4) and compound time (6/8, <strong>9/8, 12/8</strong>).</li>
<li>Read <strong>triplets</strong> in simple time.</li>
<li>Build <strong>natural and harmonic minor</strong> scales for relative minors of all keys covered.</li>
<li>Use major keys <strong>C, G, D, A, F, B♭, E♭</strong> and their relative minors (A m, E m, B m, F♯ m, D m, G m, C m).</li>

<li>Identify and write <strong>primary triads (I, IV, V)</strong> in root position in all the above keys.</li>

<h2 id="Reading-a-fast-key-signature"><a href="#Reading-a-fast-key-signature" class="headerlink" title="Reading a fast key signature"></a>Reading a fast key signature</h2>
<p>When you see <em><strong>three</strong> sharps</em> in the order <strong>F♯ C♯ G♯</strong>, the last is G♯ → up one semitone → <strong>A major</strong> (or its relative <strong>F♯ minor</strong>).</p>
<p>When you see <em><strong>three</strong> flats</em> in the order <strong>B♭ E♭ A♭</strong>, the second-to-last is E♭ → <strong>E♭ major</strong> (or its relative <strong>C minor</strong>).</p>

<h2 id="Harmonic-minor-和聲小調"><a href="#Harmonic-minor-和聲小調" class="headerlink" title="Harmonic minor (和聲小調)"></a>Harmonic minor (和聲小調)</h2>
<p><strong>Raise the 7th degree</strong> of the natural minor. The interval between 6 and 7 becomes an <strong>augmented 2nd</strong> (3 semitones) — gives the unmistakable &quot;exotic&quot; sound.</p>

<h2 id="Melodic-minor-旋律小調"><a href="#Melodic-minor-旋律小調" class="headerlink" title="Melodic minor (旋律小調)"></a>Melodic minor (旋律小調)</h2>
<p><strong>Ascending:</strong> raise the 6th <em>and</em> 7th. <strong>Descending:</strong> revert to natural minor.</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">melodic</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span> (up):    <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span>♯ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span>♯ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">melodic</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span> (down):  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I kept mixing harmonic and melodic.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Harmonic minor</strong> exists to fix <em>harmony</em> — it gives the V chord its major-3rd leading tone (G♯ in A minor) so the V → i cadence sounds strong. Raise the 7 only.</li>
<li><strong>Melodic minor</strong> exists to smooth <em>melody</em> — the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd (F → G♯) is awkward to sing, so going up you also raise the 6 (F → F♯) to &quot;smooth&quot; the gap. Coming down you don't need the lift, so revert.</li>
</ul>
<p>Framing them by <em>purpose</em> (harmony-fixing vs melody-smoothing) made them stop blurring.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Triplets"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Triplets" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Triplets"></a>Chapter 3 — Triplets</h1>
<p>A <strong>triplet</strong> is <strong>three notes played in the time of two</strong> of the same value. Notated with a &quot;3&quot; above or below a group:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">♪ ♪ ♪   ←<span class="hljs-number"> 3 </span>quavers in the time of<span class="hljs-number"> 2 </span>quavers =<span class="hljs-number"> 1 </span>crotchet beat</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>In 4/4, a beat that would normally hold two quavers (♪♪) is squeezed to fit three (♪♪♪). The three notes are <em>equal</em>, faster than quavers, slower than triplet semiquavers.</p>
<h2 id="Counting-triplets"><a href="#Counting-triplets" class="headerlink" title="Counting triplets"></a>Counting triplets</h2>
<p>Subdivide each affected beat into three syllables: &quot;<strong>tri-pa-let</strong>&quot; or &quot;<strong>1-trip-let, 2-trip-let</strong>&quot;. Pick one and stick with it.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> when first practising triplets, <strong>tap the beat with your foot</strong> while saying &quot;1-trip-let, 2-trip-let, 3-trip-let, 4-trip-let&quot; out loud. Foot is the beat (4 taps per bar), voice is the subdivision (12 syllables per bar). Once you can do this without the piano, the piano part is mechanical.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Duplet-—-the-inverse"><a href="#Duplet-—-the-inverse" class="headerlink" title="Duplet — the inverse"></a>Duplet — the inverse</h2>
<p>In compound time (where each beat naturally divides into 3), a <strong>duplet</strong> is two notes played in the time of three. Marked &quot;2&quot;. Less common at Grade 3 but worth knowing.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Augmented-and-Diminished-Intervals-just-the-tritone"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Augmented-and-Diminished-Intervals-just-the-tritone" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Augmented and Diminished Intervals (just the tritone)"></a>Chapter 5 — Augmented and Diminished Intervals (just the tritone)</h1>
<p>Grade 3 expands the interval qualities to include <strong>augmented</strong> and <strong>diminished</strong> — but only for the <strong>augmented 4th</strong> (A4) and <strong>diminished 5th</strong> (d5), which are the same six-semitone gap (the <strong>tritone, 三全音</strong>).</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-attribute">C</span> to F♯:    semitones = <span class="hljs-number">6</span> = augmented <span class="hljs-number">4</span>th</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-attribute">C</span> to G♭:    semitones = <span class="hljs-number">6</span> = diminished <span class="hljs-number">5</span>th</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>These two are <strong>enharmonic</strong>: same sound, different spelling. Whether you call it A4 or d5 depends on note names — count the staff positions:</p>
<ul>
<li>C → F♯: positions = 4 (C–D–E–F) → 4th → &quot;augmented&quot; (one semitone larger than P4).</li>
<li>C → G♭: positions = 5 (C–D–E–F–G) → 5th → &quot;diminished&quot; (one semitone smaller than P5).</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> a tritone divides the octave <strong>exactly in half</strong> (<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mn>12</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">/</mi><mn>2</mn><mo>=</mo><mn>6</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">12 / 2 = 6</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:1em;vertical-align:-0.25em;"></span><span class="mord">12/2</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6444em;"></span><span class="mord">6</span></span></span></span> semitones). It's the most dissonant interval in tonal music — historically called <em>diabolus in musica</em> (&quot;the devil in music&quot;) because medieval theorists found it so unstable they avoided writing it. Today it's the engine of jazz and blues. <em>Knowing the history hooks the memory.</em></p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Primary-Triads-I-IV-V"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Primary-Triads-I-IV-V" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Primary Triads (I, IV, V)"></a>Chapter 6 — Primary Triads (I, IV, V)</h1>
<p>A <strong>triad (三和弦)</strong> = three notes stacked in thirds. Root, third, fifth.</p>
<p>The <strong>primary triads</strong> of any key are built on degrees <strong>1, 4, 5</strong> — the tonic, subdominant, dominant. They handle the bulk of harmonic motion in tonal music.</p>

<h2 id="In-a-minor-key-harmonic"><a href="#In-a-minor-key-harmonic" class="headerlink" title="In a minor key (harmonic)"></a>In a minor key (harmonic)</h2>
<p>Because harmonic minor raises the 7th, the V chord becomes major (it has the raised leading tone) — even though the key is minor.</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span> (harmonic):</span><br><span class="line">  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">i</span>  (tonic)         <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span>       <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span></span><br><span class="line">  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">iv</span> (subdominant)   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span>       <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span></span><br><span class="line">  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">V</span>  (dominant)      <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span>♯ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span>      <span class="hljs-selector-tag">major</span>   ← <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span>♯ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">comes</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">from</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">the</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">harmonic</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">minor</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">raised</span> <span class="hljs-number">7</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> V in minor is <strong>major</strong>. This seems like a contradiction — until you realise it's <em>deliberate</em>: the major V supplies the leading tone that pulls back to the tonic. Without that, the V → i cadence would sound weak. Once you internalise this purpose, the rule is obvious.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Writing-triads-—-Grade-3-expectations"><a href="#Writing-triads-—-Grade-3-expectations" class="headerlink" title="Writing triads — Grade 3 expectations"></a>Writing triads — Grade 3 expectations</h2>
<p>Root position only. Bass note = root. Stack a 3rd and 5th above. No inversions yet (Grade 4-5 territory).</p>
<p>In 4-part writing (Grade 5+) you also need to know how to double notes, but Grade 3 only asks for the three-note triad spelled out.</p>
<hr>

<h2 id="Choosing-repertoire-intelligently"><a href="#Choosing-repertoire-intelligently" class="headerlink" title="Choosing repertoire intelligently"></a>Choosing repertoire intelligently</h2>
<p>By Grade 3 you should be playing <strong>three pieces simultaneously</strong> at different stages:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Mastery piece</strong> — old favourite, polished, played at performance tempo. Maintains confidence.</li>
<li><strong>Working piece</strong> — current focus, hands-together at slow tempo, gets the most minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Sight-read piece</strong> — new, untouched, played once per week as a &quot;see how far I get&quot;.</li>
</ol>
<p>The mix prevents staleness on any one and reinforces transferable skills.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> keep a small notebook of &quot;<strong>pieces I never want to forget</strong>&quot; — the mastery list. Every two weeks, revisit one of them for ten minutes. Without this, the pieces you spent six weeks learning will quietly evaporate from memory.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-10-—-Practice-Patterns-Voice-Independence"><a href="#Chapter-10-—-Practice-Patterns-Voice-Independence" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 10 — Practice Patterns: Voice Independence"></a>Chapter 10 — Practice Patterns: Voice Independence</h1>
<p>Grade 3 introduces real two-voice writing (Bach inventions). The hands aren't just playing chords against melody — both hands have <strong>independent musical lines</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="How-to-practise-two-voices"><a href="#How-to-practise-two-voices" class="headerlink" title="How to practise two voices"></a>How to practise two voices</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sing one line while playing the other.</strong> Sing the RH; play only the LH. Then swap. This forces your brain to &quot;hear&quot; each part separately.</li>
<li><strong>Practise the LH at twice the volume</strong>, RH softly. Then reverse. Trains dynamic independence.</li>
<li><strong>Slow loops on the join points.</strong> Two-voice writing has constant &quot;overlapping&quot; moments where one hand crosses or sustains while the other moves. Mark those bars and isolate them.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> in Bach's first invention I could play either hand alone — but together my brain treated it as one stream and the LH always reverted to chord accompaniment. Fix: I spent a week singing the LH while playing the RH. The shift in mental model is exactly what's required — <em>both</em> lines are melodies, not &quot;melody + accompaniment&quot;. Once the brain reframes it, the fingers follow.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-11-—-Aural-at-Grade-3"><a href="#Chapter-11-—-Aural-at-Grade-3" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 11 — Aural at Grade 3"></a>Chapter 11 — Aural at Grade 3</h1>
<p>ABRSM Grade 3 aural adds:</p>
<ul>
<li>Identify whether a piece is in <strong>major or minor key</strong>.</li>
<li>Identify whether a cadence is <strong>perfect</strong> (V → I) or <strong>imperfect</strong> (?? → V).</li>
<li>Sing back a slightly longer phrase (5–6 notes).</li>
<li>Identify two <strong>features</strong> of a played excerpt: dynamics, articulation, tempo change, tonality.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Practising-major-vs-minor-by-ear"><a href="#Practising-major-vs-minor-by-ear" class="headerlink" title="Practising major-vs-minor by ear"></a>Practising major-vs-minor by ear</h2>
<ul>
<li>Listen to one octave of a major scale (just C D E F G A B C). Hum it.</li>
<li>Now one octave of A minor (A B C D E F G A). Hum it.</li>
<li>The third note is the give-away. Major sounds &quot;open / bright&quot;. Minor sounds &quot;narrow / sad&quot;.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> the first three notes — <strong>do-re-mi</strong> vs <strong>la-ti-do</strong> — are the cleanest test. If the third note is a <strong>major third</strong> above the first, the key is major. If it's a <strong>minor third</strong> (one semitone less), the key is minor. Sing the test phrase silently in your head while listening; it'll often beat conscious analysis.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-12-—-Common-Grade-3-Pitfalls"><a href="#Chapter-12-—-Common-Grade-3-Pitfalls" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 12 — Common Grade 3 Pitfalls"></a>Chapter 12 — Common Grade 3 Pitfalls</h1>

<tr>
<th>Pitfall</th>
<th>Fix</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Memorising scales without the pattern</td>
<td>Always re-derive from W-H pattern. Memory + derivation = robust</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Stumbling on triplets</td>
<td>Foot taps the macro-beat. Voice taps the subdivision</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Forgetting V is major even in minor</td>
<td>Mark the raised leading tone with a coloured pencil for a month</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Treating Bach two-voice as melody + accompaniment</td>
<td>Sing one voice while playing the other</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Ignoring expression markings</td>
<td>Highlight all Italian terms before you play. Translate each. Then play</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Practising scales mechanically</td>
