11:T86de,<p>A mesh in Three.js is <code>geometry + material</code>. Geometry defines the shape — a list of vertex positions, normals, UVs, and how they form triangles. Material defines how each pixel of the shape is shaded — colour, roughness, light response, transparency. This post covers <code>BufferGeometry</code>, the built-in geometries, and the full material family from <code>MeshBasicMaterial</code> (no lighting at all) up to <code>MeshPhysicalMaterial</code> (full PBR with clearcoat, sheen, transmission, iridescence).</p>

<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Build a <code>BufferGeometry</code> from raw arrays of positions, normals, UVs, and indices.</li>
<li>Use the built-in geometries (Box, Sphere, Plane, Cylinder, Cone, Torus, TorusKnot, Tube, Lathe, Extrude, Text).</li>
<li>Distinguish the material families: Basic, Lambert, Phong, Standard, Physical, Toon, MatCap, Depth, Normal, Line, Sprite.</li>
<li>Understand which materials need lights and which don't.</li>
<li>Use wireframe, flat shading, double-sided rendering, and transparency.</li>
<li>Apply UV coordinates that make textures look right (Post 04 covers texture loading).</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="Subdivision-matters"><a href="#Subdivision-matters" class="headerlink" title="Subdivision matters"></a>Subdivision matters</h2>
<p>A <code>SphereGeometry(1, 8, 6)</code> is faceted; <code>SphereGeometry(1, 64, 32)</code> is smooth. Higher segments cost more vertices, more shading work, more memory. Profile in <code>renderer.info.triangles</code>.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> when modelling stylised / low-poly art, low segment counts plus <code>flatShading: true</code> on the material gives the faceted look on purpose.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="When-each-lights-up"><a href="#When-each-lights-up" class="headerlink" title="When each lights up"></a>When each lights up</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unlit family</strong> (<code>Basic</code>, <code>Normal</code>, <code>Matcap</code>, <code>Depth</code>, <code>Line</code>, <code>Points</code>, <code>Sprite</code>) ignore lights entirely — they render the same regardless of scene lighting.</li>
<li><strong>Lit family</strong> (<code>Lambert</code>, <code>Phong</code>, <code>Toon</code>, <code>Standard</code>, <code>Physical</code>) need at least one light in the scene; otherwise they appear black.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> a beginner's &quot;it's just black&quot; problem is almost always: lit material + no lights. Either add a <code>HemisphereLight</code> / <code>AmbientLight</code>, or switch the material to <code>MeshBasicMaterial</code> while debugging.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="DoubleSide-cost"><a href="#DoubleSide-cost" class="headerlink" title="DoubleSide cost"></a>DoubleSide cost</h2>
<p><code>DoubleSide</code> doubles the fragment shader work for that mesh — every triangle is rasterised twice (once per facing). Use only when needed (paper-thin objects, particle quads). For closed shapes, <code>FrontSide</code> (default) is correct and faster.</p>
<h2 id="Transparency-caveats"><a href="#Transparency-caveats" class="headerlink" title="Transparency caveats"></a>Transparency caveats</h2>
<p>Transparent objects must be <strong>sorted back-to-front</strong> for correct blending. Three.js does this per-mesh automatically (<code>renderer.sortObjects = true</code> by default). It cannot sort <strong>within</strong> a single mesh — long beard hair, foliage cards, particle clouds will exhibit artefacts. Workarounds:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong><code>alphaTest</code></strong> instead of <code>opacity</code> for binary cut-outs (no sorting needed).</li>
<li><strong>Separate meshes</strong> per leaf cluster (slow).</li>
<li><strong>Order-independent transparency</strong> (OIT) via custom shader (advanced).</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="Exercise-2-—-Compare-materials"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-Compare-materials" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — Compare materials"></a>Exercise 2 — Compare materials</h2>
<p>Same sphere, 5 copies side by side, each with a different material: Basic, Lambert, Phong, Standard, Physical (with <code>transmission: 1</code>). Observe the difference.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-3-—-Wireframe-flatShading-combo"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Wireframe-flatShading-combo" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — Wireframe + flatShading combo"></a>Exercise 3 — Wireframe + flatShading combo</h2>
<p>A low-poly icosahedron (<code>detail=0</code>) with <code>flatShading: true</code>. Overlay an <code>EdgesGeometry</code> outline. Should look like a polygonal gemstone.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Procedural-geometry"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Procedural-geometry" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Procedural geometry"></a>Exercise 4 — Procedural geometry</h2>
<p>Build a <code>BufferGeometry</code> of 100 random points (<code>Points</code> material). Animate each point's position by a sine wave per frame. (Foreshadows particle systems and shaders.)</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Disposal-drill"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Disposal-drill" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Disposal drill"></a>Exercise 5 — Disposal drill</h2>
<p>In a loop, every 2 seconds: remove the current mesh from the scene and add a new sphere with random colour. Log <code>renderer.info.memory.geometries</code> to confirm it stays low when you dispose properly.</p>
