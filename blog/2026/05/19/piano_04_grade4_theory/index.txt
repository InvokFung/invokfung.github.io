11:T94ba,<p>Grade 4 is the bridge between &quot;can read music&quot; and &quot;understands music&quot;. The syllabus expands keys to ±4 sharps/flats, finalises melodic minor, introduces all interval qualities including augmented and diminished, formalises cadences, and asks you to <strong>write</strong> primary triads in any key from the syllabus. Repertoire jumps too — full Bach inventions, longer sonatina movements, intermediate Romantic miniatures.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: 00 → 01 → 02 → 03 → <strong>04 Grade 4</strong> (this post). Grades 5–8 to follow.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>All simple and compound time signatures up to 12/8, with <strong>duplets</strong> and triplets in compound time.</li>
<li>All major and minor keys up to <strong>±4 sharps/flats</strong>.</li>
<li>Full melodic minor (ascending raised 6 + 7, descending revert).</li>
<li>Identify and write all interval qualities <strong>including augmented and diminished</strong> (not only the tritone).</li>
<li>Identify and write <strong>primary triads (I, IV, V/i, iv, V) in root position</strong> in any key from the syllabus.</li>
<li>Write a <strong>perfect, plagal, imperfect, or interrupted cadence</strong> in any of those keys.</li>
<li>Transpose a short passage <strong>up or down an octave</strong>, possibly with a clef change.</li>
<li>Recognise broader Italian, French, and German musical terms.</li>
<li>Play 3–5 Grade 4 pieces hands together at fair tempo.</li>
</ul>

<p><em>Trick:</em> the <strong>circle of fifths is your best friend</strong>. Memorise the <em>order</em> clockwise from C: <strong>G D A E</strong> (each adds a sharp), counter-clockwise: <strong>F B♭ E♭ A♭</strong> (each adds a flat). <h2 id="A-small-mental-tool-key-family"><a href="#A-small-mental-tool-key-family" class="headerlink" title="A small mental tool: &quot;key family&quot;"></a>A small mental tool: &quot;key family&quot;</h2>
<p>Group the Grade 4 keys into three families by sharps/flats. When you can derive any scale within a family fluently, the family acts as a single unit:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sharp family</strong>: G, D, A, E.</li>
<li><strong>Flat family</strong>: F, B♭, E♭, A♭.</li>
<li><strong>Neutral</strong>: C.</li>
</ul>
<p>Each family has the same <strong>major-key triad pattern</strong> (M m m M M m d°). Practise one family per week; rotate; by month four you own all 9 keys.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Melodic-Minor-Finalised"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Melodic-Minor-Finalised" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Melodic Minor Finalised"></a>Chapter 2 — Melodic Minor Finalised</h1>
<p>Reminder from Grade 3: three minor flavours sharing the same key signature.</p>
<p><strong>Melodic minor</strong> is the one Grade 4 wants from you in writing and playing.</p>

<p>Ascending raises the 6 and 7. Descending reverts to natural (lowers the 6 and 7 back). It's the only scale in Western theory that's deliberately <strong>asymmetric</strong> between up and down.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I kept playing the ascending shape on the way down too. Fix: in slow practice, <em>name the sharps aloud</em> as you ascend (&quot;F-sharp, G-sharp&quot;), and <em>name the naturals aloud</em> as you descend (&quot;G-natural, F-natural&quot;). The voice catches the descent change before the fingers do.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Writing-a-melodic-minor-in-any-key"><a href="#Writing-a-melodic-minor-in-any-key" class="headerlink" title="Writing a melodic minor in any key"></a>Writing a melodic minor in any key</h2>
<ol>
<li>Write the <strong>natural minor</strong> (the key signature's normal scale).</li>
<li>Mark the 6th and 7th degrees.</li>
<li><strong>Ascending:</strong> raise both with an accidental (♯ or ♮ depending on whether the natural is flat).</li>
<li><strong>Descending:</strong> revert — write naturals/flats explicitly to cancel the ascending raises.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="Augmented-vs-Diminished-—-the-rule"><a href="#Augmented-vs-Diminished-—-the-rule" class="headerlink" title="Augmented vs Diminished — the rule"></a>Augmented vs Diminished — the rule</h2>

<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> the qualities form a ladder:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-attribute">diminished</span> — minor — Major — Augmented        (<span class="hljs-number">2</span>, <span class="hljs-number">3</span>, <span class="hljs-number">6</span>, <span class="hljs-number">7</span>)</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-attribute">diminished</span> — Perfect — Augmented              (<span class="hljs-number">1</span>, <span class="hljs-number">4</span>, <span class="hljs-number">5</span>, <span class="hljs-number">8</span>)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>One step &quot;up&quot; the ladder = one semitone bigger. One step &quot;down&quot; = one semitone smaller. The 2-3-6-7 group has 4 rungs (because they have a major-minor distinction); the 1-4-5-8 group has 3 rungs (only perfect at the centre).</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Counting-strategy"><a href="#Counting-strategy" class="headerlink" title="Counting strategy"></a>Counting strategy</h2>
<ol>
<li>Count <strong>staff positions</strong> including both endpoints → number (e.g. 4th, 5th).</li>
<li>Count <strong>semitones</strong> on the keyboard → cross-reference the table → quality.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I kept calling C–D♯ a <em>minor 3rd</em>. They sound identical (both 3 semitones), and on the piano they're the same key. But the <em>staff number is different</em> (C–D = 2nd, C–E♭ = 3rd). Always read the <em>letter names</em> first, count positions, <em>then</em> count semitones. Two questions, two answers, two checks.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Writing-a-cadence"><a href="#Writing-a-cadence" class="headerlink" title="Writing a cadence"></a>Writing a cadence</h2>
<p>For Grade 4, you'll typically be asked to write <strong>two block-chord triads</strong> in root position in a given key. Voicings are open here — the syllabus doesn't yet require correct four-part voice leading.</p>
<p>Example — <strong>plagal cadence in G major</strong>:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-variable">IV</span> <span class="hljs-variable">chord</span> <span class="hljs-punctuation">(</span><span class="hljs-built_in">C</span> <span class="hljs-variable">major</span><span class="hljs-punctuation">)</span><span class="hljs-operator">:</span>    <span class="hljs-built_in">C</span>  <span class="hljs-built_in">E</span>  <span class="hljs-variable">G</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">I</span>  <span class="hljs-variable">chord</span> <span class="hljs-punctuation">(</span><span class="hljs-variable">G</span> <span class="hljs-variable">major</span><span class="hljs-punctuation">)</span><span class="hljs-operator">:</span>    <span class="hljs-variable">G</span>  <span class="hljs-variable">B</span>  <span class="hljs-built_in">D</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> perfect cadences are the <strong>period at the end of a sentence</strong>. Plagal cadences are the <strong>&quot;and we're done&quot; exhale</strong>. Imperfect is the <strong>question mark mid-phrase</strong>. Interrupted is the <strong>comma-where-you-expected-a-period</strong> — the listener feels the rug pulled. Naming the <em>emotional function</em> makes them memorable.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Detection-by-ear"><a href="#Detection-by-ear" class="headerlink" title="Detection by ear"></a>Detection by ear</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Perfect</strong> ends on the tonic with a strong &quot;thud&quot; of finality.</li>
<li><strong>Plagal</strong> ends on tonic but softer, &quot;Amen&quot;-shaped.</li>
<li><strong>Imperfect</strong> <em>doesn't</em> end on tonic — it leaves you hanging on V.</li>
<li><strong>Interrupted</strong> sets you up for tonic, then jolts to vi.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I confused plagal and perfect by ear because both end on the tonic. The fix: <strong>listen to the chord <em>before</em> the tonic</strong>. If it's a <em>bright, leading</em> chord with a sharpened 7th → V → perfect. If it's a <em>softer subdominant</em> (IV) → plagal. The lead-in chord is the give-away.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Transposition-at-the-Octave-with-clef-change"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Transposition-at-the-Octave-with-clef-change" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Transposition at the Octave (with clef change)"></a>Chapter 6 — Transposition at the Octave (with clef change)</h1>
<p>Grade 4 asks: rewrite a short passage <strong>one octave higher or lower</strong>, possibly <strong>changing clef</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="Procedure"><a href="#Procedure" class="headerlink" title="Procedure"></a>Procedure</h2>
<ol>
<li>Identify the target <strong>clef</strong> (if changing). Treble ↔ bass change requires repositioning every note.</li>
<li><strong>Shift every note by 7 staff positions</strong> (one octave) in the chosen direction.</li>
<li><strong>Key signature unchanged</strong> — transposing at the octave doesn't change key.</li>
<li><strong>Preserve all accidentals, dynamics, articulation, fingering</strong>.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="Common-confusion-clef-change-≠-key-change"><a href="#Common-confusion-clef-change-≠-key-change" class="headerlink" title="Common confusion: clef change ≠ key change"></a>Common confusion: clef change ≠ key change</h2>
<p>Moving a treble passage into the bass clef changes how the same notes <em>look</em>, not what they <em>sound like</em>. Middle C is one ledger below the treble staff; the same note is one ledger above the bass staff. The actual pitch is identical; the notation rearranges.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I miscounted octaves on bass clef constantly. The fix that stuck: sketch the <strong>grand staff</strong> at the top of every transposition exercise during practice, with Middle C clearly marked on both sides. After two weeks the eye snaps to the right ledger without thinking.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="How-to-build-a-Grade-4-programme"><a href="#How-to-build-a-Grade-4-programme" class="headerlink" title="How to build a Grade 4 programme"></a>How to build a Grade 4 programme</h2>
<p>Pick:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>1 Baroque</strong> (Bach invention)</li>
<li><strong>1 Classical</strong> (sonatina or Mozart minuet)</li>
<li><strong>1 Romantic</strong> (Schumann, Burgmüller, or Tchaikovsky)</li>
<li><strong>1 20th-century or wild card</strong> (Bartók, Heller, contemporary)</li>
</ul>
<p>This mix is exactly what the exam syllabus expects, and it's a real musical diet — period variety is what makes a pianist, not just a key-pusher.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Voicing-the-new-technique"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Voicing-the-new-technique" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Voicing (the new technique)"></a>Chapter 9 — Voicing (the new technique)</h1>
<p>By Grade 4 you're playing pieces where one voice (usually the soprano line, top of the RH) carries the melody while the other voices accompany. You need to make the melody <strong>louder</strong> than the inner voices, <em>with the same hand</em>.</p>
<h2 id="Mechanics"><a href="#Mechanics" class="headerlink" title="Mechanics"></a>Mechanics</h2>
<ul>
<li>The melody-bearing finger uses slightly more weight and arm support.</li>
<li>The accompanying fingers play softer — about 1 dynamic level below.</li>
<li>Wrist relaxes between melodic strikes; the inner voices ride along.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Practice-drill"><a href="#Practice-drill" class="headerlink" title="Practice drill"></a>Practice drill</h2>
<ol>
<li>Play the chord with full RH voicing at one steady volume.</li>
<li>Replay, this time bringing out the top note (finger 5, usually) — exaggerate.</li>
<li>Replay, this time bringing out an inner voice (finger 2 or 3).</li>
<li>Replay, this time bringing out the bottom note (finger 1).</li>
<li>Now play the actual piece with the top voice highlighted naturally.</li>
</ol>
<p>The first four steps are &quot;isolated voicing&quot;; the fifth is &quot;musical voicing&quot;.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> my finger 5 was too weak to project. The fix: <strong>finger 5 isolation exercises</strong> — Hanon-style 5-finger patterns where I deliberately <em>over</em>-strike with the pinky. Two weeks of this gave finger 5 the strength I needed. Don't skip this — finger 5 carries 80% of upper-register melodies in classical repertoire.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-10-—-Aural-at-Grade-4"><a href="#Chapter-10-—-Aural-at-Grade-4" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 10 — Aural at Grade 4"></a>Chapter 10 — Aural at Grade 4</h1>
<p>Grade 4 aural adds:</p>
<ul>
<li>Identify which note of a 4-note phrase has been changed in a played comparison.</li>
<li>Identify cadence type: <strong>perfect, imperfect, or plagal</strong> (interrupted is Grade 5).</li>
<li>Identify a stronger set of features: dynamics, articulation, rhythm change, key change (to relative minor only).</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> for cadence ID, the question is &quot;did it end on the tonic, and how did it get there?&quot;</p>
<ul>
<li>Tonic, with a leading-7 chord before → <strong>perfect</strong> (V → I)</li>
<li>Tonic, with a softer subdominant before → <strong>plagal</strong> (IV → I)</li>
<li><em>Not</em> tonic, hanging on the dominant → <strong>imperfect</strong> (something → V)</li>
</ul>
<p>Two binary tests in sequence: (a) tonic or not, (b) which chord before. Three answers cover all three cadences.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-11-—-Common-Grade-4-Pitfalls"><a href="#Chapter-11-—-Common-Grade-4-Pitfalls" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 11 — Common Grade 4 Pitfalls"></a>Chapter 11 — Common Grade 4 Pitfalls</h1>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Pitfall</th>
<th>Fix</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Calling A2 a m3</td>
<td>Always read <strong>staff position</strong> first, then count semitones</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Forgetting V is <strong>major</strong> in minor</td>
<td>Write the raised 7 with a coloured pencil. After 4 weeks, internalised</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Spelling triads wrong (e.g. F natural instead of E♯)</td>
<td>Stay in <strong>letter-name order</strong> — F♯ minor uses F-G-A-B-C-D-E; raised 7 is E♯, not F</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Practising all pieces at the same tempo</td>
<td>Pick the slowest one. Practise <em>everything</em> at that tempo for one full session</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Skipping aural</td>
<td>5 min daily of clap-back and sing-back. Doubles your speed at recognising rhythm and pitch</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Reading only the notes, not the markings</td>
<td>Highlight every Italian, French, German term before you play. Translate each</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
