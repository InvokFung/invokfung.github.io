Piano Notes — 01 · Grade 1 Theory & Repertoire

Created2026-05-16|Updated2026-05-16|Music
|Post View:

ABRSM Grade 1 covers the foundations every musician needs: clefs, note values, simple time signatures, the first few keys, basic intervals, and the very first scales. This post pairs the syllabus with practice pieces graded at the same level, plus the memory tricks I wish someone had handed me on day one.

Series map: 00 Beginner Basics → 01 Grade 1 (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 to follow.

Learning Outcome

  • Read fluently in treble and bass clef on the five staff lines, four spaces, and one ledger line above or below.
  • Identify all note values from semibreve to quaver, and matching rests.
  • Use 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 time signatures correctly.
  • Name notes in C, G, F majors and their key signatures.
  • Build major scales for C, G, F (one octave, hands separate).
  • Identify intervals between any two notes of the C major scale (number only).
  • Recognise the tonic, dominant, subdominant of those three keys.
  • Play 3 to 5 short pieces hands together at Grade 1 standard.

Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap

Terminology

  • Pitch (音高) — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.
  • Accidental — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.
  • Sharp (♯) — raise the pitch by one semitone.
  • Flat (♭) — lower the pitch by one semitone.
  • Natural (♮) — cancel a previous sharp or flat.

An accidental affects every occurrence of that note in the same octave for the rest of the bar — then the barline resets it. A key signature is the only "global" accidental.

Ledger lines (加線)

Grade 1 requires one ledger above and one below in each clef.

  • Treble: one line above the staff = A5, one line below the staff = Middle C (C4).
  • Bass: one line above the staff = Middle C (C4) as well (the same note), one line below the staff = E2.

Trick: Middle C is the connector. It sits one ledger below the treble staff AND one ledger above the bass staff. Same key on the piano, two ways to write it. Drill this until it's automatic — Grade 2 and beyond use it constantly.

Chapter 2 — Note Values (音符的時值)

English / 中文 / symbol table

EN 中文 UK name Beats in 4/4
Whole note 全音符 Semibreve 4
Half note 二分音符 Minim 2
Quarter note 四分音符 Crotchet 1
Eighth note 八分音符 Quaver ½

ABRSM uses the UK terms (semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver). American sheet music uses whole/half/quarter/eighth. Same notes, different names. Get used to both.

Rests

Every note value has a matching rest. The same beat counts apply — a crotchet rest is 1 beat of silence in 4/4.

Trick: I confused the whole-note rest (hangs down from a line) and the half-note rest (sits on top of a line). Memory aid: "the whole rest is a hole — it falls down. The half is half full — it sits up." Silly, never forgot it again.

Dots and ties

  • A dot after a note adds half its value. Dotted minim = 2 + 1 = 3 beats.
  • A tie joins two notes of the same pitch — sum their durations.
  • A slur joins two notes of different pitches — phrasing, not duration.

Chapter 3 — Time Signatures (拍號)

Grade 1 covers 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 only.

The top number = beats per bar. The bottom number = which note value gets one beat (4 = crotchet).

Time signature Beats per bar Feel
2/4 2 March (left-right-left-right)
3/4 3 Waltz (ONE-two-three)
4/4 4 Pop, hymn, default (ONE-two-three-four)

The first beat of every bar is the strongest. Place natural emphasis there; the bar lines exist precisely to tell you where to push slightly.

Chapter 4 — Keys and Key Signatures

Grade 1 syllabus: C major, G major, F major.

Key Sharps/Flats Memory anchor
C major none Easy default
G major 1 sharp: F♯ "Goes up" → sharp
F major 1 flat: B♭ "Falls down" → flat

Scale construction reminder

Major scale pattern in semitones: W W H W W W H (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half).

Apply from any tonic and you get a major scale. From C: all whites. From G: hits F♯ as the 7th. From F: hits B♭ as the 4th.

Trick: the major-scale pattern doesn't change — only the starting note does. If you can derive C major from scratch, you can derive every major scale by counting W W H W W W H with your finger on the keyboard. Don't memorise 12 scales; memorise one pattern and 12 starting points.

Tonic, Dominant, Subdominant

For any major scale:

  • Tonic (I) — the 1st degree. Home note. C in C major.
  • Subdominant (IV) — the 4th degree. F in C major.
  • Dominant (V) — the 5th degree. G in C major.

These three notes form the primary triads of the key, the most important chords in any major piece. Half of pop and folk music is built from just I, IV, V.

Where I got stuck: I assumed "dominant" meant "loudest" or "main". It actually means the 5th, always. The label is positional, not musical. Once I stopped overthinking it, every scale fell into place.

Chapter 5 — Intervals (Number Only)

At Grade 1 you only need to identify the number of an interval (2nd, 3rd, etc.), not its quality (major / minor / perfect). Quality starts at Grade 2.

Counting

Count the lower note as 1, then count up the staff lines and spaces to the upper note. The count includes both notes.

Interval Staff positions
Unison Same line/space
2nd Adjacent line + space
3rd Line to next line, or space to next space
4th Line to space-after-the-next-line
5th Line to second line above
6th Space to third space above
7th Line to fourth line above (one short of an octave)
Octave (8th) Same letter, one position up

Trick: 3rds and 5ths "jump white" — they go line-to-line or space-to-space, looking visually stacked. 2nds and 4ths "step black" — they touch a line and the adjacent space. Once your eye reads these shapes, you stop counting notes one-by-one.

Chapter 6 — Hands-Together Coordination

You played hands together in Post 00 already with "Twinkle". Grade 1 expands this. The technique:

  1. Each hand alone, played until boring. Genuinely boring — no mistakes, no thinking.
  2. Hands together at half speed. Eyes on the page, not the hands.
  3. Pulse first, accuracy second. If one hand stumbles, don't stop the pulse. Drop the wrong-hand note rather than break the beat.
  4. Slow with metronome. Crotchet = 60. Once smooth, raise by 5 BPM increments.

Where I got stuck: my left hand was 6 weeks behind my right. The fix: I practised only the left hand for one full week. No right hand at all. Result: by the next week my left could play its own line cleanly, and hands-together suddenly worked.

Chapter 7 — Suggested Practice Pieces (Grade 1 level)

These are real Grade 1 / Initial Grade pieces with public domain or freely-available scores. Hunt them on IMSLP or buy the ABRSM exam book — the album also contains your audio reference.

Easier end (still inside Initial Grade)

Piece Composer Why it's good for you
Minuet in G (BWV Anh. 114) Bach (attr. C. Petzold) Two-bar phrases, classic Baroque feel, hands-together at a comfortable tempo. Real music, not a "method book exercise"
Allegro in C Diabelli, Op. 125 No. 1 Pure C major, balanced hands, teaches the I-IV-V harmony pattern aurally
No. 9 'In Yugoslav Mode' Bartók, Mikrokosmos Vol. 1 Modern teaching piece; clean RH melody, sparse LH. Bartók wrote these for his son
Burgmüller, Op. 100 No. 1 ('La Candeur') Burgmüller Lyrical RH melody over a steady LH; brilliant first piece with real expression

Slightly stretching

Piece Composer Why
Minuet in F (K. 2) Mozart (age 6) Yes, six. Easy enough for Grade 1, written by a child — fun fact while you struggle
Russian Folk Song Op. 107 No. 3 Beethoven Two voices, both inside one octave. Tiny, perfect study of independent hands
No. 6 'Gypsy Dance' Bartók, For Children Vol. 1 Drone bass, melody floats above. Teaches "ostinato" by feel

How to study a piece

  1. Hands separate, slow, no metronome — until you can do four bars without error.
  2. Hands separate, with metronome — at the slowest tempo where you're still error-free.
  3. Hands together, slow — half the eventual target.
  4. Hands together, target tempo — and only here do you allow yourself to think about expression.
  5. Memorise the hardest bar — play it from any cold start.

Trick: number the bars and track them on a notepad. Mark each bar with ✓ when you can play it hands-together at tempo. The unchecked bars are your next 10-minute focus, not the whole piece. You'd be amazed how few bars are actually broken — usually 3-4 out of 32.

Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills

Grade 1 ABRSM practical includes an aural test. The skills:

  • Clap back a rhythm — listen to a 2- or 3-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4; clap it back.
  • Sing back a short phrase — three or four notes, played twice.
  • Identify time signature — listen to a tune; say whether it's in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4.

Practising aural without a teacher

  • Phone metronome + clap for rhythms. Tap a 4-bar pattern on the table, then clap it back without looking.
  • Sing scale degrees — call out "1, 2, 3" up the major scale while playing it. Builds the do-re-mi reflex.
  • Listen to any piece and tap your foot. If your tapping settles into pairs, it's 2/4 or 4/4. If into threes, it's 3/4. Public music is your unlimited test bank.

Trick: whistling is sneaky-good for aural training. If you can whistle the tune you've been practising, you've internalised the pitch shape. If you can only "sort of" whistle it, you haven't. The whistle is a free pitch test.

Chapter 9 — Common Grade 1 Pitfalls

Pitfall Fix
Counting in your head, not out loud Out loud — especially on rests. The voice doesn't lie
Forgetting accidentals carry through the bar Re-write any tricky bar on paper, accidentals included
Reading every note individually Train shapes (steps vs leaps, scale-fragments) — see Chapter 5
Skipping the LH because RH is "the melody" LH is half the music. Practise LH alone twice as much for a month
Slowing down only the hard bars Slow down the whole piece. Speed differences mid-piece hide as nervousness in exam
Practising past tiredness 25 min focused > 60 min tired. Stop when accuracy drops

Useful Shortcut

Cheat-sheet

What How

Find any key in F major Same as C, except B → B♭

Identify interval number Count up staff positions, include both endpoints
Dotted note value note + half its value
Bar must equal Top number of time signature
Tonic / subdominant / dominant 1st / 4th / 5th degrees

Daily routine (30 min)

Block Time Content
Five-finger warm-up 3 min C, G, F positions, both hands
Scale 5 min C, G, F major hands separate, one octave
Sight-reading 5 min New short piece each day
Repertoire 12 min Current piece, focus on the worst 4 bars
Theory 5 min One past-paper question, written out by hand

Suggested listening (context, not homework)

Piece Composer Why
Anna Magdalena Notebook (full) Bach Bach wrote this for his second wife — beginner-friendly but real music. Many Grade 1-3 pieces sit inside it
Album for the Young, Op. 68 Schumann Romantic counterpart to Bach's notebook. Hear what's coming in Grade 2-3
Minute Waltz, Op. 64 No. 1 Chopin Aspirational; impossible at Grade 1 but plant the seed
Goldberg Variations, BWV 988 Bach The Mount Everest of solo keyboard. Listen for context, not to play (yet)
Children's Album, Op. 39 Tchaikovsky Russian counterpart to Schumann's; many pieces sit at Grade 3-4
