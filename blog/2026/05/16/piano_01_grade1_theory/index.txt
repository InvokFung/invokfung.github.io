11:T6888,<p>ABRSM Grade 1 covers the foundations every musician needs: clefs, note values, simple time signatures, the first few keys, basic intervals, and the very first scales. This post pairs the syllabus with practice pieces graded at the same level, plus the memory tricks I wish someone had handed me on day one.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: 00 Beginner Basics → <strong>01 Grade 1</strong> (this post) → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4. Grades 5–8 to follow.</p>

<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Read fluently in treble and bass clef on the five staff lines, four spaces, and one ledger line above or below.</li>
<li>Identify all note values from semibreve to quaver, and matching rests.</li>
<li>Use 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 time signatures correctly.</li>
<li>Name notes in C, G, F majors and their key signatures.</li>
<li>Build major scales for C, G, F (one octave, hands separate).</li>
<li>Identify intervals between any two notes of the C major scale (number only).</li>
<li>Recognise the <em>tonic</em>, <em>dominant</em>, <em>subdominant</em> of those three keys.</li>
<li>Play 3 to 5 short pieces hands together at Grade 1 standard.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-Pitch-Notation-Recap"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-Pitch-Notation-Recap" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap"></a>Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pitch (音高)</strong> — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.</li>
<li><strong>Accidental</strong> — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.</li>
<li><strong>Sharp (♯)</strong> — raise the pitch by one semitone.</li>
<li><strong>Flat (♭)</strong> — lower the pitch by one semitone.</li>
<li><strong>Natural (♮)</strong> — cancel a previous sharp or flat.</li>
</ul>
<p>An accidental affects every occurrence of that note <strong>in the same octave for the rest of the bar</strong> — then the barline resets it. A key signature is the only &quot;global&quot; accidental.</p>
<h2 id="Ledger-lines-加線"><a href="#Ledger-lines-加線" class="headerlink" title="Ledger lines (加線)"></a>Ledger lines (加線)</h2>
<p>Grade 1 requires one ledger above and one below in each clef.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treble:</strong> one line above the staff = <strong>A5</strong>, one line below the staff = <strong>Middle C (C4)</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Bass:</strong> one line above the staff = <strong>Middle C (C4)</strong> as well (the same note), one line below the staff = <strong>E2</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> Middle C is <em>the</em> connector. It sits one ledger below the treble staff AND one ledger above the bass staff. Same key on the piano, two ways to write it. Drill this until it's automatic — Grade 2 and beyond use it constantly.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Rests"><a href="#Rests" class="headerlink" title="Rests"></a>Rests</h2>
<p>Every note value has a matching rest. The same beat counts apply — a crotchet rest is 1 beat of silence in 4/4.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> I confused the <strong>whole-note rest</strong> (hangs down from a line) and the <strong>half-note rest</strong> (sits on top of a line). Memory aid: &quot;the <strong>whole</strong> rest is a <strong>hole</strong> — it falls down. The half is half full — it sits up.&quot; Silly, never forgot it again.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Dots-and-ties"><a href="#Dots-and-ties" class="headerlink" title="Dots and ties"></a>Dots and ties</h2>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>dot</strong> after a note adds <strong>half</strong> its value. Dotted minim = 2 + 1 = <strong>3</strong> beats.</li>
<li>A <strong>tie</strong> joins two notes of the <em>same pitch</em> — sum their durations.</li>
<li>A <strong>slur</strong> joins two notes of <em>different pitches</em> — phrasing, not duration.</li>
</ul>

<p>The <strong>first beat of every bar is the strongest</strong>. Place natural emphasis there; the bar lines exist precisely to tell you where to push slightly.</p>
<h2 id="Bar-小節-maths"><a href="#Bar-小節-maths" class="headerlink" title="Bar (小節) maths"></a>Bar (小節) maths</h2>
<p>The notes in any bar must add up to the time signature's beat count. If 4/4 has only crotchets and quavers, common bars include:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-number">4</span>/<span class="hljs-number">4</span>:    ♩ ♩ ♩ ♩         = <span class="hljs-number">4</span></span><br><span class="line">        ♩ ♩ ♪♪ ♩         = <span class="hljs-number">4</span>    <span class="hljs-comment">(♪♪ = two quavers, value 1)</span></span><br><span class="line">        ♩. ♪ ♩ ♩         = <span class="hljs-number">4</span>    <span class="hljs-comment">(dotted crotchet + quaver = 2)</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> when sight-reading, <strong>count out loud</strong> on every beat — including rests. The voice keeps the pulse even when your hands stumble. Silent counting is for performance, not learning.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Scale-construction-reminder"><a href="#Scale-construction-reminder" class="headerlink" title="Scale construction reminder"></a>Scale construction reminder</h2>
<p>Major scale pattern in semitones: <strong>W W H W W W H</strong> (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half).</p>
<p>Apply from any tonic and you get a major scale. From <strong>C</strong>: all whites. From <strong>G</strong>: hits F♯ as the 7th. From <strong>F</strong>: hits B♭ as the 4th.</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">major</span>:  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">major</span>:  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span>♯ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">major</span>:  <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span>♭ <span class="hljs-selector-tag">C</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">D</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">E</span> <span class="hljs-selector-tag">F</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> the major-scale <strong>pattern doesn't change</strong> — only the starting note does. If you can derive C major from scratch, you can derive every major scale by counting <strong>W W H W W W H</strong> with your finger on the keyboard. Don't memorise 12 scales; memorise <em>one pattern</em> and 12 starting points.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Tonic-Dominant-Subdominant"><a href="#Tonic-Dominant-Subdominant" class="headerlink" title="Tonic, Dominant, Subdominant"></a>Tonic, Dominant, Subdominant</h2>
<p>For any major scale:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tonic (I)</strong> — the 1st degree. Home note. C in C major.</li>
<li><strong>Subdominant (IV)</strong> — the 4th degree. F in C major.</li>
<li><strong>Dominant (V)</strong> — the 5th degree. G in C major.</li>
</ul>
<p>These three notes form the <strong>primary triads</strong> of the key, the most important chords in any major piece. Half of pop and folk music is built from just I, IV, V.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I assumed &quot;dominant&quot; meant &quot;loudest&quot; or &quot;main&quot;. It actually means <strong>the 5th</strong>, <em>always</em>. The label is positional, not musical. Once I stopped overthinking it, every scale fell into place.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Intervals-Number-Only"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Intervals-Number-Only" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Intervals (Number Only)"></a>Chapter 5 — Intervals (Number Only)</h1>
<p>At Grade 1 you only need to identify the <strong>number</strong> of an interval (2nd, 3rd, etc.), not its quality (major / minor / perfect). Quality starts at Grade 2.</p>
<h2 id="Counting"><a href="#Counting" class="headerlink" title="Counting"></a>Counting</h2>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Hands-Together-Coordination"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Hands-Together-Coordination" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Hands-Together Coordination"></a>Chapter 6 — Hands-Together Coordination</h1>
<p>You played hands together in Post 00 already with &quot;Twinkle&quot;. Grade 1 expands this. The technique:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Each hand alone, played until boring.</strong> Genuinely boring — no mistakes, no thinking.</li>
<li><strong>Hands together at half speed.</strong> Eyes on the page, not the hands.</li>
<li><strong>Pulse first, accuracy second.</strong> If one hand stumbles, <em>don't stop the pulse</em>. Drop the wrong-hand note rather than break the beat.</li>
<li><strong>Slow with metronome.</strong> Crotchet = 60. Once smooth, raise by 5 BPM increments.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> my left hand was 6 weeks behind my right. The fix: I practised <em>only</em> the left hand for one full week. No right hand at all. Result: by the next week my left could play its own line cleanly, and hands-together suddenly worked.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="How-to-study-a-piece"><a href="#How-to-study-a-piece" class="headerlink" title="How to study a piece"></a>How to study a piece</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Hands separate, slow, no metronome</strong> — until you can do four bars without error.</li>
<li><strong>Hands separate, with metronome</strong> — at the slowest tempo where you're still error-free.</li>
<li><strong>Hands together, slow</strong> — half the eventual target.</li>
<li><strong>Hands together, target tempo</strong> — and only here do you allow yourself to think about expression.</li>
<li><strong>Memorise the hardest bar</strong> — play it from any cold start.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> number the bars and track them on a notepad. Mark each bar with ✓ when you can play it hands-together at tempo. The unchecked bars are your next 10-minute focus, not the whole piece. You'd be amazed how few bars are actually broken — usually 3-4 out of 32.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-Aural-Listening-Skills"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-Aural-Listening-Skills" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills"></a>Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills</h1>
<p>Grade 1 ABRSM practical includes an aural test. The skills:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Clap back a rhythm</strong> — listen to a 2- or 3-bar rhythm in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4; clap it back.</li>
<li><strong>Sing back a short phrase</strong> — three or four notes, played twice.</li>
<li><strong>Identify time signature</strong> — listen to a tune; say whether it's in 2/4, 3/4, or 4/4.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Practising-aural-without-a-teacher"><a href="#Practising-aural-without-a-teacher" class="headerlink" title="Practising aural without a teacher"></a>Practising aural without a teacher</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Phone metronome + clap</strong> for rhythms. Tap a 4-bar pattern on the table, then clap it back without looking.</li>
<li><strong>Sing scale degrees</strong> — call out &quot;1, 2, 3&quot; up the major scale while playing it. Builds the do-re-mi reflex.</li>
<li><strong>Listen to <em>any</em> piece</strong> and tap your foot. If your tapping settles into pairs, it's 2/4 or 4/4. If into threes, it's 3/4. Public music is your unlimited test bank.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> whistling is sneaky-good for aural training. If you can whistle the tune you've been practising, you've internalised the pitch shape. If you can only &quot;sort of&quot; whistle it, you haven't. The whistle is a free pitch test.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Common-Grade-1-Pitfalls"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Common-Grade-1-Pitfalls" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Common Grade 1 Pitfalls"></a>Chapter 9 — Common Grade 1 Pitfalls</h1>

<tr>
<th>Pitfall</th>
<th>Fix</th>
</tr>
