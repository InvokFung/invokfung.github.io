11:T75c5,<p>The first post in a self-study violin series. Goal: by the end you can hold the violin without straining, name every open string, draw a straight bow that makes one sustained tone, and play three short open-string pieces. The violin is harder than the piano at the start — there's no &quot;pressed key = right pitch&quot; guarantee — but the payoff is the most expressive single-line instrument we have.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: <strong>00</strong> Beginner (this post) → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → 04 Grade 4 → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8.</p>

<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Hold the violin with relaxed posture, no shoulder hunching.</li>
<li>Hold the bow with the correct grip and produce a straight stroke.</li>
<li>Name the four open strings (G, D, A, E) low to high and find them on the staff.</li>
<li>Tune with a clip-on tuner or app.</li>
<li>Play three open-string exercises that produce a clear, ringing tone.</li>
<li>Understand the difference between violin, viola, cello, and double bass — pick the right one.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-Why-violin-and-why-now"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-Why-violin-and-why-now" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now)"></a>Chapter 1 — Why violin (and why now)</h1>
<p>The violin produces sound by drawing horsehair across a string. Unlike the piano (where the pitch is fixed by the keyboard), every note on the violin is <em>unfretted</em> — you place your finger somewhere and the <strong>precision of placement</strong> decides whether it's in tune or sour. The first six months are essentially an ear-training course in disguise.</p>
<p>In return: the violin gives you the most direct path to the human singing voice. Phrasing, vibrato, bow weight — all of it shapes a single line in ways no fretted or keyed instrument can match.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I started:</em> I almost quit in week three because my own playing sounded scratchy. The fix wasn't talent — it was bow weight and bow speed (Chapter 5). Sound quality is mechanical first, expressive later. Don't blame your ear; blame the bow contact point.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Key parts to know by name (you'll hear teachers say these):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Scroll, pegs, peg box</strong> — top, where strings wind around tuning pegs</li>
<li><strong>Nut</strong> — small ridge where strings cross from peg box to fingerboard</li>
<li><strong>Fingerboard</strong> — the long black wooden strip your fingers press</li>
<li><strong>Bridge</strong> — small wooden arch that holds strings off the body and transmits vibration</li>
<li><strong>F-holes</strong> — the two carved openings; let sound out</li>
<li><strong>Soundpost</strong> — small wooden dowel <em>inside</em> the violin between top and back plates. Critical for tone. Don't touch.</li>
<li><strong>Bass bar</strong> — wooden strip glued under the top plate. Also don't touch.</li>
<li><strong>Tailpiece</strong> — anchors the strings at the bottom</li>
<li><strong>Fine tuners</strong> — small screws on the tailpiece for precise tuning (your E string almost always has one; the others may or may not)</li>
<li><strong>Chinrest</strong> — where your chin rests</li>
<li><strong>Shoulder rest</strong> — clips onto the back, sits on your shoulder</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Posture-and-how-to-hold-the-violin"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Posture-and-how-to-hold-the-violin" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin"></a>Chapter 4 — Posture and how to hold the violin</h1>
<p>Posture is everything. Get this wrong and you're fighting your own body for years.</p>
<h2 id="Standing"><a href="#Standing" class="headerlink" title="Standing"></a>Standing</h2>
<ol>
<li>Feet shoulder-width apart, weight on both.</li>
<li>Spine tall, shoulders relaxed (not hunched, not pulled back).</li>
<li>Head facing forward, then turn it slightly <strong>left</strong> to look down the fingerboard.</li>
<li>Place the violin on your <strong>left collarbone</strong>, chin gently on the chinrest, shoulder rest cushioning underneath.</li>
<li>The violin should stay in place when your left hand drops — <em>don't grip with your jaw</em>. The shoulder rest does the work.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I clamped my jaw on the chinrest for six months and developed neck pain. Fix: adjust the shoulder rest <em>higher</em> until your jaw can rest light, like resting your chin on a friend's shoulder for a slow dance. The violin is <em>supported</em>, not <em>gripped</em>. Two weeks of correct setup undoes the bad habit.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Sitting"><a href="#Sitting" class="headerlink" title="Sitting"></a>Sitting</h2>
<p>Same upper-body posture. Sit on the <strong>front half</strong> of the chair, both feet flat. Cellists slump; violinists must not — gravity pulls the violin off the shoulder if you slouch.</p>
<h2 id="Left-hand-position"><a href="#Left-hand-position" class="headerlink" title="Left-hand position"></a>Left-hand position</h2>
<ul>
<li>Thumb rests on the <strong>side</strong> of the neck, around the same level as the first finger.</li>
<li>Fingers curve over the strings.</li>
<li>Wrist <em>straight</em> — neither collapsed inward nor pushed out.</li>
<li>A peace-sign gap visible between the neck and the base of the index finger.</li>
</ul>
<p>Common mistakes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Wrist collapsed</strong> under the neck (the &quot;frying pan grip&quot;) — kills agility.</li>
<li><strong>Thumb wrapped over the top</strong> of the fingerboard — guitar players do this; violinists can't.</li>
<li><strong>Fingers flat</strong> on the strings — must be curled, fingertips down.</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-The-bow"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-The-bow" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — The bow"></a>Chapter 5 — The bow</h1>
<h2 id="Anatomy-of-the-bow"><a href="#Anatomy-of-the-bow" class="headerlink" title="Anatomy of the bow"></a>Anatomy of the bow</h2>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">frog                                                                        tip</span><br><span class="line"> ╔══╗═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗</span><br><span class="line"> ║  ║  hair (horsehair, rosined)                                             ║</span><br><span class="line"> ╚══╝═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝</span><br><span class="line">  ↑                                                                          ↑</span><br><span class="line"> thumb <span class="hljs-built_in">area</span>                                                              <span class="hljs-built_in">point</span>/tip</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stick</strong> — wood (Pernambuco traditionally, carbon fibre common today)</li>
<li><strong>Hair</strong> — horsehair, needs rosin to grip strings</li>
<li><strong>Frog</strong> — black bottom piece where the screw lives</li>
<li><strong>Screw / button</strong> — adjusts hair tension</li>
<li><strong>Tip / point</strong> — top end</li>
<li><strong>Grip / wrap</strong> — leather + silver winding around the stick near the frog</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Bow-hold-the-most-important-5-minutes-of-your-beginner-life"><a href="#Bow-hold-the-most-important-5-minutes-of-your-beginner-life" class="headerlink" title="Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life)"></a>Bow hold (the most important 5 minutes of your beginner life)</h2>
<ol>
<li>Pick the bow up at the frog with your <strong>right hand</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Thumb</strong> bent, tip placed in the <strong>notch between the frog and the wrap</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Middle finger</strong> opposite the thumb, around the frog ferrule.</li>
<li><strong>Ring finger</strong> rests on the side of the frog.</li>
<li><strong>Pinky</strong> curved on top of the stick, near the screw.</li>
<li><strong>Index finger</strong> wraps around the stick a little further toward the tip, controlling weight.</li>
</ol>
<p>All fingers <strong>curved</strong>, never locked straight. The grip is <em>light</em> — too tight and the bow can't move.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> practise the bow hold on a pencil first. Hold the pencil where the bow stick would be. If you can do it without thinking — <em>then</em> pick up the actual bow. Mid-week 1, your hand is still learning the geometry; don't waste bow hair on dropped bows.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Tightening-the-bow"><a href="#Tightening-the-bow" class="headerlink" title="Tightening the bow"></a>Tightening the bow</h2>
<p>Turn the screw clockwise. The hair should be tight enough that a <strong>standard pencil</strong> fits between hair and stick at the midpoint. Too tight = warps the stick over time + tone gets harsh. Too loose = the stick touches the strings.</p>
<p><strong>Always loosen</strong> after every session (a few turns counter-clockwise). Storing tight ruins the stick's camber.</p>
<h2 id="Rosin"><a href="#Rosin" class="headerlink" title="Rosin"></a>Rosin</h2>
<p>Cake of tree resin. Run the bow hair across it 10-20 times <strong>before every session</strong>. New bows or freshly-rehaired bows need 30-50 strokes the first time. Without rosin, the hair slips silently across the string and makes no sound. Too much = squeaky, dusty tone.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Tuning"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Tuning" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Tuning"></a>Chapter 6 — Tuning</h1>
<p>The four strings, <strong>low to high</strong>: <strong>G — D — A — E</strong>. Each interval is a <strong>perfect 5th</strong> (7 semitones). On a piano: G3, D4, A4, E5.</p>
<h2 id="With-a-clip-on-tuner-or-app"><a href="#With-a-clip-on-tuner-or-app" class="headerlink" title="With a clip-on tuner or app"></a>With a clip-on tuner or app</h2>
<ol>
<li>Clip tuner to the scroll or peg box.</li>
<li>Pluck or bow each string one at a time.</li>
<li>Read pitch. If sharp, lower; if flat, raise.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="Coarse-tuning-with-pegs-large-adjustments"><a href="#Coarse-tuning-with-pegs-large-adjustments" class="headerlink" title="Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments)"></a>Coarse tuning with pegs (large adjustments)</h2>
<p>Pegs are friction-fit — turn while pushing <em>into</em> the peg box. If you only turn, the peg slips back. Push-and-turn together.</p>
<ul>
<li>Turn <strong>away from you</strong> (top of peg moves forward) = string winds up = pitch rises.</li>
<li>Turn <strong>toward you</strong> = pitch falls.</li>
</ul>
<p>Small turns. Pegs are sensitive. Tune the G first, then D, A, E.</p>
<h2 id="Fine-tuning-small-adjustments"><a href="#Fine-tuning-small-adjustments" class="headerlink" title="Fine tuning (small adjustments)"></a>Fine tuning (small adjustments)</h2>
<p>The little screws on the tailpiece. Turn <strong>clockwise</strong> to raise pitch, <strong>counter-clockwise</strong> to lower. Use these for daily small corrections (&lt; quarter-tone).</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I terrified myself snapping a string in week one. Fix: tune <strong>slowly and small</strong>. If a string is way off, raise it gradually, not in one big peg crank. Snapped strings are normal — buy a backup E string ($5) and stop being afraid.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Tuning-A-first-orchestral-convention"><a href="#Tuning-A-first-orchestral-convention" class="headerlink" title="Tuning A first (orchestral convention)"></a>Tuning A first (orchestral convention)</h2>
<p>In orchestral settings, tune A first (because the oboe gives an A). Then tune D, G, E by listening for <strong>perfect fifths</strong> with the open A. This is your first ear-training exercise — when the two strings are exactly a 5th apart, the combined sound is <em>clean</em>. When slightly off, you hear &quot;beats&quot; — a wobbling pulse.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Your-first-sound-open-string-bowing"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Your-first-sound-open-string-bowing" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing"></a>Chapter 7 — Your first sound: open-string bowing</h1>
<p>Before fingers go on strings, master pulling a clean tone on each open string.</p>
<h2 id="The-straight-bow"><a href="#The-straight-bow" class="headerlink" title="The straight bow"></a>The straight bow</h2>
<p>The bow must move <strong>perpendicular</strong> to the strings (parallel to the bridge). If it angles toward the fingerboard or the bridge, the contact point changes and the tone breaks up.</p>
<p>Practice exercise:</p>
<ol>
<li>Place the bow flat on the <strong>A string</strong>, about halfway between bridge and fingerboard.</li>
<li>Slowly pull a downstroke (bow moving from frog toward tip). Count &quot;1-2-3-4&quot;.</li>
<li>Pause. Reset. Upstroke (tip toward frog). Same count.</li>
<li>Watch the bow in a mirror — is it staying parallel to the bridge?</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="Contact-point"><a href="#Contact-point" class="headerlink" title="Contact point"></a>Contact point</h2>
<p>Three knobs control your sound:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weight</strong> — how much the bow presses down (mostly from the index finger via the stick)</li>
<li><strong>Speed</strong> — how fast the bow moves</li>
<li><strong>Contact point</strong> — distance from the bridge (closer to bridge = louder + sharper; closer to fingerboard = softer + warmer)</li>
</ul>
<p>Balance these three. A faster bow needs more weight to maintain pressure. A bow close to the bridge needs slower speed to avoid scratching. The &quot;sweet spot&quot; for a clean, warm tone is about <strong>1.5–2 cm from the bridge</strong> for a normal <em>mezzo-forte</em> dynamic.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Analogy:</em> think of the bow like painting. <strong>Speed</strong> is how fast you move the brush. <strong>Weight</strong> is how hard you press. <strong>Contact point</strong> is which part of the paper you're stroking. Change any one and the paint stroke changes. Beginners change all three accidentally and produce a mess. Master one knob at a time.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Three-first-exercises"><a href="#Three-first-exercises" class="headerlink" title="Three first exercises"></a>Three first exercises</h2>
<h3 id="Exercise-1-—-Long-bows-on-each-string"><a href="#Exercise-1-—-Long-bows-on-each-string" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string"></a>Exercise 1 — Long bows on each string</h3>
<p>Whole bow length, 8 beats per stroke. Down-up-down-up on each string in turn: G, D, A, E. Listen for a clean, sustained tone with no scratches or wobbles. Five minutes daily.</p>
<h3 id="Exercise-2-—-String-crossings"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-String-crossings" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — String crossings"></a>Exercise 2 — String crossings</h3>
<p>Bow A for one beat, then D for one beat. The forearm rotates from elbow; wrist stays neutral. Do A-D-A-D-A-D, then D-A-D-A. Then add G-D and A-E. Tests bow-arm coordination without fingers complicating things.</p>
<h3 id="Exercise-3-—-Hot-cross-buns-open-strings-only"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Hot-cross-buns-open-strings-only" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — &quot;Hot cross buns&quot; (open strings only)"></a>Exercise 3 — &quot;Hot cross buns&quot; (open strings only)</h3>
<p>The melody fits perfectly on three open strings if you treat it as A-D-A-D-G-D-A. Just bow each string in the rhythm of the tune. You'll feel embarrassed playing nursery rhymes — and then proud, because you sound <em>musical</em>.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-How-to-practise"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-How-to-practise" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — How to practise"></a>Chapter 8 — How to practise</h1>
<p>The single most important rule: <strong>slow and small reps beat fast big ones</strong>. Five minutes of focused open-string bowing teaches more than thirty minutes of trying to play melodies you can't yet.</p>

<h2 id="Slow-practice-rules"><a href="#Slow-practice-rules" class="headerlink" title="Slow practice rules"></a>Slow practice rules</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>No scratch, no rush.</strong> If the tone is bad, slow down.</li>
<li><strong>One thing at a time.</strong> Bow speed <em>or</em> bow weight <em>or</em> contact point — not all three at once.</li>
<li><strong>Mirror.</strong> Use a mirror or phone front-camera for 5 min per session for the first 3 months. The eye catches what the hand doesn't.</li>
<li><strong>Stop before fatigue.</strong> Tired = tense = bad habits. Better to stop at 20 minutes feeling fresh than 45 minutes feeling done.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> after every session, <strong>record 30 seconds</strong> on your phone. Open string, long bow, full attention. Listen back. Week 1 will sound rough. Week 8 will sound transformed. The recording is your honest progress meter when your ears in the moment lie.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Common-beginner-mistakes"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Common-beginner-mistakes" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Common beginner mistakes"></a>Chapter 9 — Common beginner mistakes</h1>

<th>Mistake</th>
<th>Fix</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Crooked bow (drifts toward fingerboard)</td>
<td>Mirror practice. Mark the bridge-side bow lane with tape on your bow if needed</td>

<h2 id="When-to-move-on"><a href="#When-to-move-on" class="headerlink" title="When to move on"></a>When to move on</h2>
<p>You're ready for <strong>Post 01 — Grade 1 Theory &amp; Repertoire</strong> when you can:</p>
<ul>
<li>Tune all four strings with a tuner unaided.</li>
<li>Produce a clean, sustained tone on each open string for 8 beats.</li>
<li>Cross strings smoothly without scratching.</li>
<li>Play &quot;Hot cross buns&quot; or &quot;Twinkle Twinkle&quot; (open-string version) recognisably.</li>
<li>Hold the violin and bow for 15 minutes without fatigue.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you're not there yet, stay here another 2-3 weeks. Grade 1 adds fingers; without solid open-string tone, fingered notes will sound <em>worse</em>, not better.</p>
