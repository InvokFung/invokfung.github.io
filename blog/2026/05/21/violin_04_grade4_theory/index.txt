11:T546f,<p>Grade 4 ABRSM violin is where the left hand finally <strong>leaves first position</strong>. Real 2nd and 3rd position shifting becomes required, melodic minor enters the playing syllabus, more flat/sharp keys (E♭, E, A♭) appear, and the right hand learns <em>spiccato</em> — the controlled bouncing bow. The repertoire moves into early concertos and substantial Baroque sonatas.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → 03 Grade 3 → <strong>04 Grade 4</strong> (this post) → 05 Grade 5 → … → 08 Grade 8.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Play two-octave scales in <strong>all keys up to 3 sharps and 3 flats</strong> majors.</li>
<li>Play <strong>melodic minor</strong> in playing form (not just paper).</li>
<li>Shift cleanly to <strong>2nd and 3rd position</strong> and back.</li>
<li>Begin <strong>spiccato</strong> (controlled bouncing bow) on open strings + simple scales.</li>
<li>Identify all interval qualities at sight.</li>
<li>Play 6–8 Grade 4 pieces including one early concerto movement.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-Position-shifting-becomes-routine"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-Position-shifting-becomes-routine" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine"></a>Chapter 1 — Position shifting becomes routine</h1>
<h2 id="3rd-position-—-the-workhorse"><a href="#3rd-position-—-the-workhorse" class="headerlink" title="3rd position — the workhorse"></a>3rd position — the workhorse</h2>
<p>3rd position is the <strong>second most used position</strong> after 1st. Most professional violinists spend 60–70% of playing time in 1st or 3rd.</p>
<h3 id="Finding-3rd-position"><a href="#Finding-3rd-position" class="headerlink" title="Finding 3rd position"></a>Finding 3rd position</h3>
<p>On the A string:</p>
<ol>
<li>Play open A.</li>
<li>Slide 1st finger up until it sounds D5 (the same pitch as 3rd finger in 1st position).</li>
<li>Now: 1=D5, 2=E5, 3=F♯5 (in D major), 4=G5.</li>
</ol>
<p>Other strings analogous:</p>
<ul>
<li>G string: 1=C, 2=D, 3=E (depending on key), 4=F or F♯.</li>
<li>D string: 1=G, 2=A, 3=B (or B♭), 4=C.</li>
<li>E string: 1=A, 2=B, 3=C♯ (or C), 4=D.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="The-shift-motion"><a href="#The-shift-motion" class="headerlink" title="The shift motion"></a>The shift motion</h3>
<p>Three components, in this order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Release</strong> finger pressure (don't lift the finger — just release the press).</li>
<li><strong>Thumb and hand travel together</strong> up the neck.</li>
<li><strong>Press the new finger</strong> at the target.</li>
</ol>
<p>The finger that <em>leads</em> the shift is called the <strong>guide finger</strong>. In a 1→3 shift, the guide is whichever finger is currently down — usually 1.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> practise silent shifts. Bow open string, shift the left hand up and down while the bow drones the open string. You should hear no glissando — only the open string ringing. If you hear a &quot;scrape&quot;, the guide finger is pressing too hard during the slide.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="2nd-position"><a href="#2nd-position" class="headerlink" title="2nd position"></a>2nd position</h2>
<p>Less common than 3rd. The hand sits <strong>halfway</strong> between 1st and 3rd. On the A string: 1=C♯/C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F♯/F.</p>
<p>2nd position is the answer when a phrase sits <strong>just above</strong> 1st position but doesn't go high enough to need 3rd. Used for awkward fingerings, not new pitches.</p>
<h2 id="How-to-choose-position"><a href="#How-to-choose-position" class="headerlink" title="How to choose position"></a>How to choose position</h2>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Melodic-minor-in-playing"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Melodic-minor-in-playing" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Melodic minor in playing"></a>Chapter 2 — Melodic minor in playing</h1>
<p>Grade 3 introduced melodic minor on paper. Grade 4 requires playing.</p>
<h2 id="D-melodic-minor-one-or-two-octaves"><a href="#D-melodic-minor-one-or-two-octaves" class="headerlink" title="D melodic minor (one or two octaves)"></a>D melodic minor (one or two octaves)</h2>
<p>Ascending: D E F G A B C♯ D — raise 6 (B) and 7 (C♯).<br>
Descending: D C B♭ A G F E D — back to natural.</p>
<p>Fingering (one octave on D + A):</p>

<p>That <strong>B♭ on descent / B♮ on ascent</strong> on the same string is the tricky part. Same finger (1), different position.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> my hand could not switch B♮ ↔ B♭ in one scale. Fix: bow only the A string portion (B♭ down, B♮ up) until the finger shape change became automatic. Don't add the rest of the scale until that bar is reliable.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Spiccato-the-controlled-bounce"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Spiccato-the-controlled-bounce" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce"></a>Chapter 3 — Spiccato: the controlled bounce</h1>
<p><em>Spiccato</em> is the foundation of all advanced bowing. The bow lifts off the string between notes — not because you lifted it, but because the <strong>bow's elasticity</strong> bounces it.</p>
<h2 id="How-spiccato-works"><a href="#How-spiccato-works" class="headerlink" title="How spiccato works"></a>How spiccato works</h2>
<p>The bow is <strong>flexible</strong>. When dropped onto the string and pulled, the wood + hair vibrate vertically — they want to bounce. Find the &quot;sweet spot&quot; (middle to slightly upper-middle of the bow), drop with just enough weight, draw a short stroke, and the bow rebounds for the next note.</p>
<h2 id="Spiccato-practice-progression"><a href="#Spiccato-practice-progression" class="headerlink" title="Spiccato practice progression"></a>Spiccato practice progression</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Drop test.</strong> Hold the bow over the A string, let it fall from 1 cm above, watch it bounce. Find the bow position where bounce is most controlled (usually 4–6 inches above the middle, near the balance point).</li>
<li><strong>One bounce per drop.</strong> Drop, lift, drop. Slow.</li>
<li><strong>Two bounces per drop</strong> without re-lifting. The bow bounces itself.</li>
<li><strong>Continuous bouncing on open string</strong> at metronome 60.</li>
<li><strong>Add scale notes.</strong> D major scale, spiccato.</li>
<li><strong>Speed up gradually</strong> to 100, then 120.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> spiccato fails when you grip the bow. Relax thumb + pinky; let the bow do the work. If your wrist hurts after spiccato practice, you're forcing it. Stop, shake out, restart looser.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-New-keys-E-major-A♭-major"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-New-keys-E-major-A♭-major" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — New keys: E major, A♭ major"></a>Chapter 4 — New keys: E major, A♭ major</h1>
<h2 id="E-major-4-sharps-F♯-C♯-G♯-D♯"><a href="#E-major-4-sharps-F♯-C♯-G♯-D♯" class="headerlink" title="E major (4 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯)"></a>E major (4 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯)</h2>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">E string:  E   <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=F♯   <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=G♯   <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=A   <span class="hljs-attribute">4</span>=B</span><br><span class="line">A string:  A(open) → continues up</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Fingering: normal &quot;high&quot; positions everywhere. Coming after the flat keys, E major <strong>feels easy</strong> because the violin's open strings (E, A, D) are all natural to the key.</p>
<h2 id="A♭-major-4-flats-B♭-E♭-A♭-D♭"><a href="#A♭-major-4-flats-B♭-E♭-A♭-D♭" class="headerlink" title="A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭)"></a>A♭ major (4 flats: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭)</h2>
<p>The hardest playable key at Grade 4. Two &quot;low&quot; fingers on most strings; A♭ itself (instead of open A) creates an unusual hand position.</p>
<p>Most editions require A♭ for <strong>one octave only</strong> at Grade 4. Two-octave A♭ is Grade 5+.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> don't memorise A♭ note-by-note. Memorise it as a <strong>modified D major shape</strong>, with low 1 on the D string and a low everywhere else. Pattern recognition over note recall.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-All-four-cadences-theory"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-All-four-cadences-theory" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — All four cadences (theory)"></a>Chapter 5 — All four cadences (theory)</h1>
<p>Grade 4 paper requires recognition of all four cadences:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Cadence</th>
<th>Chords</th>
<th>Sound</th>
<th>Example</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Perfect</strong> (Authentic)</td>
<td>V → I</td>
<td>&quot;Full stop&quot;</td>
<td>End of <em>Twinkle</em></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Plagal</strong> (&quot;Amen&quot;)</td>
<td>IV → I</td>
<td>&quot;Settled, churchy&quot;</td>

<tr>

<td>&quot;Surprise&quot;</td>
<td>Romantic deception</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Identify by ear in aural: play tonic chord + the cadence chord. Listen to the resolution.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> sing &quot;Happy Birthday&quot; — the last two chords are <strong>V → I</strong>, perfect. Sing the church &quot;Amen&quot; at the end of any hymn — that's <strong>IV → I</strong>, plagal. Pre-load these references; any cadence question becomes &quot;which of my references does this match?&quot;.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Vivaldi-Concerto-in-A-minor-a-deeper-note"><a href="#Vivaldi-Concerto-in-A-minor-a-deeper-note" class="headerlink" title="Vivaldi Concerto in A minor: a deeper note"></a>Vivaldi Concerto in A minor: a deeper note</h2>
<p>The first movement is the <strong>first concerto every Suzuki violinist plays</strong>. It includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Energetic 16th-note passages</li>
<li>A cadenza-like solo break</li>
<li>Sequences (repeating patterns at different pitches)</li>
<li>Multiple shifts to 3rd position</li>
<li>A sense of dialogue between soloist and orchestra</li>
</ul>
<p>Practise this slowly: 4 weeks at half tempo before any attempt at performance speed.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Aural-Listening-Skills"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Aural-Listening-Skills" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills"></a>Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills</h1>
<p>Grade 4 adds:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sing back</strong> a 4-bar phrase with one or two leaps.</li>
<li><strong>Identify cadences</strong> (perfect, plagal, imperfect, interrupted).</li>
<li><strong>Identify</strong> features in a passage (modulation? change of texture?).</li>
<li><strong>Sight-sing</strong> a simple 4-bar phrase (Grade 4+ feature).</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Sight-singing-strategy"><a href="#Sight-singing-strategy" class="headerlink" title="Sight-singing strategy"></a>Sight-singing strategy</h2>
<ol>
<li>Look at the time signature + key signature.</li>
<li>Hum the tonic note (the examiner gives it).</li>
<li>Trace the contour with your finger silently.</li>
<li>Sing <strong>slowly</strong> with the rhythm correct.</li>
<li>Don't worry about perfect pitch — relative shape matters more than absolute tuning at Grade 4.</li>
</ol>
