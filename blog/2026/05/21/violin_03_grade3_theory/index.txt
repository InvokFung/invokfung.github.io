11:T5fbd,<p>Grade 3 ABRSM violin is the gateway between &quot;beginner who can play tunes&quot; and &quot;real student&quot;. Two new flat keys appear (B♭ and E♭), the three minor scales must be distinguished, a hint of position shifting is introduced, and bowings begin to diversify (slurred staccato, hooked bowing). The repertoire shifts toward Baroque dances and early Classical pieces.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → 02 Grade 2 → <strong>03 Grade 3</strong> (this post) → 04 Grade 4 → … → 08 Grade 8.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Play two-octave scales of G, D, A, B♭ majors and one-octave E♭ major.</li>
<li>Play D, G, A natural and harmonic minors (one or two octaves as syllabus).</li>
<li>Distinguish the three minor forms by ear and on paper.</li>
<li>Use <strong>slurred staccato</strong> and <strong>hooked bowing</strong>.</li>
<li>Prepare for 3rd position shifting (theoretical introduction).</li>
<li>Identify interval <strong>quality</strong> (major, minor, perfect) at sight.</li>
<li>Play 5–7 Grade 3 pieces with confident phrasing.</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-B♭-major-two-octaves"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-B♭-major-two-octaves" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves)"></a>Chapter 1 — B♭ major (two octaves)</h1>
<p>B♭ has two flats: B♭ and E♭. Translated to violin fingering:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">G string:  G   <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=A   <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=B♭(low 2!)   <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=C</span><br><span class="line">D string:  D   <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=E♭(low 1!)  <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=F   <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=G</span><br><span class="line">A string:  A   <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=B♭(low 1!)  <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=C   <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=D</span><br><span class="line">E string:  E   <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=F   <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=G   <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=A</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Wait — that's three &quot;low&quot; fingers across the scale. The hand shape changes per string.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> the rule for any key with flats: <strong>drop the finger that plays the flatted note</strong> half a finger-width toward the nut. For B♭ major, that's 2 on G string, 1 on D, 1 on A. Memorise the <strong>pattern of which finger is low</strong>, not the abstract scale.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Top note: B♭5 (4th finger on E in 1st position).</p>
<h2 id="Why-this-key-matters"><a href="#Why-this-key-matters" class="headerlink" title="Why this key matters"></a>Why this key matters</h2>
<p>B♭ is the <strong>first key violinists genuinely struggle with</strong>. The hand has to relearn its default spacing. Master B♭ by Grade 3, and every flat key after becomes intuitive.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-E♭-major-one-octave"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-E♭-major-one-octave" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave)"></a>Chapter 2 — E♭ major (one octave)</h1>
<p>E♭ has three flats: B♭, E♭, A♭. One octave only at Grade 3.</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">D string:  <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=G   (start position: 3rd finger E♭? Most editions start on E♭ on D string)</span><br><span class="line">A string:  <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=B♭(low) <span class="hljs-attribute">2</span>=C <span class="hljs-attribute">3</span>=D♭? — varies</span><br><span class="line">E string:  <span class="hljs-attribute">1</span>=E♭? — usually only goes <span class="hljs-keyword">to</span> B♭5 max</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Different editions fingerprint this differently. The point: 3 flats means 3 &quot;low&quot; finger placements. Get the <em>pattern of lowness</em> right and your fingers will land.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I tried to memorise E♭ as &quot;B♭, E♭, A♭&quot; abstractly. Fix: I stopped naming the flats and just thought &quot;low-low-low&quot; in the right places. Faster, more reliable.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-The-three-minor-forms"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-The-three-minor-forms" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — The three minor forms"></a>Chapter 3 — The three minor forms</h1>
<p>Grade 2 introduced <strong>natural minor</strong>. Grade 3 adds <strong>harmonic</strong> and (sometimes) <strong>melodic</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="Natural-minor-review"><a href="#Natural-minor-review" class="headerlink" title="Natural minor (review)"></a>Natural minor (review)</h2>
<p>Same notes as the relative major. A natural minor:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span>   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span>   C   D   E   F   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span>   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<h2 id="Harmonic-minor-—-raises-the-7th"><a href="#Harmonic-minor-—-raises-the-7th" class="headerlink" title="Harmonic minor — raises the 7th"></a>Harmonic minor — raises the 7th</h2>
<p>The leading-tone effect that classical music expects:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span>   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">B</span>   C   D   E   F   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">G</span>♯   <span class="hljs-selector-tag">A</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>That raised 7th creates an <strong>augmented 2nd</strong> between F and G♯ (3 semitones — a full step + half-step). This gives harmonic minor its Middle-Eastern or Spanish flavour.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> harmonic minor sounds <em>exotic</em> compared to natural minor. Sing both ascending — natural feels &quot;modal/folk&quot;, harmonic feels &quot;classical/dramatic&quot;. After ten A/B comparisons, your ear locks the difference.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Melodic-minor-—-raises-6th-and-7th-going-up-naturals-coming-down"><a href="#Melodic-minor-—-raises-6th-and-7th-going-up-naturals-coming-down" class="headerlink" title="Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down"></a>Melodic minor — raises 6th and 7th going up, naturals coming down</h2>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-variable">Ascending</span><span class="hljs-operator">:</span> <span class="hljs-variable">A</span> <span class="hljs-variable">B</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">C</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">D</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">E</span> <span class="hljs-variable">F</span>♯ <span class="hljs-variable">G</span>♯ <span class="hljs-variable">A</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-variable">Descending</span><span class="hljs-operator">:</span> <span class="hljs-variable">A</span> <span class="hljs-variable">G</span> <span class="hljs-variable">F</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">E</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">D</span> <span class="hljs-built_in">C</span> <span class="hljs-variable">B</span> <span class="hljs-variable">A</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Two different note sets in one scale. Composers chose this in the Baroque to handle melodic lines without the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd &quot;bump&quot;.</p>
<p>Grade 3 typically requires melodic minor only on paper; full playing comes Grade 4.</p>

<thead>

<th>Relative minor</th>

</tr>

<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Slurred-staccato"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Slurred-staccato" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato"></a>Chapter 4 — Slurred staccato</h1>
<p>A new bowing. Notation: slur (curved line) + dot under each note within the slur.</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">   .  .  .  .</span><br><span class="line">♪‿♪‿♪‿♪    = play <span class="hljs-number">4</span> notes <span class="hljs-keyword">in</span> <span class="hljs-literal">one</span> bow, but <span class="hljs-keyword">each</span> separated <span class="hljs-keyword">by</span> <span class="hljs-keyword">a</span> tiny pause</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The bow stays on the string; the wrist makes a small <strong>stop-start motion</strong> for each note while moving in one direction.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Where I got stuck:</em> I lifted the bow between notes — that's <em>staccato volante</em> (flying staccato), Grade 6 stuff. Fix: practise on open strings, bow stays on the string, only the wrist pulses. Picture a bow that &quot;hesitates&quot; 4 times in one stroke.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Hooked-bowing"><a href="#Hooked-bowing" class="headerlink" title="Hooked bowing"></a>Hooked bowing</h2>
<p>Two unequal notes (often dotted-quaver + semiquaver) on <strong>one bow</strong> with a tiny stop between them:</p>
<pre><code><figure class="highlight plaintext"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="plaintext"><span class="line">♪.  𝅘𝅥𝅯   &lt;-- <span class="hljs-keyword">both </span>on down-<span class="hljs-keyword">bow, </span>stop <span class="hljs-keyword">between </span>them</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Used to keep the dotted figure on the same bow direction throughout a phrase. Saves bow-direction confusion.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Shifting-a-theoretical-preview"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Shifting-a-theoretical-preview" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview"></a>Chapter 5 — Shifting: a theoretical preview</h1>
<p>True shifting (moving the hand to 2nd/3rd/etc. positions) is Grade 4 territory, but Grade 3 introduces the concept and may use one shift in a piece or as a &quot;step out&quot; of 1st position.</p>
<h2 id="Why-shift"><a href="#Why-shift" class="headerlink" title="Why shift?"></a>Why shift?</h2>
<ol>
<li>Reach higher notes the 1st position can't.</li>
<li>Stay on <strong>one string</strong> for a phrase (changing tone colour).</li>
<li>Make awkward fingerings easier.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="What-3rd-position-feels-like"><a href="#What-3rd-position-feels-like" class="headerlink" title="What 3rd position feels like"></a>What 3rd position feels like</h2>
<p>In 3rd position, the <strong>1st finger plays what the 3rd finger plays in 1st position</strong>. So on the A string:</p>
<ul>
<li>1st position: 3rd finger = D5.</li>
<li>3rd position: 1st finger = D5.</li>
</ul>
<p>The hand moves up the neck by about 1 inch.</p>
<h2 id="First-shift-exercise"><a href="#First-shift-exercise" class="headerlink" title="First shift exercise"></a>First shift exercise</h2>
<p>On the A string:</p>
<ol>
<li>Open A, then 3rd finger D (1st position).</li>
<li>Slide the <strong>1st finger</strong> up the string until it sounds D.</li>
<li>Now you're in 3rd position. Play 2 (E) and 3 (F♯).</li>
</ol>
<p>Don't lift; <strong>slide</strong> smoothly. The thumb travels with the hand.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Trick:</em> the thumb moves at the same time as the fingers. If the thumb stays back, you get a stretched, painful hand. Practise the shift with eyes closed, only feeling the smooth slide. Listen for the slide pitch — at first it'll glissando, later it'll be silent.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Grade 3 may include one piece with a marked shift; Grade 4 requires the technique.</p>

<h2 id="The-5-perfect-intervals-6-majorminor-pairs-tritone"><a href="#The-5-perfect-intervals-6-majorminor-pairs-tritone" class="headerlink" title="The 5 &quot;perfect&quot; intervals + 6 major/minor pairs + tritone"></a>The 5 &quot;perfect&quot; intervals + 6 major/minor pairs + tritone</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Perfect</strong>: 1st, 4th, 5th, 8ve. Stable, &quot;pure&quot; sound.</li>
<li><strong>Major / minor</strong>: 2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th. Have two flavours.</li>
<li><strong>Tritone</strong>: unstable, called <em>diabolus in musica</em> historically.</li>
</ul>

<p><em>Trick:</em> the song hooks for intervals — sing the first two notes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Major 2nd: &quot;Happy Birthday&quot; (&quot;Happy&quot;)</li>
<li>Minor 3rd: &quot;Greensleeves&quot; (&quot;A-las&quot;)</li>
<li>Major 3rd: &quot;When the Saints&quot; (&quot;Oh when&quot;)</li>
<li>Perfect 4th: &quot;Here Comes the Bride&quot;</li>
<li>Perfect 5th: &quot;Twinkle, Twinkle&quot; (&quot;Twinkle&quot;)</li>
<li>Minor 6th: &quot;The Entertainer&quot; (&quot;ta-ta&quot;)</li>
<li>Major 6th: &quot;My Bonnie&quot;</li>
<li>Octave: &quot;Somewhere Over the Rainbow&quot; (&quot;Some-where&quot;)</li>
</ul>
<p>Memorise one song per interval; identify intervals in 1 second.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>The Bach Gavottes (both G major and G minor in Suzuki 3) are based on dance forms. Listen to Janine Jansen or Hilary Hahn play any Bach Partita gavotte. Notice how the dance accent (beat 3 of 4/4) is <strong>felt</strong> rather than punched.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-Aural-Listening-Skills"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-Aural-Listening-Skills" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills"></a>Chapter 8 — Aural / Listening Skills</h1>
<p>Grade 3 aural expands to:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sing back</strong> a 4–5 note phrase (more contour).</li>
<li><strong>Identify</strong> major / minor / chromatic.</li>
<li><strong>Identify</strong> key features: same, different, or modified (transposition or change in dynamics).</li>
<li><strong>Clap</strong> rhythms with dotted figures.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Pitch-matching-pressure-test"><a href="#Pitch-matching-pressure-test" class="headerlink" title="Pitch matching pressure-test"></a>Pitch matching pressure-test</h2>
<p>The examiner plays a note on the piano in a key you haven't been told. You must identify it and play it on the violin. Practise daily for 30 seconds; by Grade 3 exam, you should pick the right string + finger 90% of the time within 5 seconds.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Common-Grade-3-Pitfalls"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Common-Grade-3-Pitfalls" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Common Grade 3 Pitfalls"></a>Chapter 9 — Common Grade 3 Pitfalls</h1>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Pitfall</th>
<th>Fix</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Low-finger pattern slips back to normal</td>
<td>Drill the low-finger shape on open string mute; visualise the hand position</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Confusing harmonic minor (raised 7) with melodic (raised 6+7 up)</td>
<td>Always play them adjacent — A/B compare daily</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Slurred staccato becomes lift-staccato</td>
<td>Watch bow hair: must stay on string</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Shifting causes glissando in performance</td>
<td>Slow practice with silent shift (lighten thumb during slide)</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Tritone identification wrong (called Perfect 4 or 5)</td>
<td>Memorise &quot;Maria&quot; (West Side Story) opening: it's a tritone</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Ignoring dotted-rhythm bow distribution</td>
<td>Pencil-mark bow direction + amount for first read</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Bow arm tension on B♭ scale</td>
<td>Stop, drop arm to side, shake, restart</td>
</tr>
