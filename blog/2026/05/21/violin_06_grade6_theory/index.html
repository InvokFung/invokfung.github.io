Violin Notes — 06 · Grade 6 Double Stops, Higher Positions, Chromatic Scales

Created2026-05-21|Updated2026-05-21|Music
|Post View:

Grade 6 ABRSM violin is where the instrument doubles itself. Double stops — two strings sounded together — become a syllabus requirement. The right hand learns flying staccato, the left hand uses all positions through 5th regularly, and chromatic scales add 12-note fluency. The repertoire is now full Baroque sonatas, mid-Romantic showpieces, and the first complete concerto movements expected at concert level.

Series map: 00 Beginner → 01 Grade 1 → … → 05 Grade 5 → 06 Grade 6 (this post) → 07 Grade 7 → 08 Grade 8.

Learning Outcome

  • Play 3rds and 6ths double stops through one octave on selected keys.
  • Play chromatic scale (one or two octaves) starting on G or A.
  • Use 1st–5th positions fluently in a single piece.
  • Play three-octave scales in G, A, B♭ major (and some minor).
  • Use staccato volante ("flying staccato") in showpieces.
  • Identify ornaments + execute trills, mordents, turns idiomatically.
  • Perform a complete sonata movement + a Romantic showpiece.

Chapter 1 — Double stops: the violin singing in two voices

A double stop is two strings bowed simultaneously, each producing a different pitch. The bow must contact both strings at once.

Bow angle for double stops

The bow normally tilts to one side to contact one string. For double stops, the bow lies flat on both strings at the same angle. Bow arm height adjusts to suit.

Easy double stops: open string + stopped string

Examples:

  • Open D + 3rd finger A on D = A4 + D5 (a 4th)
  • Open A + 1st finger B on E = B5 + open A (depends; usually combined notes)

These are the entry-level double stops; you've been hearing them in pieces since Grade 3 (as the drone effect).

Stopped double stops: 3rds

To get a 3rd, you need adjacent fingers giving notes 3 letters apart. In 3rd position on A + E:

The Grade 6 syllabus typically asks for a one-octave scale in 3rds in keys like G major.

6ths

Two-finger spread; the two fingers are usually on different strings, one slightly behind the other.

Where I got stuck: my fingers couldn't independently stop adjacent strings at different positions. Fix: very slow practice with a tuner on each note separately, then together. 5 minutes per day, 6 weeks, before any scale in 3rds was reliable.

Chapter 2 — Chromatic scale

12 semitones in one octave, played either with traditional fingering or sliding-finger chromatic.

Traditional fingering (G chromatic ascending, one octave on G + D string)

The same finger plays two consecutive semitones by sliding (1 plays G♯ then A; 2 plays A♯ then B). The finger slides up about ½ a finger-width.

Galamian's chromatic fingering

Uses a different finger pattern that avoids slides. Faster but harder to learn.

Trick: the slide-finger chromatic is more idiomatic and what most pieces expect. Practise it very slowly with metronome. The "click" of the finger sliding between two semitones should be inaudible — the bow disguises it.

Chapter 3 — Flying staccato (staccato volante)

A series of bouncing staccato notes all on one bow direction, the bow lifting between each.

Distinct from slurred staccato (which stays on the string). Flying staccato lifts.

How to learn it

  1. Solid up-bow staccato first (easier direction). 4 notes per bow at slow tempo.
  2. Each note: bow stops and lifts a millimetre, then drops for the next note.
  3. Build to 8 notes per bow.
  4. Reverse to down-bow staccato.

Used famously in: Sarasate Zigeunerweisen, Wieniawski concertos, encore showpieces.

Trick: the wrist + fingers produce the "stop+lift" motion; the arm stays steady. If your arm is jerking, you're forcing it. Relax, slow down.

Chapter 4 — Higher positions: 6th, 7th, half position

Half position

The hand sits below 1st position — 1st finger plays where the open string normally sounds (or a semitone above). Used for low chromatic passages.

6th and 7th positions

6th pos on A: 1=G♯5.
7th pos on A: 1=A5 (an octave above open A).

In 7th position, the hand sits over the body of the violin. Thumb adjusts to a different position; left hand has limited mobility. Used mostly for very high passages in concertos and showpieces.

Where I got stuck: my left wrist collapsed in 7th position — the angle felt wrong. Fix: explicitly lift the left elbow forward (more underneath the violin) when shifting above 5th. The hand pivots more from underneath.

Chapter 5 — Ornaments executed idiomatically

Grade 6 demands playing, not just identifying, ornaments.

Trill (tr or ⟵⟶)

Rapid alternation between the written note and the note a step above. In Baroque: starts on the upper note. In Classical/Romantic: starts on the written note.

The 2nd finger trills (lift-drop) while bow sustains. Trill speed: 6–10 oscillations per second. Practise slowly first, build speed.

Mordent (𝆚)

Quick three-note ornament: written note → step below (or above) → written note.

Turn (∾)

Four-note ornament: above-note → written → below-note → written.

Appoggiatura (small note before main note)

Takes half the value of the following note in Classical music.

Acciaccatura (small note with slash through stem)

A "crush" note — played as fast as possible, then immediately to the main note.

Chapter 7 — Aural / Listening Skills

Grade 6 aural expands:

  • Sing/play back 4-bar phrases with modulations.
  • Identify modulations to dominant, subdominant, relative minor.
  • Identify chord progressions including II-V-I, secondary dominants.
  • Identify formal sections (binary, ternary, rondo).
  • Sight-sing 8-bar phrase with chromatic notes.

Modulation identification

Listen for the new key's tonic as the resolution point. The dominant of the new key (V/V if modulating to dominant) often precedes it.

Trick: practise modulation identification using piano hymnals or simple chorales. Sing the new tonic when it arrives. Build the habit of locating "the new home".

Chapter 8 — Common Grade 6 Pitfalls

Pitfall Fix
Double stop sounds scratchy Bow weight balanced between strings; even contact
Double stop intonation wrong Tune each note separately first; combine slowly
Chromatic scale uneven rhythm Metronome with subdivisions; slow practice
Flying staccato becomes legato Visible lift between notes — film yourself
Trill speed inconsistent Slow trill drill: 60 bpm @ 4 notes per beat → 8 → 12
7th position cramped Lift left elbow more; pivot wrist position
Ornaments start wrong (Baroque vs Classical) Mark the start note above the trill in pencil
Sonata movement memory failure mid-performance Memorise in chunks (4 bars per session)

Useful Shortcut

Cheat-sheet

What How
Double stop bow Flat on both strings, balanced weight
3rds Two fingers, one per string, 3 letters apart
6ths Two fingers, different strings, slight stagger
Chromatic finger slide Same finger plays two semitones (½ finger-width)
Flying staccato Bow lifts between each note, same direction
Half position 1st finger lower than 1st position
7th position Above 5th; elbow lifts forward
Trill (Baroque) Start on upper note
Trill (Classical) Start on written note
Appoggiatura Half the value of the main note

Daily routine (90 min)

Block Time Content
Tune 2 min Tuner + 5ths
Bow warm-up 8 min Long bows + spiccato + flying staccato drill
Vibrato + tone 5 min Slow vibrato on each string in turn
Scales (single notes) 10 min 1 three-octave + 1 two-octave
Scales (double stops) 10 min 3rds + 6ths in selected key
Chromatic scale 5 min Slow → fast metronome
Shifting drill 5 min 1↔3↔5↔7 silent shifts
Étude 15 min Kreutzer or Mazas
Sight-read 5 min New 16–24 bar piece
Repertoire 20 min Full sonata or concerto movement
Cool-down 5 min Slow open string + favourite phrase
