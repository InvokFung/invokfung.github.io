11:Te326,<p>The slicer is where almost all of your decisions actually live. The printer executes; the slicer decides. Yet most people use it as a box with a &quot;quality&quot; dropdown, which means the interesting settings — the ones that decide whether a part is strong, accurate, fast or pretty — are being chosen by a vendor who has never seen your part. This post opens the box: what the slicer computes, what the G-code it emits actually says, and what each of the two hundred settings is really controlling. The organising principle throughout is that a slicer setting is only worth changing if you can state the physical mechanism it acts through.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → <strong>02 Slicing in Depth</strong> (this post) → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Learning-Outcome"><a href="#Learning-Outcome" class="headerlink" title="Learning Outcome"></a>Learning Outcome</h1>
<ul>
<li>Describe the slicer's computation from mesh to G-code, stage by stage, and say which stage a given artefact comes from.</li>
<li>Read G-code fluently enough to find a layer change, a retraction, a temperature command and an extrusion, and to compute how much filament a move deposits.</li>
<li>Choose layer height with reference to nozzle diameter, and state precisely what layer height does and does not control.</li>
<li>Explain why extrusion width is decoupled from nozzle diameter, and use that decoupling deliberately.</li>
<li>Set perimeter count, perimeter order and seam placement from the mechanism rather than from habit.</li>
<li>Specify solid skins by thickness rather than by layer count.</li>
<li>Compare infill patterns honestly, separating what is measurable at hobby level from what is not.</li>
<li>Reason about speed and acceleration well enough to know when a speed setting is not being reached at all.</li>
<li>Use modifiers, per-region settings and the quality features (ironing, adaptive layers, fuzzy skin, Arachne) for the cases each was built for.</li>
<li>Maintain a profile discipline that makes a good print reproducible six months later.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-What-a-slicer-actually-computes"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-What-a-slicer-actually-computes" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes"></a>Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mesh</strong> — the triangle soup the slicer receives. Must be <strong>manifold</strong> (watertight, every edge shared by exactly two faces, consistent normals) for slicing to be well defined.</li>
<li><strong>Layer polygon</strong> — the set of closed 2D contours produced by intersecting the mesh with a horizontal plane at one Z. Outer contours are the part; inner contours are holes.</li>
<li><strong>Offset (inset)</strong> — shrinking a polygon inward by a fixed distance. Perimeter generation is repeated offsetting.</li>
<li><strong>Perimeter / wall</strong> — a closed extrusion loop following the layer's contour.</li>
<li><strong>Skin / solid layer</strong> — fully dense region on a top or bottom surface.</li>
<li><strong>Sparse infill</strong> — the low-density interior lattice.</li>
<li><strong>Toolpath</strong> — an ordered sequence of moves with extrusion amounts attached.</li>
<li><strong>Seam</strong> — the point where a closed perimeter loop starts and ends.</li>
<li><strong>Extrusion width</strong> — the intended width of one deposited road. A slicer parameter, not a nozzle property.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-pipeline"><a href="#The-pipeline" class="headerlink" title="The pipeline"></a>The pipeline</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span><br><span class="line">13</span><br><span class="line">14</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">1  Load mesh          triangles in; validate and repair</span><br><span class="line">                      (non-manifold edges, flipped normals, self-intersections)</span><br><span class="line">2  Transform          orientation, scale, placement on the bed</span><br><span class="line">3  Slice              intersect with a plane at each Z → closed 2D polygons per layer</span><br><span class="line">4  Perimeters         offset each contour inward by ½ width, then by 1 width, ...</span><br><span class="line">5  Region split       remaining interior → sparse infill region</span><br><span class="line">                      minus everything within N layers of a top/bottom face → solid skin</span><br><span class="line">6  Skin detection     boolean of this layer&#x27;s region against the layers above and below</span><br><span class="line">7  Supports           overhang detection → support volume → support toolpaths + interface</span><br><span class="line">8  Ordering           which island first, which loop first, where the seam goes,</span><br><span class="line">                      travel routing, retraction decisions</span><br><span class="line">9  Extrusion amounts  for each path: length × cross-section ÷ filament cross-section</span><br><span class="line">10 Emit               G-code with per-feature speeds, accelerations, fan and temperature</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Two things are worth noticing about this list.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3 is why mesh quality matters.</strong> A plane–mesh intersection only yields closed loops if the mesh is closed. A hole in the mesh gives an open polyline, which the slicer must guess how to close — and its guess is where the weird stray extrusion in the middle of your part comes from. Fix the model, not the slicer settings.</p>
<p><strong>Step 5 is why &quot;top layers&quot; behaves unintuitively.</strong> Solid skin is not a property of the top face; it is a boolean result computed per layer. A shallow dome has a top surface at nearly every layer, so it gets skin over a large part of its height, and that is why gently sloped surfaces are slow and material-hungry.</p>
<h2 id="The-extrusion-amount"><a href="#The-extrusion-amount" class="headerlink" title="The extrusion amount"></a>The extrusion amount</h2>
<p>For each extrusion path, the slicer computes how many millimetres of <em>filament</em> to feed:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">E = (path_length × road_cross_section × flow) / filament_cross_section</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">filament_cross_section = π × (filament_diameter / 2)²</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The road cross-section is where slicers differ slightly. A naive model treats it as a rectangle, <code>width × height</code>. A better one treats it as a rectangle with semicircular ends — the shape a squashed extrudate actually takes:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">rectangle model:  A = w × h</span><br><span class="line">stadium model:    A = (w − h) × h + π × (h/2)²</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>For a 0.45 mm wide, 0.20 mm tall road these differ by around 10 %. That difference is real, it is one reason two slicers produce visibly different results from the same settings, and it is exactly the kind of systematic error your flow calibration from post 01 absorbs — which is why flow is calibrated <em>in the slicer you actually use</em>.</p>

<ul>
<li>The <code>;LAYER_CHANGE</code> and <code>;TYPE:</code> lines are <strong>comments</strong>. The printer ignores them; the slicer's preview, and any post-processing script, rely on them entirely. Cura uses <code>;LAYER:n</code> instead — different dialect, same idea.</li>

</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Layer-height-and-extrusion-width"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Layer-height-and-extrusion-width" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width"></a>Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width</h1>
<h2 id="Layer-height-versus-nozzle-diameter"><a href="#Layer-height-versus-nozzle-diameter" class="headerlink" title="Layer height versus nozzle diameter"></a>Layer height versus nozzle diameter</h2>
<p>The widely used rule of thumb is a practical ceiling of roughly <strong>75–80 % of nozzle diameter</strong> — about 0.30–0.32 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical constant, and it exists for a mechanical reason worth understanding.</p>
<p>The flat annular face around the nozzle orifice acts as a <strong>shoe</strong>. It rides above the previous layer at the layer height and irons the emerging extrudate down into it, which is what creates the wide contact patch and the pressure that drives interdiffusion (post 00). As layer height approaches nozzle diameter, the shoe stops touching the extrudate at all: material simply falls out of the orifice as a round noodle, keys poorly to the layer below, and stacks unstably.</p>
<p>Caveats in both directions:</p>
<ul>
<li>High-flow hotends and internally-restructured nozzles change the melting side of the problem but not the shoe geometry. Some users run higher ratios successfully; verify on your own machine before designing around it.</li>
<li>Going very thin has its own floor. Below roughly a quarter of nozzle diameter, small Z errors and bed-mesh residuals become a large fraction of the layer, and print time grows without a proportional gain in quality.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-first-layer-exception"><a href="#The-first-layer-exception" class="headerlink" title="The first-layer exception"></a>The first-layer exception</h2>
<p>The first layer routinely ignores the rule and runs thicker — commonly 0.2–0.3 mm even in fine profiles — for two reasons: a thicker layer swallows small bed irregularities and mesh residuals, and it produces a larger contact patch for adhesion. It is also frequently printed <strong>wider</strong> than other layers for the same reason.</p>
<h2 id="What-layer-height-does-and-does-not-control"><a href="#What-layer-height-does-and-does-not-control" class="headerlink" title="What layer height does and does not control"></a>What layer height does and does not control</h2>

<p>On strength, resist the confident claim in either direction. Thinner layers give more interfaces but each one is smaller, cools differently and reheats more often. The published results are material- and geometry-dependent, and the effect is generally much smaller than the effect of orientation (post 03) or of wall count. Spend your attention there instead.</p>
<h2 id="Extrusion-width-is-not-nozzle-diameter"><a href="#Extrusion-width-is-not-nozzle-diameter" class="headerlink" title="Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter"></a>Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter</h2>
<p>This surprises people, and it is the single most useful conceptual unlock in the slicer.</p>
<p>The width of a deposited road is set by <strong>how much material you push per unit of travel and how high the nozzle is</strong>, not by the orifice diameter. Push more, and the extrudate spreads sideways under the shoe until it is wider. Push less, and it is narrower. The orifice only limits how fast you can push it.</p>
<p>Usable range is roughly <strong>100–200 % of nozzle diameter</strong>. Below about 100 %, the road becomes unreliable and the nozzle drags. Above about 200 %, the extrudate escapes past the shoe's control and the surface becomes lumpy.</p>
<p>How to use the decoupling deliberately:</p>

<td>narrower roads follow corners more tightly and give better dimensional control on the visible surface</td>
</tr>

<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Walls-count-order-seams-gap-fill"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Walls-count-order-seams-gap-fill" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill"></a>Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill</h1>
<h2 id="Perimeter-count"><a href="#Perimeter-count" class="headerlink" title="Perimeter count"></a>Perimeter count</h2>
<p>Wall thickness ≈ perimeter count × extrusion width. Two perimeters at 0.45 mm gives a 0.9 mm wall; four gives 1.8 mm.</p>
<p>Perimeter count is usually a better investment than infill density for stiffness and strength, and the reason is elementary beam mechanics: in bending, material's contribution to stiffness scales with the square of its distance from the neutral axis. The walls are the material furthest from the neutral axis. Infill in the middle of the section contributes comparatively little. Going from two walls to four on a bracket typically does more than doubling infill, and costs less time and material.</p>
<p>Typical practice: 2 walls for decorative parts, 3–4 for general functional parts, 5+ for parts under real load or that need to be watertight or tapped.</p>
<h2 id="Perimeter-order"><a href="#Perimeter-order" class="headerlink" title="Perimeter order"></a>Perimeter order</h2>
<p>Two orders, both defensible:</p>
<p><strong>Inner first, external last</strong> (the usual default). The external perimeter is laid against already-solid inner walls, so it has something to lean on, and nothing is printed afterwards to disturb it. Generally better surface quality and better behaviour on overhanging walls.</p>
<p><strong>External first.</strong> The outer wall is laid before any inner wall can push it outward, which can improve dimensional accuracy on some machines. Costs surface quality and overhang reliability.</p>
<p>Slicers in the PrusaSlicer lineage expose this as an &quot;external perimeters first&quot; toggle, and some offer a hybrid ordering. The honest guidance is that the effect size is machine-dependent: print a test part both ways, measure it with calipers <em>and</em> look at it, and decide for your machine. Post 05 returns to the accuracy side of this.</p>
<h2 id="Seams"><a href="#Seams" class="headerlink" title="Seams"></a>Seams</h2>
<p>Every closed loop must start and stop somewhere. At that point the extruder transitions from zero pressure to steady pressure and back, and the result is a small defect — a blob, a notch, or both — stacked on every layer.</p>
<p>Seam placement strategies:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Strategy</th>
<th>Result</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Nearest</strong></td>
<td>seam goes wherever travel is shortest; fastest, scattered, unpredictable</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Aligned</strong></td>
<td>seams stack into a single vertical line; ugly but <em>predictable</em>, and you can rotate it to the back</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Rear</strong></td>
<td>forced to the back of the plate</td>
</tr>
<tr>

<p>Mechanically, a stack of aligned seams is a line of small stress concentrations and reduced wall continuity, so on a pressure-bearing or load-bearing part, do not let the seam line up with the region of highest stress.</p>
<p>Reducing the seam artefact itself: tune pressure advance (post 01), and use the slicer's <strong>wipe</strong> and <strong>coasting</strong> options, which stop extruding slightly before the end of the loop and move the nozzle to smear the remainder. Some slicers additionally offer a <strong>scarf</strong> or ramped seam that spreads the transition over a Z ramp instead of a single point; where available, it is the most effective option for a visible cylindrical surface.</p>
<h2 id="Gap-fill"><a href="#Gap-fill" class="headerlink" title="Gap fill"></a>Gap fill</h2>
<p>When a region is too narrow for another whole perimeter but too wide to ignore, classic perimeter generation leaves a gap and fills it with a short, thin extrusion. Gap fill is a common source of noise, vibration, blobs and stuttering motion, because it is many tiny moves with rapid flow changes.</p>
<p>The modern answer is variable-width extrusion (Chapter 6), which mostly eliminates the problem by widening the perimeters themselves to close the gap.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Skins-and-infill"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Skins-and-infill" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Skins and infill"></a>Chapter 5 — Skins and infill</h1>
<h2 id="The-thickness-not-count-rule"><a href="#The-thickness-not-count-rule" class="headerlink" title="The thickness-not-count rule"></a>The thickness-not-count rule</h2>
<p>Solid layers are specified in most slicers as a <em>count</em>, which is a trap. What matters physically is total <strong>thickness</strong> of solid material.</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">5 top layers × 0.20 mm = 1.00 mm   ← fine</span><br><span class="line">5 top layers × 0.12 mm = 0.60 mm   ← the same profile, now too thin</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Halving your layer height silently halves your top skin. The result is <strong>pillowing</strong>: the top surface sags into the infill voids and shows holes and bumps.</p>
<p>The rule: <strong>specify a thickness, then derive the count.</strong> Around 0.8–1.2 mm of top solid is a sensible general target; more if infill density is low, because thin skin has to bridge longer unsupported spans. Modern slicers have a &quot;minimum shell thickness&quot; setting that enforces this automatically — turn it on. Bottom skins can be thinner, because the build plate supports them.</p>
<p><strong>Monotonic ordering</strong> for the top solid layer is worth enabling where available: it forces solid infill lines to be laid in a consistent direction so each new road overlaps the previous one the same way, producing a visually uniform surface instead of a patchwork.</p>
<h2 id="Infill-patterns-honestly"><a href="#Infill-patterns-honestly" class="headerlink" title="Infill patterns, honestly"></a>Infill patterns, honestly</h2>
<p>First, what is <em>reliably measurable</em> at hobby level and what is not.</p>
<p><strong>Measurable, repeatable, and worth optimising:</strong> estimated and actual print time, material mass (weigh the part), whether the pattern self-intersects within a layer (which makes the nozzle run over its own deposits, adding noise and artefacts), and whether the pattern is 2D (identical every layer) or 3D (varies with Z, giving out-of-plane structure).</p>
<p><strong>Not reliably measurable without a test rig, standardised coupons and repeated samples:</strong> the small percentage strength differences between patterns that appear in blog comparisons. These figures are usually a single specimen, in a single load direction, on a single machine, and they do not generalise. Treat them as anecdote.</p>

<h2 id="Density"><a href="#Density" class="headerlink" title="Density"></a>Density</h2>
<p>Strength does not scale linearly with infill density; the returns diminish, and the time cost does not. The efficient move is almost always: <strong>add walls before you add infill</strong>, for the second-moment-of-area reason in Chapter 4. Typical practice:</p>
<ul>
<li>Decorative: 5–15 %, or lightning.</li>
<li>General functional: 15–25 % with 3–4 walls.</li>
<li>Loaded parts: 30–50 % with 5+ walls — and read post 07 before assuming that is enough.</li>
<li>100 %: rarely correct. Over-packing solid infill causes over-extrusion, dimensional growth and worse surfaces. If you need a solid part, increase walls until they meet in the middle.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Motion-and-the-quality-features"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Motion-and-the-quality-features" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features"></a>Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features</h1>
<h2 id="Per-feature-speeds"><a href="#Per-feature-speeds" class="headerlink" title="Per-feature speeds"></a>Per-feature speeds</h2>
<p>Slicers set a different speed for external perimeters, internal perimeters, solid infill, sparse infill, top solid, bridges, gap fill and travel. The important asymmetry:</p>
<p><strong>Outer-wall speed dominates surface quality, and costs very little total time.</strong> The external perimeter is the only extrusion anyone sees; it is where ringing lands, where pressure-advance error is visible, and where corner rounding shows. It is also a small fraction of total extruded volume on most parts. Halving outer-wall speed typically adds a few percent to print time and produces a visibly better part. This is the best quality-per-minute trade in the whole slicer.</p>
<p>Conversely, sparse infill speed is nearly free to raise — until it hits the volumetric flow ceiling you measured in post 01.</p>
<h2 id="Acceleration-is-the-real-print-time-variable"><a href="#Acceleration-is-the-real-print-time-variable" class="headerlink" title="Acceleration is the real print-time variable"></a>Acceleration is the real print-time variable</h2>
<p>On small parts the toolhead rarely reaches its commanded speed at all. The distance needed to accelerate from rest to speed <code>v</code> at acceleration <code>a</code> is:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">d = v² / (2a)</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">a = 4000 mm/s², v = 250 mm/s  →  d = 62500 / 8000 = 7.81 mm</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Accelerating <em>and</em> decelerating therefore needs about 15.6 mm of travel. A 10 mm wall segment never sees 250 mm/s. Its peak speed is <code>√(a × d/2)</code> = <code>√(4000 × 5)</code> ≈ 141 mm/s, and its average is lower still.</p>
<p>Consequences:</p>
<ul>
<li>Raising the speed setting on a part made of short segments changes nothing. Raising acceleration does.</li>
<li>Raising acceleration excites resonance, which is what input shaping (post 01) exists to manage. The two settings are coupled.</li>
<li><strong>Jerk</strong> (Marlin's classic model), <strong>junction deviation</strong> and Klipper's <strong>square corner velocity</strong> all describe the same thing: how much speed is retained through a direction change. Raising it speeds up detailed parts and adds ringing.</li>
<li><strong>Minimum layer time</strong> / &quot;slow down for short layers&quot; forces small cross-sections to take long enough to solidify. It is a cooling setting wearing a speed setting's clothes.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Cooling"><a href="#Cooling" class="headerlink" title="Cooling"></a>Cooling</h2>
<p>Set from material, as measured in post 01. Briefly: PLA wants a lot, PETG a moderate amount, ABS/ASA very little in an enclosure, nylon and PC little. Cooling buys overhang and detail quality and pays in layer adhesion; that trade never goes away.</p>
<h2 id="The-quality-features"><a href="#The-quality-features" class="headerlink" title="The quality features"></a>The quality features</h2>
<p><strong>Ironing.</strong> After the top solid layer is complete, the nozzle sweeps the surface again at very low flow, remelting and smoothing it. Produces a genuinely flat, near-glossy top face. Costs time proportional to top-surface area, and can push a bead of material to the edges if flow is too high. Only applies to flat, horizontal top surfaces.</p>
<p><strong>Adaptive layer height.</strong> Layer height varies with local surface slope — thin layers where the surface is shallow (where stepping is visible), thick where it is vertical (where stepping is invisible). Excellent on organic and curved shapes, useless on prisms, and it complicates any setting that is expressed per layer. Some slicers additionally let you paint a height range by hand.</p>
<p><strong>Fuzzy skin.</strong> The outer wall is deliberately jittered in XY by a small random amount, giving a matte, textured surface. It hides layer lines, hides seams, and improves grip on handles. Costs a little time and a little dimensional precision on the fuzzed surface — never fuzz a mating face. Better slicers let you paint it onto selected surfaces only.</p>
<p><strong>Variable-width extrusion (Arachne).</strong> The perimeter generator that, instead of laying fixed-width loops and dumping the remainder into gap fill, varies each loop's width to fill the available space exactly. Thin walls, tapering ribs, embossed text and small features all improve substantially. It originated in the Cura engine and became the default perimeter generator in PrusaSlicer from version 2.5; OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio, both of PrusaSlicer lineage, carry it too. The classic fixed-width generator remains selectable and is occasionally preferred when you want perfectly uniform road width for its own sake.</p>
<h2 id="Modifiers-and-per-region-settings"><a href="#Modifiers-and-per-region-settings" class="headerlink" title="Modifiers and per-region settings"></a>Modifiers and per-region settings</h2>
<p>The most under-used capability in every modern slicer. You are not limited to one setting per part.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Per-object settings</strong> — different infill, walls or supports for each object on the plate.</li>
<li><strong>Height ranges</strong> — different settings for a Z band, e.g. more solid layers around a threaded region.</li>
<li><strong>Modifier meshes</strong> — drop a box or cylinder into the scene and any setting inside its volume is overridden. The canonical use is 100 % infill only inside a screw boss, or extra walls only around a bearing seat.</li>
<li><strong>Negative volumes</strong> — subtract geometry at slice time without touching the CAD model. Useful for a quick clearance test.</li>
<li><strong>Support blockers and enforcers</strong> — force supports on or off in a region rather than fighting the global threshold.</li>
<li><strong>Painted seams and painted supports</strong> — direct manual control on the model surface.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> a modifier mesh is the correct answer almost every time you are tempted to raise a global setting for the sake of one feature. Raising global infill from 20 % to 60 % because one boss needs to be solid costs you the whole part's print time; a modifier box costs you the boss.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Profiles-slicers-and-estimates"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Profiles-slicers-and-estimates" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Profiles, slicers, and estimates"></a>Chapter 7 — Profiles, slicers, and estimates</h1>
<h2 id="Profile-discipline"><a href="#Profile-discipline" class="headerlink" title="Profile discipline"></a>Profile discipline</h2>
<p>The rule: <strong>start from the vendor profile, change one variable, keep notes.</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Begin from the printer manufacturer's profile for your machine and a material profile for your filament. These encode machine limits you may not know about.</li>
<li>Change one thing. Print the test that isolates it. Record the result.</li>
<li>Save as a new named profile whose name says what changed — <code>PETG 0.2 walls4 outer25</code> beats <code>PETG final v3 REAL final</code>.</li>
<li>Export the project as a <strong>3MF with settings embedded</strong> for anything you might reprint. A bare STL plus a memory of what you did is not reproducible; a project file is.</li>
<li>Keep the same notebook you started in post 01. Slicer changes and machine changes belong in the same log because they interact.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="Print-time-and-material-cost-estimates"><a href="#Print-time-and-material-cost-estimates" class="headerlink" title="Print time and material cost estimates"></a>Print time and material cost estimates</h2>
<p><strong>Print time.</strong> The slicer estimates by simulating its own motion model: every move, with acceleration and jerk applied. It is a good model, and the error comes from the places where the printer does not behave like the model — firmware acceleration limits that clamp the slicer's requests, input shaping smoothing that changes effective acceleration, per-move minimum times, and heating and homing at the start.</p>
<p>The right response is calibration rather than complaint:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">correction_factor = actual_print_time / estimated_print_time</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Time three real prints of different sizes, compute the ratio, and apply it</span><br><span class="line">mentally from then on. It is usually stable for a given printer + profile.</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>If the ratio is wildly different for small versus large parts, the discrepancy is in acceleration handling, not in the estimator.</p>
<p><strong>Material.</strong> The slicer reports extruded filament length and usually a mass. Mass comes from <code>volume × density</code>, and the density value lives in the filament profile. For unfilled PLA and PETG the default is usually close; for carbon- or glass-filled, metal-filled, wood-filled or foaming materials it can be significantly off. Verify once per material by weighing a printed part on a kitchen scale and comparing.</p>
<p><strong>Cost.</strong> The naive figure is <code>mass(g) / 1000 × spool_price</code>. The realistic figure adds electricity, amortised machine and consumable cost (nozzles, plates, belts), and — the term everyone omits — a <strong>failure allowance</strong>. If one print in eight fails at 70 % completion, your effective material cost is meaningfully higher than the slicer's number, and your effective time cost is much higher.</p>

<p>Your profile requests 200 mm/s for internal perimeters, and your printer's acceleration is set to 3000 mm/s². Compute the distance needed to accelerate to 200 mm/s, and the peak speed actually reached on a 12 mm straight segment.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Accel distance to 200 mm/s:</span><br><span class="line">  d = v² / (2a) = 200² / (2 × 3000) = 40000 / 6000 = 6.67 mm</span><br><span class="line">  accelerate + decelerate = 13.3 mm  →  a 12 mm segment never reaches 200 mm/s.</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Peak on a 12 mm segment (accelerate for half, decelerate for half):</span><br><span class="line">  v_peak = √(a × d/2) = √(3000 × 6) = √18000 = 134 mm/s</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">So the commanded 200 mm/s is fiction for this feature. <h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Walls-versus-infill"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Walls-versus-infill" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Walls versus infill"></a>Exercise 4 — Walls versus infill</h2>
<p>Slice the same bracket twice: (a) 2 walls with 40 % gyroid infill, (b) 4 walls with 15 % gyroid infill. Record estimated print time and estimated mass for each. Then state which you would choose for a load-bearing application and justify it in terms of where the material sits relative to the neutral axis.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Audit-your-own-G-code"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Audit-your-own-G-code" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Audit your own G-code"></a>Exercise 5 — Audit your own G-code</h2>
<p>Slice a 20 mm cube. In the G-code, find: the first <code>M109</code>, the first layer change, one retraction, one external-perimeter extrusion, and the fan command. For the external-perimeter move you found, compute the road cross-section from the move length and <code>E</code> value, and compare it to your configured extrusion width and layer height.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Modifier-mesh"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Modifier-mesh" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Modifier mesh"></a>Exercise 6 — Modifier mesh</h2>
<p>Take any part with a screw boss or a bearing seat. Instead of raising global infill, add a modifier box around that feature only, set to 100 % infill and extra walls. Compare estimated print time to the global-setting version. Record the difference.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-7-—-Calibrate-your-time-estimate"><a href="#Exercise-7-—-Calibrate-your-time-estimate" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 7 — Calibrate your time estimate"></a>Exercise 7 — Calibrate your time estimate</h2>
<p>Time three real prints of clearly different sizes with a stopwatch or the printer's own log. Compute <code>actual / estimated</code> for each. Report whether the ratio is consistent, and if it is not, state which direction the discrepancy runs with part size and what that implies about acceleration handling.</p>
