3D Printing Course — 02 · Slicing in Depth

Created2026-08-26|Updated2026-08-26|Engineering > 3D Printing
|Post View:

The slicer is where almost all of your decisions actually live. The printer executes; the slicer decides. Yet most people use it as a box with a "quality" dropdown, which means the interesting settings — the ones that decide whether a part is strong, accurate, fast or pretty — are being chosen by a vendor who has never seen your part. This post opens the box: what the slicer computes, what the G-code it emits actually says, and what each of the two hundred settings is really controlling. The organising principle throughout is that a slicer setting is only worth changing if you can state the physical mechanism it acts through.

Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth (this post) → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts.

Learning Outcome

  • Describe the slicer's computation from mesh to G-code, stage by stage, and say which stage a given artefact comes from.
  • Read G-code fluently enough to find a layer change, a retraction, a temperature command and an extrusion, and to compute how much filament a move deposits.
  • Choose layer height with reference to nozzle diameter, and state precisely what layer height does and does not control.
  • Explain why extrusion width is decoupled from nozzle diameter, and use that decoupling deliberately.
  • Set perimeter count, perimeter order and seam placement from the mechanism rather than from habit.
  • Specify solid skins by thickness rather than by layer count.
  • Compare infill patterns honestly, separating what is measurable at hobby level from what is not.
  • Reason about speed and acceleration well enough to know when a speed setting is not being reached at all.
  • Use modifiers, per-region settings and the quality features (ironing, adaptive layers, fuzzy skin, Arachne) for the cases each was built for.
  • Maintain a profile discipline that makes a good print reproducible six months later.

Chapter 1 — What a slicer actually computes

Terminology

  • Mesh — the triangle soup the slicer receives. Must be manifold (watertight, every edge shared by exactly two faces, consistent normals) for slicing to be well defined.
  • Layer polygon — the set of closed 2D contours produced by intersecting the mesh with a horizontal plane at one Z. Outer contours are the part; inner contours are holes.
  • Offset (inset) — shrinking a polygon inward by a fixed distance. Perimeter generation is repeated offsetting.
  • Perimeter / wall — a closed extrusion loop following the layer's contour.
  • Skin / solid layer — fully dense region on a top or bottom surface.
  • Sparse infill — the low-density interior lattice.
  • Toolpath — an ordered sequence of moves with extrusion amounts attached.
  • Seam — the point where a closed perimeter loop starts and ends.
  • Extrusion width — the intended width of one deposited road. A slicer parameter, not a nozzle property.

The pipeline

Two things are worth noticing about this list.

Step 3 is why mesh quality matters. A plane–mesh intersection only yields closed loops if the mesh is closed. A hole in the mesh gives an open polyline, which the slicer must guess how to close — and its guess is where the weird stray extrusion in the middle of your part comes from. Fix the model, not the slicer settings.

Step 5 is why "top layers" behaves unintuitively. Solid skin is not a property of the top face; it is a boolean result computed per layer. A shallow dome has a top surface at nearly every layer, so it gets skin over a large part of its height, and that is why gently sloped surfaces are slow and material-hungry.

Chapter 2 — Reading the G-code

G-code is a flat list of commands. There is no loop, no variable, no geometry — every move is spelled out. A 40 MB file is 40 MB of G1 lines.

The commands that matter

Command Meaning
G0 / G1 linear move. G0 nominally means "rapid, no extrusion"; most printer firmwares treat the two identically, and many slicers emit G1 for everything
X Y Z target coordinates in mm
E the extruder axis, in mm of filament — not mm of travel
F feedrate in mm per minute. Divide by 60 to get the mm/s in your slicer UI
M82 / M83 absolute / relative extrusion mode. Relative (M83) is now the common choice
G92 E0 reset the extruder position counter (used by absolute-mode slicers to avoid huge E values)
M104 S<t> set nozzle temperature and continue
M109 S<t> set nozzle temperature and wait until reached
M140 / M190 same pair for the bed
M106 S<0-255> part cooling fan speed
M107 fan off
G28 home axes
M204 set acceleration
M900 K<v> linear advance (Marlin)

A layer change, annotated

Reading it back:

  • The ;LAYER_CHANGE and ;TYPE: lines are comments. The printer ignores them; the slicer's preview, and any post-processing script, rely on them entirely. Cura uses ;LAYER:n instead — different dialect, same idea.
  • The layer change is a Z move surrounded by a retract/unretract pair. That pair is where seam blobs are born.
  • The travel move has no E word, so the extruder does not turn.
  • E0.5063 on a 15.176 mm move means 0.5063 mm of 1.75 mm filament was fed over that distance. Working backwards gives the road cross-section, which is how you audit what your slicer is really doing.

Trick: when a slicer setting does not appear to be doing what you expect, slice a 20 mm cube, open the G-code in a text editor and search for the feature. Five minutes of reading beats an hour of speculation, and G-code cannot lie about what it is going to do.

Chapter 3 — Layer height and extrusion width

Layer height versus nozzle diameter

The widely used rule of thumb is a practical ceiling of roughly 75–80 % of nozzle diameter — about 0.30–0.32 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle. It is a rule of thumb, not a physical constant, and it exists for a mechanical reason worth understanding.

The flat annular face around the nozzle orifice acts as a shoe. It rides above the previous layer at the layer height and irons the emerging extrudate down into it, which is what creates the wide contact patch and the pressure that drives interdiffusion (post 00). As layer height approaches nozzle diameter, the shoe stops touching the extrudate at all: material simply falls out of the orifice as a round noodle, keys poorly to the layer below, and stacks unstably.

Caveats in both directions:

  • High-flow hotends and internally-restructured nozzles change the melting side of the problem but not the shoe geometry. Some users run higher ratios successfully; verify on your own machine before designing around it.
  • Going very thin has its own floor. Below roughly a quarter of nozzle diameter, small Z errors and bed-mesh residuals become a large fraction of the layer, and print time grows without a proportional gain in quality.

The first-layer exception

The first layer routinely ignores the rule and runs thicker — commonly 0.2–0.3 mm even in fine profiles — for two reasons: a thicker layer swallows small bed irregularities and mesh residuals, and it produces a larger contact patch for adhesion. It is also frequently printed wider than other layers for the same reason.

What layer height does and does not control

Layer height controls Layer height does not control
Visible stepping on sloped and curved surfaces detail in the XY plane — that is nozzle diameter and extrusion width
The Z resolution of features (a 0.15 mm step cannot exist in a 0.2 mm layer) hole roundness or XY dimensional accuracy
Print time — roughly inversely, but not exactly wall thickness
The number of layer interfaces per millimetre of height whether a part is strong (interface count and interface quality pull in opposite directions; see below)
Overhang behaviour, indirectly (each layer's unsupported step scales with height) support requirement

On strength, resist the confident claim in either direction. Thinner layers give more interfaces but each one is smaller, cools differently and reheats more often. The published results are material- and geometry-dependent, and the effect is generally much smaller than the effect of orientation (post 03) or of wall count. Spend your attention there instead.

Extrusion width is not nozzle diameter

This surprises people, and it is the single most useful conceptual unlock in the slicer.

The width of a deposited road is set by how much material you push per unit of travel and how high the nozzle is, not by the orifice diameter. Push more, and the extrudate spreads sideways under the shoe until it is wider. Push less, and it is narrower. The orifice only limits how fast you can push it.

Usable range is roughly 100–200 % of nozzle diameter. Below about 100 %, the road becomes unreliable and the nozzle drags. Above about 200 %, the extrudate escapes past the shoe's control and the surface becomes lumpy.

How to use the decoupling deliberately:

Setting Typical choice Why
First layer wider than default more contact patch, better adhesion, more tolerance of bed variation
External perimeter at or slightly below nozzle diameter narrower roads follow corners more tightly and give better dimensional control on the visible surface
Internal perimeters above nozzle diameter (often ~110–120 %) fewer, fatter roads for the same wall thickness — faster and fewer internal interfaces
Solid infill above nozzle diameter fewer passes to fill an area
Sparse infill wider still infill is invisible; width buys speed directly
Support interface narrower finer surface where the part rests on it

Common pitfall: setting an extrusion width and forgetting that flow calibration (post 01) targets a specific width. Change your external perimeter width and your measured wall thickness changes, which looks like a flow error and is not.

Chapter 4 — Walls: count, order, seams, gap fill

Perimeter count

Wall thickness ≈ perimeter count × extrusion width. Two perimeters at 0.45 mm gives a 0.9 mm wall; four gives 1.8 mm.

Perimeter count is usually a better investment than infill density for stiffness and strength, and the reason is elementary beam mechanics: in bending, material's contribution to stiffness scales with the square of its distance from the neutral axis. The walls are the material furthest from the neutral axis. Infill in the middle of the section contributes comparatively little. Going from two walls to four on a bracket typically does more than doubling infill, and costs less time and material.

Typical practice: 2 walls for decorative parts, 3–4 for general functional parts, 5+ for parts under real load or that need to be watertight or tapped.

Perimeter order

Two orders, both defensible:

Inner first, external last (the usual default). The external perimeter is laid against already-solid inner walls, so it has something to lean on, and nothing is printed afterwards to disturb it. Generally better surface quality and better behaviour on overhanging walls.

External first. The outer wall is laid before any inner wall can push it outward, which can improve dimensional accuracy on some machines. Costs surface quality and overhang reliability.

Slicers in the PrusaSlicer lineage expose this as an "external perimeters first" toggle, and some offer a hybrid ordering. The honest guidance is that the effect size is machine-dependent: print a test part both ways, measure it with calipers and look at it, and decide for your machine. Post 05 returns to the accuracy side of this.

Seams

Every closed loop must start and stop somewhere. At that point the extruder transitions from zero pressure to steady pressure and back, and the result is a small defect — a blob, a notch, or both — stacked on every layer.

Seam placement strategies:

Strategy Result
Nearest seam goes wherever travel is shortest; fastest, scattered, unpredictable
Aligned seams stack into a single vertical line; ugly but predictable, and you can rotate it to the back
Rear forced to the back of the plate
Random scattered; hides the line at the cost of scattering small defects across the whole surface
Painted you choose, per region, in the slicer — put it inside a corner where geometry hides it

Mechanically, a stack of aligned seams is a line of small stress concentrations and reduced wall continuity, so on a pressure-bearing or load-bearing part, do not let the seam line up with the region of highest stress.

Reducing the seam artefact itself: tune pressure advance (post 01), and use the slicer's wipe and coasting options, which stop extruding slightly before the end of the loop and move the nozzle to smear the remainder. Some slicers additionally offer a scarf or ramped seam that spreads the transition over a Z ramp instead of a single point; where available, it is the most effective option for a visible cylindrical surface.

Gap fill

When a region is too narrow for another whole perimeter but too wide to ignore, classic perimeter generation leaves a gap and fills it with a short, thin extrusion. Gap fill is a common source of noise, vibration, blobs and stuttering motion, because it is many tiny moves with rapid flow changes.

The modern answer is variable-width extrusion (Chapter 6), which mostly eliminates the problem by widening the perimeters themselves to close the gap.

Chapter 5 — Skins and infill

The thickness-not-count rule

Solid layers are specified in most slicers as a count, which is a trap. What matters physically is total thickness of solid material.

Halving your layer height silently halves your top skin. The result is pillowing: the top surface sags into the infill voids and shows holes and bumps.

The rule: specify a thickness, then derive the count. Around 0.8–1.2 mm of top solid is a sensible general target; more if infill density is low, because thin skin has to bridge longer unsupported spans. Modern slicers have a "minimum shell thickness" setting that enforces this automatically — turn it on. Bottom skins can be thinner, because the build plate supports them.

Monotonic ordering for the top solid layer is worth enabling where available: it forces solid infill lines to be laid in a consistent direction so each new road overlaps the previous one the same way, producing a visually uniform surface instead of a patchwork.

Infill patterns, honestly

First, what is reliably measurable at hobby level and what is not.

Measurable, repeatable, and worth optimising: estimated and actual print time, material mass (weigh the part), whether the pattern self-intersects within a layer (which makes the nozzle run over its own deposits, adding noise and artefacts), and whether the pattern is 2D (identical every layer) or 3D (varies with Z, giving out-of-plane structure).

Not reliably measurable without a test rig, standardised coupons and repeated samples: the small percentage strength differences between patterns that appear in blog comparisons. These figures are usually a single specimen, in a single load direction, on a single machine, and they do not generalise. Treat them as anecdote.

Pattern Structure Print speed Material at equal density Honest strength note
Rectilinear / lines 2D, alternating direction per layer fastest lowest no intersections within a layer; layers only touch at crossings, so poor out-of-plane contribution
Grid 2D, both directions in the same layer fast low nozzle crosses its own deposits at every intersection — can cause knocking and artefacts at high density
Triangles 2D, three directions moderate moderate good in-plane isotropy; more material at nodes
Honeycomb 2D hexagonal slow — many direction changes moderate high in-plane stiffness for its mass, paid for in time
Cubic / adaptive cubic 3D, stacked cubes on a corner moderate moderate; adaptive saves a lot contributes in all three axes; adaptive subdivides only near surfaces
Gyroid 3D, smooth continuous non-intersecting surface moderate moderate near-isotropic by construction; smooth continuous motion; the common default for functional parts, and good for flexibles because it compresses evenly
Lightning tree-like, grows only where a top surface needs support very fast very low contributes essentially nothing structurally — that is the point. For decorative parts and hollow bodies
Concentric follows the contour moderate moderate used for flexible parts, where following the wall keeps the part uniformly compliant

Density

Strength does not scale linearly with infill density; the returns diminish, and the time cost does not. The efficient move is almost always: add walls before you add infill, for the second-moment-of-area reason in Chapter 4. Typical practice:

  • Decorative: 5–15 %, or lightning.
  • General functional: 15–25 % with 3–4 walls.
  • Loaded parts: 30–50 % with 5+ walls — and read post 07 before assuming that is enough.
  • 100 %: rarely correct. Over-packing solid infill causes over-extrusion, dimensional growth and worse surfaces. If you need a solid part, increase walls until they meet in the middle.

Chapter 6 — Motion, and the quality features

Per-feature speeds

Slicers set a different speed for external perimeters, internal perimeters, solid infill, sparse infill, top solid, bridges, gap fill and travel. The important asymmetry:

Outer-wall speed dominates surface quality, and costs very little total time. The external perimeter is the only extrusion anyone sees; it is where ringing lands, where pressure-advance error is visible, and where corner rounding shows. It is also a small fraction of total extruded volume on most parts. Halving outer-wall speed typically adds a few percent to print time and produces a visibly better part. This is the best quality-per-minute trade in the whole slicer.

Conversely, sparse infill speed is nearly free to raise — until it hits the volumetric flow ceiling you measured in post 01.

Acceleration is the real print-time variable

On small parts the toolhead rarely reaches its commanded speed at all. The distance needed to accelerate from rest to speed v at acceleration a is:

Accelerating and decelerating therefore needs about 15.6 mm of travel. A 10 mm wall segment never sees 250 mm/s. Its peak speed is √(a × d/2) = √(4000 × 5) ≈ 141 mm/s, and its average is lower still.

Consequences:

  • Raising the speed setting on a part made of short segments changes nothing. Raising acceleration does.
  • Raising acceleration excites resonance, which is what input shaping (post 01) exists to manage. The two settings are coupled.
  • Jerk (Marlin's classic model), junction deviation and Klipper's square corner velocity all describe the same thing: how much speed is retained through a direction change. Raising it speeds up detailed parts and adds ringing.
  • Minimum layer time / "slow down for short layers" forces small cross-sections to take long enough to solidify. It is a cooling setting wearing a speed setting's clothes.

Cooling

Set from material, as measured in post 01. Briefly: PLA wants a lot, PETG a moderate amount, ABS/ASA very little in an enclosure, nylon and PC little. Cooling buys overhang and detail quality and pays in layer adhesion; that trade never goes away.

The quality features

Ironing. After the top solid layer is complete, the nozzle sweeps the surface again at very low flow, remelting and smoothing it. Produces a genuinely flat, near-glossy top face. Costs time proportional to top-surface area, and can push a bead of material to the edges if flow is too high. Only applies to flat, horizontal top surfaces.

Adaptive layer height. Layer height varies with local surface slope — thin layers where the surface is shallow (where stepping is visible), thick where it is vertical (where stepping is invisible). Excellent on organic and curved shapes, useless on prisms, and it complicates any setting that is expressed per layer. Some slicers additionally let you paint a height range by hand.

Fuzzy skin. The outer wall is deliberately jittered in XY by a small random amount, giving a matte, textured surface. It hides layer lines, hides seams, and improves grip on handles. Costs a little time and a little dimensional precision on the fuzzed surface — never fuzz a mating face. Better slicers let you paint it onto selected surfaces only.

Variable-width extrusion (Arachne). The perimeter generator that, instead of laying fixed-width loops and dumping the remainder into gap fill, varies each loop's width to fill the available space exactly. Thin walls, tapering ribs, embossed text and small features all improve substantially. It originated in the Cura engine and became the default perimeter generator in PrusaSlicer from version 2.5; OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio, both of PrusaSlicer lineage, carry it too. The classic fixed-width generator remains selectable and is occasionally preferred when you want perfectly uniform road width for its own sake.

Modifiers and per-region settings

The most under-used capability in every modern slicer. You are not limited to one setting per part.

  • Per-object settings — different infill, walls or supports for each object on the plate.
  • Height ranges — different settings for a Z band, e.g. more solid layers around a threaded region.
  • Modifier meshes — drop a box or cylinder into the scene and any setting inside its volume is overridden. The canonical use is 100 % infill only inside a screw boss, or extra walls only around a bearing seat.
  • Negative volumes — subtract geometry at slice time without touching the CAD model. Useful for a quick clearance test.
  • Support blockers and enforcers — force supports on or off in a region rather than fighting the global threshold.
  • Painted seams and painted supports — direct manual control on the model surface.

Trick: a modifier mesh is the correct answer almost every time you are tempted to raise a global setting for the sake of one feature. Raising global infill from 20 % to 60 % because one boss needs to be solid costs you the whole part's print time; a modifier box costs you the boss.

Brims, rafts and skirts

Feature What it is Use when
Skirt one or more loops around the part, not touching it always — it purges the nozzle and gives you a free look at the first layer before the part starts
Brim a flat collar attached to the part's first layer small footprints, tall thin parts, warp-prone materials, sharp corners that lift. Costs cleanup and can mar the bottom edge
Raft a full printed platform under the part a genuinely bad bed surface, materials that will not adhere directly, or parts whose footprint is nearly nothing. Costs material, time and the bottom-face finish

"Mouse ear" brims — small discs added only at the corners that lift — give most of the benefit of a full brim with a fraction of the cleanup, and are available as a brim type or can be added as small modifier objects.

Chapter 7 — Profiles, slicers, and estimates

Profile discipline

The rule: start from the vendor profile, change one variable, keep notes.

  1. Begin from the printer manufacturer's profile for your machine and a material profile for your filament. These encode machine limits you may not know about.
  2. Change one thing. Print the test that isolates it. Record the result.
  3. Save as a new named profile whose name says what changed — PETG 0.2 walls4 outer25 beats PETG final v3 REAL final.
  4. Export the project as a 3MF with settings embedded for anything you might reprint. A bare STL plus a memory of what you did is not reproducible; a project file is.
  5. Keep the same notebook you started in post 01. Slicer changes and machine changes belong in the same log because they interact.

The slicer landscape, by capability

All four of the mainstream desktop slicers below share substantial code lineage, so most setting names transfer between them.

Slicer Lineage Notable capabilities
PrusaSlicer descends from Slic3r; open source strong documentation; Arachne default from 2.5; organic supports from 2.6; modifier meshes, height ranges, negative volumes; wide community printer profiles
OrcaSlicer fork in the PrusaSlicer/Bambu Studio lineage; open source built-in guided calibration suite (flow, pressure advance, temperature, maximum volumetric speed, retraction); broad printer support; fast adoption of new features
Cura Ultimaker; open source very large printer profile library; plugin marketplace; the engine where tree supports, lightning infill and the Arachne generator originated
Bambu Studio PrusaSlicer lineage tight integration with its own hardware, multi-material and multi-plate workflow

Choose on the capability you need — the calibration suite, the profile library, the plugin, the hardware integration — rather than on identity. Being able to read a setting in one and find it in another is a more valuable skill than loyalty to any of them.

Print time and material cost estimates

Print time. The slicer estimates by simulating its own motion model: every move, with acceleration and jerk applied. It is a good model, and the error comes from the places where the printer does not behave like the model — firmware acceleration limits that clamp the slicer's requests, input shaping smoothing that changes effective acceleration, per-move minimum times, and heating and homing at the start.

The right response is calibration rather than complaint:

If the ratio is wildly different for small versus large parts, the discrepancy is in acceleration handling, not in the estimator.

Material. The slicer reports extruded filament length and usually a mass. Mass comes from volume × density, and the density value lives in the filament profile. For unfilled PLA and PETG the default is usually close; for carbon- or glass-filled, metal-filled, wood-filled or foaming materials it can be significantly off. Verify once per material by weighing a printed part on a kitchen scale and comparing.

Cost. The naive figure is mass(g) / 1000 × spool_price. The realistic figure adds electricity, amortised machine and consumable cost (nozzles, plates, belts), and — the term everyone omits — a failure allowance. If one print in eight fails at 70 % completion, your effective material cost is meaningfully higher than the slicer's number, and your effective time cost is much higher.

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1 — Compute an extrusion

A move is 15.20 mm long, extrusion width 0.45 mm, layer height 0.20 mm, filament 1.75 mm, flow 1.00. Compute the E value under both the rectangle and stadium cross-section models, and state the percentage difference.

Solution

Exercise 2 — Does the move reach its speed?

Your profile requests 200 mm/s for internal perimeters, and your printer's acceleration is set to 3000 mm/s². Compute the distance needed to accelerate to 200 mm/s, and the peak speed actually reached on a 12 mm straight segment.

Solution

Exercise 3 — Fix a silently broken profile

You take a working 0.20 mm profile with 5 top layers and 5 bottom layers, and switch to a 0.12 mm layer height for a finer finish. The top surfaces now show holes and dimpling. Diagnose it and give the corrected values.

Solution

Exercise 4 — Walls versus infill

Slice the same bracket twice: (a) 2 walls with 40 % gyroid infill, (b) 4 walls with 15 % gyroid infill. Record estimated print time and estimated mass for each. Then state which you would choose for a load-bearing application and justify it in terms of where the material sits relative to the neutral axis.

Exercise 5 — Audit your own G-code

Slice a 20 mm cube. In the G-code, find: the first M109, the first layer change, one retraction, one external-perimeter extrusion, and the fan command. For the external-perimeter move you found, compute the road cross-section from the move length and E value, and compare it to your configured extrusion width and layer height.

Exercise 6 — Modifier mesh

Take any part with a screw boss or a bearing seat. Instead of raising global infill, add a modifier box around that feature only, set to 100 % infill and extra walls. Compare estimated print time to the global-setting version. Record the difference.

Exercise 7 — Calibrate your time estimate

Time three real prints of clearly different sizes with a stopwatch or the printer's own log. Compute actual / estimated for each. Report whether the ratio is consistent, and if it is not, state which direction the discrepancy runs with part size and what that implies about acceleration handling.
