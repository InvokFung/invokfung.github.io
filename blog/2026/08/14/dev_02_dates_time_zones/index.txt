<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-UTC-offsets-and-IANA-zones"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-UTC-offsets-and-IANA-zones" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — UTC, offsets and IANA zones"></a>Chapter 2 — UTC, offsets and IANA zones</h1>
<h2 id="Offsets"><a href="#Offsets" class="headerlink" title="Offsets"></a>Offsets</h2>
<p>An offset says how far a clock is from UTC at one moment. <code>15:00+08:00</code> and <code>07:00Z</code> are the same instant. To convert to UTC, subtract the offset: 15:00 − 8 h = 07:00.</p>
<h2 id="Zones-are-more-than-offsets"><a href="#Zones-are-more-than-offsets" class="headerlink" title="Zones are more than offsets"></a>Zones are more than offsets</h2>
<p>The <strong>IANA time zone database</strong> (also called tzdata or the Olson database) names zones as <code>Area/Location</code>: <code>Asia/Hong_Kong</code>, <code>Europe/London</code>, <code>America/New_York</code>. Each zone stores <strong>every offset change</strong> in that region's history, plus the current rules for the future.</p>

</tbody>
</table>
<p>So an offset tells you about <strong>one moment</strong>. A zone tells you about <strong>every moment</strong>, including next year. Governments change the rules, and tzdata is updated when they do, so keep your OS, language runtime and <code>tzdata</code> packages current.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> Windows uses its own zone IDs. This Windows machine reports <code>China Standard Time</code> (&quot;Beijing, Chongqing, Hong Kong, Urumqi&quot;), and Node.js on it reports the IANA zone <code>Asia/Shanghai</code>, not <code>Asia/Hong_Kong</code>. The two zones agree today but not in 1979. Pass an explicit IANA zone to your code rather than trusting the machine.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Writing-timestamps-ISO-8601-and-RFC-3339"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Writing-timestamps-ISO-8601-and-RFC-3339" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Writing timestamps: ISO 8601 and RFC 3339"></a>Chapter 4 — Writing timestamps: ISO 8601 and RFC 3339</h1>
<p><strong>ISO 8601</strong> is the international standard for dates and times. <strong>RFC 3339</strong> is a strict, simple profile of it for the internet. Use it for every timestamp you send or store as text.</p>

<p>Notes from the RFCs:</p>
<ul>
<li>RFC 3339's grammar uses <code>T</code> between date and time. A note allows a space &quot;for the sake of readability&quot;, but many parsers only accept <code>T</code>.</li>
<li>RFC 3339 used <code>-00:00</code> to mean &quot;UTC time known, local offset unknown&quot;. RFC 9557 (2024) updates this: <code>Z</code> now carries that meaning too.</li>
<li>RFC 9557 adds bracketed suffixes such as <code>[Asia/Hong_Kong]</code>. Temporal in JavaScript (Chapter 7) prints this format.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> <code>05/06/2026</code> is 6 May in the US and 5 June in the UK. Never use a local display format for storage or APIs. ISO 8601 also sorts correctly as plain text.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="The-year-2038-problem"><a href="#The-year-2038-problem" class="headerlink" title="The year 2038 problem"></a>The year 2038 problem</h2>
<p>A <strong>signed 32-bit</strong> count of seconds tops out at <code>2^31 − 1 = 2147483647</code>, which is <code>2038-01-19T03:14:07Z</code>. One second later it wraps to a negative number (a date in 1901). Old C code with a 32-bit <code>time_t</code>, file formats and database columns are at risk. For example, MySQL documents its <code>TIMESTAMP</code> type range as <code>'1970-01-01 00:00:01'</code> UTC to <code>'2038-01-19 03:14:07'</code> UTC. Use 64-bit integers or proper date types.</p>
<p>For comparison, a JavaScript <code>Date</code> holds milliseconds in a range of exactly ±100,000,000 days around 1970 (±8.64 × 10^15 ms), so it reaches the year 275760.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-The-storage-rule"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-The-storage-rule" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — The storage rule"></a>Chapter 6 — The storage rule</h1>
<ol>
<li><strong>Store instants in UTC.</strong> Use a real date-time type (<code>timestamptz</code> in PostgreSQL, BSON Date in MongoDB, <code>Instant</code> in Java) or ISO 8601 text with <code>Z</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the zone when the user's local time matters.</strong> For a future meeting or a &quot;every day at 09:00&quot; rule, store the wall-clock time and the IANA zone name. Work out the instant when you need it, so new DST rules are picked up.</li>
<li><strong>Convert to local time only at the edges</strong>: when you display, and when you read user input.</li>
<li><strong>Store pure dates as dates.</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>Why rule 2 matters: a meeting at 09:00 <code>Europe/London</code> is <code>09:00Z</code> on 2026-12-07 but <code>08:00Z</code> on 2026-06-08. A stored UTC instant would put the June meeting at the wrong local hour.</p>

<ul>
<li>It reached <strong>Stage 4</strong> at TC39 in March 2026 and is listed for <strong>ECMAScript 2027</strong>.</li>
<li>Shipped in <strong>Chrome 144</strong> (and Edge), <strong>Firefox 139</strong>, <strong>Node.js 26</strong> and <strong>Deno 2.7</strong>, per MDN's compatibility data.</li>

<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-Cron-jobs-and-DST"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-Cron-jobs-and-DST" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — Cron jobs and DST"></a>Chapter 8 — Cron jobs and DST</h1>
<p>Classic cron runs jobs by the machine's <strong>local</strong> clock. On a machine in a DST zone, a job at <code>30 2 * * *</code> meets the gap in spring and the overlap in autumn. The Debian <code>cron(8)</code> manual describes its behaviour for clock changes of less than 3 hours:</p>
<ul>
<li>Moving <strong>forward</strong>: &quot;those jobs which would have run in the time that was skipped will be run soon after the change.&quot;</li>
<li>Moving <strong>back</strong>: &quot;those jobs that fall into the repeated time will not be re-run.&quot;</li>
<li>Jobs with <code>*</code> in the hour or minute field &quot;are run based on the new time immediately&quot;.</li>
</ul>
<p>Other schedulers behave differently, so check yours. Safe habits:</p>
<ul>
<li>Run servers in <strong>UTC</strong>. Then there is no DST at all.</li>
<li>If a job must follow local time, avoid 01:00–03:00 local in zones with DST.</li>
<li><strong>GitHub Actions</strong>: <code>on.schedule</code> cron runs in <strong>UTC</strong> by default. You can add an IANA <code>timezone:</code>; for zones with DST, GitHub's docs say a schedule inside the skipped hour advances to the next valid time (a 2:30 AM schedule runs at 3:00 AM). More in Dev Essentials 16 · CI/CD.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-9-—-Case-study-when-is-this-blogs-post-published"><a href="#Chapter-9-—-Case-study-when-is-this-blogs-post-published" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 9 — Case study: when is this blog's post published?"></a>Chapter 9 — Case study: when is this blog's post published?</h1>
<p>Every post on this blog starts with frontmatter like this:</p>
<pre><code class="language-yaml"><figure class="highlight yaml"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="yaml"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-attr">date:</span> <span class="hljs-number">2026-05-21 15:00:00</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>There is no zone. The author means 15:00 <strong>Hong Kong</strong> time. Three readers turn this text into an instant.</p>
<h2 id="Reader-1-—-gray-matter-js-yaml-YAML-11-rule"><a href="#Reader-1-—-gray-matter-js-yaml-YAML-11-rule" class="headerlink" title="Reader 1 — gray-matter / js-yaml (YAML 1.1 rule)"></a>Reader 1 — gray-matter / js-yaml (YAML 1.1 rule)</h2>
<p>The Next.js port of the blog parses frontmatter with gray-matter, which uses js-yaml. The YAML 1.1 timestamp type says: &quot;If the time zone is omitted, the timestamp is assumed to be specified in UTC.&quot; So js-yaml returns <code>2026-05-21T15:00:00.000Z</code>, which is <strong>23:00</strong> in Hong Kong. (Checked with gray-matter 4.0.3 and js-yaml 3.14.)</p>
<h2 id="Reader-2-—-the-ports-post-loader"><a href="#Reader-2-—-the-ports-post-loader" class="headerlink" title="Reader 2 — the port's post loader"></a>Reader 2 — the port's post loader</h2>

<h2 id="Reader-3-—-Hexo"><a href="#Reader-3-—-Hexo" class="headerlink" title="Reader 3 — Hexo"></a>Reader 3 — Hexo</h2>
<p>The original Hexo site has <code>timezone: ''</code> in <code>_config.yml</code>. The Hexo docs say: &quot;Hexo uses the setting on your computer by default.&quot; In Hexo 6, the front-matter parser (hexo-front-matter 3.x) reads each zone-less date as the <strong>build machine's</strong> local wall-clock time, and Hexo then re-anchors it only if <code>timezone</code> is set. The Hexo site builds on a GitHub Actions <code>ubuntu-latest</code> runner. GitHub-hosted runners run in UTC in practice (GitHub states UTC explicitly for scheduled workflows; add a <code>run: date</code> step to see it).</p>

<p>The URL date is the same in all four, because each reader keeps the written calendar date. The instant is not, and anything that shows or compares instants (a feed timestamp, a &quot;published&quot; <code>&lt;time&gt;</code> tag, a future-post filter) can differ by 8 hours.</p>
<h2 id="Where-future-fits"><a href="#Where-future-fits" class="headerlink" title="Where future fits"></a>Where <code>future</code> fits</h2>
<p>The Hexo config also has <code>future: true</code>. The docs describe it as &quot;Display future posts?&quot;, default <code>true</code>. With <code>true</code>, a post dated later than the build time is still published. With <code>false</code>, it is skipped until a later build, and &quot;later than now&quot; is judged with whatever instant the build machine computed. Near the boundary, the zone decides.</p>
<h2 id="The-lesson-pin-the-zone"><a href="#The-lesson-pin-the-zone" class="headerlink" title="The lesson: pin the zone"></a>The lesson: pin the zone</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Say the zone once, in config.</strong> For Hexo: <code>timezone: Asia/Hong_Kong</code> (an IANA name; the docs give <code>America/New_York</code> as an example). Then the build machine's zone no longer matters.</li>
<li><strong>Or write the offset in the data</strong>, such as <code>date: 2026-05-21T15:00:00+08:00</code>. This is the portable fix for YAML readers. Check each consumer first: the current hexo-front-matter source honours an explicit offset, but version 3.x (Hexo 6) reads every date's UTC fields as local wall-clock time, so the offset does not survive there.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> the port's loader is written for zone-less dates. It reads the UTC fields of whatever js-yaml returns and treats them as HKT. If posts ever gain an explicit offset, update the loader in the same change, or those dates will move by 8 hours.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Practice-Exercises"><a href="#Practice-Exercises" class="headerlink" title="Practice Exercises"></a>Practice Exercises</h1>
<h2 id="Exercise-1-—-Classify"><a href="#Exercise-1-—-Classify" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 1 — Classify"></a>Exercise 1 — Classify</h2>
<p>For each, say instant, wall-clock + zone, or calendar date: (a) a server log line, (b) a user's birthday, (c) a weekly 09:00 team call in Singapore, (d) a payment's time, (e) a public holiday.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>(a) instant (UTC). (b) calendar date. (c) wall-clock + zone: <code>09:00</code> + <code>Asia/Singapore</code> + a weekly rule. (d) instant (UTC). (e) calendar date (and it may differ by country).</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-2-—-The-London-gap"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-The-London-gap" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — The London gap"></a>Exercise 2 — The London gap</h2>
<p>What does Python print for <code>datetime(2026, 3, 29, 1, 30, tzinfo=ZoneInfo(&quot;Europe/London&quot;))</code> after a round trip through UTC?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> datetime <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> datetime, UTC</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> zoneinfo <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> ZoneInfo</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">lon = ZoneInfo(<span class="hljs-string">&quot;Europe/London&quot;</span>)</span><br><span class="line">t = datetime(<span class="hljs-number">2026</span>, <span class="hljs-number">3</span>, <span class="hljs-number">29</span>, <span class="hljs-number">1</span>, <span class="hljs-number">30</span>, tzinfo=lon)</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(t.astimezone(UTC).astimezone(lon).isoformat())   <span class="hljs-comment"># 2026-03-29T02:30:00+01:00</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>01:30 does not exist on that day (01:00 GMT jumps to 02:00 BST). With <code>fold=0</code>, Python uses the offset from before the jump (+00:00), which is 01:30Z, and that is 02:30 BST.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-3-—-Seconds-or-milliseconds"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Seconds-or-milliseconds" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — Seconds or milliseconds?"></a>Exercise 3 — Seconds or milliseconds?</h2>
<p>An API returns <code>1779346800</code>. Another returns <code>1779346800000</code>. Convert both to ISO 8601 in Python.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> datetime <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> datetime, UTC</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(datetime.fromtimestamp(<span class="hljs-number">1779346800</span>, tz=UTC).isoformat())           <span class="hljs-comment"># 2026-05-21T07:00:00+00:00</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(datetime.fromtimestamp(<span class="hljs-number">1779346800000</span> / <span class="hljs-number">1000</span>, tz=UTC).isoformat()) <span class="hljs-comment"># same instant</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>10 digits means seconds and 13 digits means milliseconds, for dates between 2001 and 2286.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Predict-the-JavaScript"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Predict-the-JavaScript" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Predict the JavaScript"></a>Exercise 4 — Predict the JavaScript</h2>
<p>With <code>TZ=America/New_York</code>, what do <code>new Date(&quot;2026-05-21&quot;).getDate()</code> and <code>new Date(&quot;2026-05-21T00:00&quot;).getDate()</code> return?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p><code>20</code> and <code>21</code>. The date-only string is parsed as UTC midnight, which is 20:00 on 20 May in New York (UTC−4 in May). The date-time string is parsed as local midnight on 21 May. To get a plain date safely, use <code>Temporal.PlainDate.from(&quot;2026-05-21&quot;)</code>, or split the string yourself.</p>
</details>

<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Pin-this-blogs-dates"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Pin-this-blogs-dates" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Pin this blog's dates"></a>Exercise 6 — Pin this blog's dates</h2>
<p>Give two ways to make <code>date: 2026-05-21 15:00:00</code> mean 15:00 Hong Kong time for every reader, and one risk of each.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<ol>
<li>Set <code>timezone: Asia/Hong_Kong</code> in the Hexo config. Risk: it only fixes Hexo. Other readers (plain js-yaml, scripts) still read the text as UTC, so the port keeps its own +8 correction.</li>
<li>Write <code>date: 2026-05-21T15:00:00+08:00</code>. Risk: every reader must honour offsets. Hexo 6's front-matter parser and the port's current loader do not, so both would need changing together.</li>
</ol>
</details>
