<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Peer review</strong> — other experts read the paper before it is accepted. They check that it is new, sound and clear. They do not re-run everything.</li>
<li><strong>Venue</strong> — where the paper appeared: a conference (SOSP, OSDI, NeurIPS), a journal, or a workshop. In computer science, top conferences often matter more than journals.</li>
<li><strong>Proceedings</strong> — the book (now a website) of all papers from one conference.</li>
<li><strong>Preprint</strong> — a version posted before or without peer review, often on arXiv.</li>
<li><strong>Artifact</strong> — the code, data or scripts behind a paper.</li>
<li><strong>DOI</strong> — a permanent ID for a paper, like <code>10.1145/1273445.1273458</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Survey paper</strong> — a paper that summarises a whole area. Gold for a newcomer.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-The-three-pass-method"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-The-three-pass-method" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — The three-pass method"></a>Chapter 2 — The three-pass method</h1>
<p>The source is S. Keshav, &quot;How to Read a Paper&quot;, <em>ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review</em> 37(3), July 2007, pages 83-84. It is two pages long. Keshav has updated it over the years; later versions (for example, one dated February 17, 2016) keep the same method.</p>
<p>The key idea: read a paper in <strong>up to three passes</strong>, not once from start to end. Each pass has its own goal. You can stop after any pass.</p>
<h2 id="First-pass-—-the-birds-eye-view-about-5-10-minutes"><a href="#First-pass-—-the-birds-eye-view-about-5-10-minutes" class="headerlink" title="First pass — the bird's-eye view (about 5-10 minutes)"></a>First pass — the bird's-eye view (about 5-10 minutes)</h2>
<p>Keshav's steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Carefully read the <strong>title, abstract and introduction</strong>.</li>
<li>Read the <strong>section and sub-section headings</strong>, and ignore everything else.</li>
<li><strong>Glance at the math</strong> (if any) to see what theory it rests on.</li>
<li>Read the <strong>conclusions</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Glance over the references</strong> and tick off the ones you have already read.</li>
</ol>
<p>At the end, you should be able to answer the <strong>five Cs</strong>:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>C</th>
<th>Question</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Category</strong></td>
<td>What type of paper is this? A measurement paper? An analysis of an existing system? A description of a research prototype?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Context</strong></td>
<td>Which other papers is it related to? What theory does it use?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Correctness</strong></td>
<td>Do the assumptions seem valid?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Contributions</strong></td>
<td>What are the main contributions?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Clarity</strong></td>
<td>Is it well written?</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Now you decide. Maybe the paper does not interest you. Maybe you lack the background. Maybe the assumptions look wrong. Stopping here is fine. Keshav says the first pass is enough for papers outside your area that may matter some day.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> the same logic works in reverse when <em>you</em> write. Most readers make only one pass. Clear headings and a tight abstract decide whether anyone reads further. (See Dev Essentials 22 · Design Docs.)</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Second-pass-—-the-content-up-to-about-an-hour"><a href="#Second-pass-—-the-content-up-to-about-an-hour" class="headerlink" title="Second pass — the content (up to about an hour)"></a>Second pass — the content (up to about an hour)</h2>
<p>Read with more care, but <strong>skip details such as proofs</strong>. Write key points and questions in the margin as you go. Two special jobs:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Look hard at the figures, diagrams and graphs.</strong> Are the axes labelled? Are there error bars, so the result is statistically meaningful? Keshav notes that mistakes like these separate rushed work from excellent work.</li>
<li><strong>Mark relevant references you have not read.</strong> They are your reading list for background.</li>
</ol>
<p>After this pass you should be able to <strong>summarise the main idea, with evidence, to someone else</strong>. Keshav says this pass takes up to an hour for an experienced reader.</p>
<p>Sometimes you still will not understand the paper. The topic may be new, the terms unfamiliar, or the writing poor. You then have three choices: (a) put it aside, (b) come back after reading background material, or (c) push on to the third pass.</p>
<h2 id="Third-pass-—-virtual-re-implementation-hours"><a href="#Third-pass-—-virtual-re-implementation-hours" class="headerlink" title="Third pass — virtual re-implementation (hours)"></a>Third pass — virtual re-implementation (hours)</h2>
<p>The key is to <strong>virtually re-implement</strong> the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work in your head (or on paper, or in code). Then compare your version with theirs. The gaps show you both the new ideas and the hidden weak points.</p>
<ul>
<li>Challenge every assumption in every statement.</li>
<li>Ask how <em>you</em> would present each idea.</li>
<li>Write down ideas for future work.</li>
</ul>
<p>Time: the 2007 version says about four or five hours for beginners and about an hour for an experienced reader. The 2016 version is less optimistic: many hours for beginners and more than an hour or two even for experienced readers.</p>
<p>At the end, you should be able to rebuild the paper's structure from memory, and point out implicit assumptions, missing citations and problems with the experiments or analysis.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-A-literature-survey-in-three-steps"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-A-literature-survey-in-three-steps" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — A literature survey in three steps"></a>Chapter 3 — A literature survey in three steps</h1>
<p>Keshav's paper also shows how to survey a new field. You may need to read tens of papers.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Seed.</strong> Use an academic search engine (Keshav names Google Scholar and CiteSeer) with good keywords. Find <strong>three to five recent, highly cited papers</strong>. Do one pass on each. Read their related-work sections. If one of them points to a recent <strong>survey paper</strong>, read that and you are nearly done.</li>
<li><strong>Find the core.</strong> Look for <strong>shared citations</strong> and <strong>repeated author names</strong> across the seed papers. These are the key papers and people. Visit the key researchers' pages and see where they publish. That tells you the <strong>top venues</strong> of the field.</li>
<li><strong>Scan the venues.</strong> Go to those conferences' recent proceedings. A quick scan finds recent strong work. These papers, plus the key papers from step 2, are the first version of your survey. Do two passes on them. If they all cite a key paper you missed, read it and repeat.</li>
</ol>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Read-figures-and-tables-first"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Read-figures-and-tables-first" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Read figures and tables first"></a>Chapter 5 — Read figures and tables first</h1>
<p>In pass 2, the figures often tell the story faster than the text. Use this checklist on every graph:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Axes:</strong> labelled? Units? Linear or <strong>log</strong> scale? Does the y-axis start at zero, or is a small gap made to look big?</li>
<li><strong>What is compared:</strong> which <strong>baseline</strong>? Is it a fair, well-tuned one, or a weak straw man?</li>
<li><strong>Spread:</strong> error bars, confidence intervals, or at least several runs? One run proves little.</li>
<li><strong>Sample:</strong> how many machines, users, datasets or trials?</li>
<li><strong>Range:</strong> does the plot stop right before things get interesting?</li>
<li><strong>Caption:</strong> can you understand the figure from the caption alone?</li>
</ul>
<p>For tables:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bold usually marks the best number. Check how big the gap to second place is, and whether it is within noise.</li>
<li>An <strong>ablation</strong> table removes one part at a time. It shows which parts of the idea actually matter.</li>
<li>Look for missing rows: a dataset or setting the method might lose on.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> before reading the evaluation text, write one sentence per figure: &quot;This shows that ___.&quot; Then read the text and compare. Your version and the authors' version will not always match.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Math is a compact language. You do not need to follow every line on pass 2. Some habits help:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Build a notation table</strong> as you read. Each symbol, its meaning, and its shape or unit.</li>
<li><strong>Read the words around the equation.</strong> Authors usually say in plain words what it does.</li>
<li><strong>Plug in small numbers.</strong> Two items, three dimensions. Work it out by hand.</li>
<li><strong>Skip proofs on pass 2.</strong> Trust the statement first; check it in pass 3.</li>
<li><strong>Turn it into code.</strong> Code forces you to decide every shape.</li>
</ol>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Judging-quality"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Judging-quality" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Judging quality"></a>Chapter 7 — Judging quality</h1>
<p>A paper is a claim with evidence, not a fact. Weigh it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Peer review</strong> is a filter, not a proof. Reviewers check that the work is new, sound and clear. They rarely re-run the experiments.</li>
<li><strong>Venue.</strong> A paper at a top venue passed a hard filter. A workshop paper is often early work. Neither guarantees the result holds.</li>
<li><strong>Preprints.</strong> arXiv states that its &quot;moderation process is not a peer-review process&quot;. A preprint may be excellent, or wrong. Check whether a later peer-reviewed version exists.</li>
<li><strong>Replication.</strong> Can others get the same result? Many CS conferences now run <strong>artifact evaluation</strong>. ACM badges mark this: <em>Artifacts Available</em> (code is public), <em>Results Reproduced</em> (another team got the main results using the authors' artifacts) and <em>Results Replicated</em> (another team got them without the authors' artifacts).</li>
<li><strong>Retractions.</strong> The Retraction Watch database has been free and open since Crossref acquired it in September 2023. Check it for papers you plan to rely on.</li>
<li><strong>Citation counts</strong> show attention, not truth. They also lag: a strong new paper can have few citations.</li>
<li><strong>Industry papers</strong> (such as Dynamo and MapReduce) describe systems at a scale you cannot rebuild. Judge them by the data they show and by what later systems learned.</li>
</ul>
<p>Red flags: vague baselines, a single run, no limitations section, claims in the abstract that the evaluation does not support, and code &quot;available on request&quot;.</p>

<h1 id="Practice-Exercises"><a href="#Practice-Exercises" class="headerlink" title="Practice Exercises"></a>Practice Exercises</h1>
<h2 id="Exercise-1-—-A-timed-first-pass"><a href="#Exercise-1-—-A-timed-first-pass" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 1 — A timed first pass"></a>Exercise 1 — A timed first pass</h2>
<p>Pick one of the three papers. Set a 10-minute timer. Do only Keshav's five first-pass steps. Stop when the timer rings.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>A good first pass reads the title, abstract and intro in full, only the headings of the body, glances at any math, reads the conclusion and scans the references. If you read a full section of the body, you went too deep. The output is a go / no-go decision, not understanding.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-2-—-Write-the-five-Cs"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-Write-the-five-Cs" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — Write the five Cs"></a>Exercise 2 — Write the five Cs</h2>
<p>Using the template in Chapter 8, write the five Cs for the paper from Exercise 1. One or two lines each.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Example for MapReduce:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Category:</strong> description of a working system, with a performance evaluation and experience report.</li>
<li><strong>Context:</strong> parallel and distributed processing; the paper compares itself with earlier parallel programming systems in its related-work section.</li>
<li><strong>Correctness:</strong> assumes cheap commodity machines that fail often, and tasks that can be split into independent map and reduce steps. Reasonable for batch jobs at Google.</li>
<li><strong>Contributions:</strong> a simple programming model; a runtime that handles splitting, scheduling and machine failures; lessons from real use.</li>
<li><strong>Clarity:</strong> high; short sections and one clear overview figure.</li>
</ul>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-3-—-Critique-a-figure"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Critique-a-figure" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — Critique a figure"></a>Exercise 3 — Critique a figure</h2>
<p>Take any results figure from your chosen paper. Answer the Chapter 5 checklist for it.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Answer each point in writing: axes and units, scale, baseline, spread (error bars or number of runs), sample size, range, and whether the caption stands alone. A useful last line: &quot;The figure supports / partly supports / does not support the claim that ___.&quot;</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Notation-to-code"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Notation-to-code" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Notation to code"></a>Exercise 4 — Notation to code</h2>
<p>Write a notation table for the attention formula, then change the code in Chapter 6 so <code>Q</code> has 3 queries. Predict the output shape before running.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p><code>Q = rng.normal(size=(3, 4))</code>. The scores are <code>(3, 3)</code> (3 queries by 3 keys), the weights are <code>(3, 3)</code>, and the output is <code>(3, 5)</code>: one row per query, <code>d_v</code> columns. <code>w.sum(axis=1)</code> prints three ones.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Mini-survey"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Mini-survey" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Mini survey"></a>Exercise 5 — Mini survey</h2>
<p>Pick a topic (for example &quot;vector databases&quot;). Find three recent, highly cited papers. Put 5-10 references from each into <code>shared_citations.py</code>.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>The references cited by two or more seeds are your core reading list. Look up their authors on dblp to see which venues come up again and again. Those are the venues to scan in Keshav's step 3.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Judge-a-preprint"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Judge-a-preprint" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Judge a preprint"></a>Exercise 6 — Judge a preprint</h2>
<p>Find an arXiv paper in your area from the last year. Is there a peer-reviewed version? Is the code public? Has anyone reproduced it?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Check the arXiv &quot;Comments&quot; and &quot;Journal reference&quot; fields, then search the title on Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar for a venue version. Look for a code link and for later papers that report re-running it. If none exist, treat the claims as unconfirmed.</p>
</details>
