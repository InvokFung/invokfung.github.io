Dev Essentials — 23 · Money Basics for Engineers — Budget, Emergency Fund, Compound Interest and Index Funds

Created2026-09-22|Updated2026-09-22|Dev Essentials
|Post View:

Engineers learn exponential growth for algorithms, then often ignore it for their own money. Compound interest works for you in savings and against you in debt. Small fees grow into large sums over decades. This post teaches the basic ideas with formulas and small Python calculators you can run on your own numbers: budgeting, an emergency fund, debt, compound interest, inflation, index funds, fees, and the mistakes people make under stress.

Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 21 Estimating → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics (this post) → 24 Desk Health. Part E · Growth outside code.

Watch out: this post is general education only, not personal financial advice. It does not recommend any product, fund, provider or asset. Account types, tax rules and pension schemes differ by country, so check your own country's regulator or government money-guidance service. For decisions about your own situation, talk to a qualified, regulated adviser.

Learning Outcome

  • Track spending and test it against the 50/30/20 rule of thumb.
  • Explain "pay yourself first" and size an emergency fund.
  • Show with code how card interest makes a small debt last for years.
  • Use A=P(1+r/n)ntA = P(1 + r/n)^{nt}A=P(1+r/n)nt and know how wrong the rule of 72 is.
  • Turn a nominal return into a real (after-inflation) return.
  • Explain stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification in plain words.
  • Measure how a 1.0% fee compares with a 0.1% fee over 30 years.
  • Describe what one Vanguard study found about lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging.
  • Name the behaviour mistakes that cost investors the most.

Chapter 1 — Track spending, then budget

You cannot plan what you do not measure. Track every outflow for one full month. Most banks let you export a CSV. Put each line into one of three buckets:

  • Needs — you would face real trouble if you did not pay: housing, basic food, transport to work, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.
  • Wants — everything else you spend on.
  • Savings — money moved to savings or investments, plus debt payments above the minimum.

The 50/30/20 split is a rule of thumb from Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book All Your Worth. It says to aim for about 50% of after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings. It is a starting point, not a law. In a high-rent city, needs may be well above 50%. That tells you where the pressure is; it does not mean you failed.

To use your own data, replace io.StringIO(DATA) with open("export.csv", encoding="utf-8") and add a bucket column. The 470 "left over" is money with no job. Unplanned money tends to disappear, so give it one (often: more savings).

Watch out: these calculators use float for teaching. For a real ledger, store money as integer cents or Decimal (see Dev Essentials 08 · Floating Point).

Chapter 2 — Pay yourself first

"Pay yourself first" means you move money to savings on payday, before you spend, instead of saving whatever is left at month end. Leftovers are usually close to zero.

The easiest way is an automatic transfer that runs the day your salary arrives. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) calls saving automatically "one of the easiest ways" to make saving consistent. It turns a daily choice into a one-time setup.

Trick: raise the automatic transfer each time your pay goes up. You never get used to spending the extra.

Chapter 3 — The emergency fund

An emergency fund is cash for surprises: job loss, a medical bill, a broken laptop. It stops a bad month from turning into high-interest debt.

  • Size. US regulator FINRA says financial planners often recommend three to six months of living expenses. This is common guidance, not a rule. People with variable income, one income in the household, or a job that is hard to replace often aim higher.
  • Where. Somewhere safe and easy to reach quickly, kept apart from spending money. It is not an investment; its job is to be there.
  • Start small. If six months feels impossible, the CFPB suggests thinking about the unexpected costs you have actually had before and what they cost. A first target of one month is still a real safety net.

Size the fund on needs, not total spending. In a real emergency, the wants stop.

Chapter 4 — High-interest debt: compounding against you

Credit card rates are high. The US Federal Reserve's G.19 release (September 2026) puts the average rate on card accounts that were charged interest at 22.15% APR for Q2 2026. Rates differ by country and by card; this number is only for scale.

APR is the yearly rate. A card with 22% APR charges roughly 22%/12≈1.83%22\% / 12 \approx 1.83\%22%/12≈1.83% per month on the unpaid balance. On a 5,000 balance, that is about 92 of interest in the first month. If you pay 100, only about 8 reduces the debt.

Paying 100 a month takes over 11 years and costs more in interest than the original debt. Paying 300 takes under 2 years. FINRA calls paying down high-rate debt, such as credit card debt, "one of the best ways" to improve your financial base. The reason is simple: clearing a 22% debt is like earning a sure 22% return, which almost no investment can promise.

Common pitfall: paying only the minimum. The minimum is designed to keep the account in good standing, not to clear the debt quickly.

Chapter 5 — Compound interest and the rule of 72

With compound interest, you earn interest on past interest. For a principal PPP, yearly rate rrr, compounding nnn times a year, for ttt years:

A=P(1+rn)ntA = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} A=P(1+nr​)nt

As nnn grows without limit, this becomes continuous compounding, A=PertA = Pe^{rt}A=Pert.

The compounding frequency matters a little. The rate and the time matter a lot.

The rule of 72 (an approximation)

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the yearly rate in percent: at 6%, about 72/6=1272 / 6 = 1272/6=12 years. The exact answer for yearly compounding is

Trick: the rule works for debt too. A card balance at 24% with no payments doubles in about 3 years.

Chapter 6 — Inflation: real versus nominal

Inflation means prices rise, so each unit of money buys less. The nominal return is the number on your statement. The real return is what you gain in buying power:

1+rreal=1+rnominal1+πsorreal≈rnominal−π1 + r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi} \qquad\text{so}\qquad r_{\text{real}} \approx r_{\text{nominal}} - \pi 1+rreal​=1+π1+rnominal​​sorreal​≈rnominal​−π

where π\piπ is the inflation rate.

  • A 5% return with 3% inflation is a real return of about 1.94%.
  • With 3% inflation, 1,000 held as cash for 10 years buys what 744 buys today.
  • Cash earning 1% during 3% inflation still loses buying power: 1,000 becomes 822 in today's money.

This is why long-term money is often invested rather than held as cash, and why an emergency fund (short-term money) is kept as cash anyway: for it, being available matters more than growth.

Chapter 7 — Stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification

Terminology

  • Stock (share, equity) — a small piece of ownership in a company. Value goes up and down with the company and the market. Historically higher long-run returns, with large swings.
  • Bond — a loan to a government or company, which pays interest and returns the loan amount at the end. Usually smaller swings than stocks; can still lose value, for example when interest rates rise or the borrower fails.
  • Fund — a pool of many investors' money that buys many stocks and/or bonds.
  • Mutual fund / ETF — two common fund structures. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) trades on an exchange during the day like a stock; a traditional mutual fund is bought and sold at one price per day.
  • Index fund — the US SEC's investor site defines it as a mutual fund, ETF or unit investment trust that follows a passive strategy, aiming to match the return of a market index (such as the S&P 500) before fees.
  • Expense ratio — a fund's yearly running cost as a percentage of your money in it.
  • Asset allocation — how you split money between asset types (stocks, bonds, cash).
  • Diversification — spreading money across many investments so that one failure does not sink you.

Why diversification helps

A single company can fail. A broad index holds hundreds or thousands of companies, so no single one decides your result. The SEC's beginners' guide explains that the right mix of investments may reduce losses and smooth returns without giving up too much potential gain. Diversification does not remove market risk: when the whole market falls, a diversified portfolio falls too, just less wildly than one bad stock.

Common pitfall: "diversifying" into many funds that hold the same companies. Five large-company funds from the same market may be one bet with five fees.

Chapter 8 — Risk versus time horizon

Time horizon is how long until you need the money. The SEC's guide says the right asset allocation depends largely on your time horizon and your ability to take risk.

A toy model shows why. It assumes each year's return is random with a mean of 6% and a standard deviation of 15%, years independent. These are made-up numbers, not a forecast. Real markets have crashes bigger than this model allows, and bad years can cluster.

In this model, the yearly average over a long period swings much less than a single year does, and the chance of ending below where you started falls. That is the logic behind a common guideline: money you need within a few years is kept out of volatile assets, while money for decades can ride out bad years. (Note also that the median is below 6%: big swings lower the typical compound result. This is often called volatility drag.)

Chapter 9 — Why fees matter

A fee is taken every year, from a balance that keeps growing. The loss compounds, just like returns do. The US SEC's investor bulletin on fees shows 100,000 invested for 20 years at 4% a year: with a 1% yearly fee, the result is nearly 30,000 lower than with a 0.25% fee.

Now a longer case. Assumptions (made up for illustration): start with 10,000, add 500 every month for 30 years, earn 6.0% a year before fees every year, fee charged monthly as a share of the balance, no taxes or inflation.

The gap (90,800) is much bigger than the extra fees paid (about 48,000). The rest is the growth those fees would have earned. The SEC check reproduces the bulletin: 0.50% costs about 10,000 and 1.00% costs about 28,000 compared with 0.25%.

Trick: when comparing funds that track the same index, the expense ratio is one of the few things you know for certain in advance. Returns are uncertain; fees are not.

Chapter 10 — Lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging

Suppose you have a large sum to invest at once (a bonus, an inheritance). You can invest it all now (lump sum) or feed it in over months (dollar-cost averaging, DCA).

One well-known study: Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later" (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos and Wimmer, July 2012). Setup and findings:

  • Markets: United States (1926–2011), United Kingdom (1976–2011), Australia (1984–2011).
  • Portfolio: 60% stocks / 40% bonds. DCA: equal monthly amounts over 12 months, the rest held in cash. Results compared over rolling 10-year periods.
  • Lump sum ended ahead in about two-thirds of periods: 67% (US), 67% (UK), 66% (Australia).
  • On average the lump-sum portfolio ended 2.3% higher in the US (2.2% UK, 1.3% Australia).
  • Longer DCA made it worse: in the US, lump sum beat 36-month DCA in about 90% of periods.
  • DCA did better in falling markets. In US 12-month windows, lump sum lost money in 22.4% of periods and DCA in 17.6%.

The authors' reading: lump sum wins more often because stocks and bonds usually beat cash; DCA can make sense for someone mainly worried about short-term losses and regret. Note this is one study of past data; it does not predict any future period. Vanguard published a follow-up on the same question in February 2023.

Watch out: this study is about a sum you already have. Investing part of each paycheck as it arrives is also called DCA, but it is a different situation: the money did not exist earlier. The same paper calls that a prudent way to invest.

Chapter 11 — Behaviour: the costly mistakes

The biggest risk is often the investor, not the market.

  • Chasing performance — buying what went up most recently, often near its peak.
  • Panic selling — FINRA describes it as selling large amounts quickly out of fear of further falls, without careful analysis. It turns a paper loss into a real one and can miss the recovery.
  • Overconfidence and over-trading — frequent changes add costs and timing mistakes.
  • Checking too often — daily prices are mostly noise, and noise makes people act.

The damage is measurable. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" 2025 study (one annual industry study) estimated that over the 10 years to December 2024, the average dollar in US mutual funds and ETFs earned about 7.0% a year, while the funds themselves returned about 8.2%. The 1.2-point gap, about 15% of the total return, came mostly from the timing of investors' buys and sells.

Defences that work because they remove in-the-moment decisions:

  1. Write a one-page plan: goal, time horizon, allocation, what you will do when markets fall 20%.
  2. Automate contributions (Chapter 2).
  3. Rebalance on a schedule (for example once a year), not on feelings.
  4. Rule of thumb: wait 24 hours before any unplanned change.

Exercise 5 — Real return

A savings account pays 4% while inflation is 4.5%. What is the real return? Is money in that account growing?

Solution

(1.04/1.045)−1≈−0.48%(1.04 / 1.045) - 1 \approx -0.48\%(1.04/1.045)−1≈−0.48%. The balance grows in numbers but loses buying power. For an emergency fund that is an acceptable cost of safety; for 30-year money it is a slow leak.

Exercise 6 — Fees on your numbers

Change fees.py to your own start amount, monthly amount and years. Try fees of 0.05%, 0.2%, 0.5% and 1.5%.

Solution

Loop over (0.0005, 0.002, 0.005, 0.015). The pattern holds for any positive return: the gap grows faster than linearly with time, because each year's fee also removes that money's future growth. Keep the gross return the same for all fees so the comparison stays fair.
