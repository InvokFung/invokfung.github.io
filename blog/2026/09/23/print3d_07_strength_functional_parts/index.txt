<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-The-mechanics-you-actually-need"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-The-mechanics-you-actually-need" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — The mechanics you actually need"></a>Chapter 1 — The mechanics you actually need</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stress (σ)</strong> — force per unit area, in pascals (N/m²) or MPa. What the material experiences, independent of the part's size.</li>
<li><strong>Strain (ε)</strong> — deformation per unit length, dimensionless. How much the material stretched relative to its original length.</li>
<li><strong>Stiffness (elastic modulus, E)</strong> — the slope of stress against strain in the elastic region. <em>How much it deflects under load.</em> A stiff material barely moves.</li>
<li><strong>Strength</strong> — the stress at which the material fails, whether by yielding or by fracturing. <em>How much load it takes before something goes wrong.</em> Stiffness and strength are independent properties: a material can be stiff and weak (glass), or compliant and strong (many elastomers).</li>
<li><strong>Yield strength</strong> — the stress at which deformation stops being elastic and becomes permanent. Past yield the part is bent, even after the load comes off.</li>
<li><strong>Ultimate tensile strength (UTS)</strong> — the maximum stress the material carries before fracture.</li>
<li><strong>Toughness</strong> — the energy absorbed before fracture; the area under the stress-strain curve. <em>How much abuse it takes before breaking.</em> A tough material bends, absorbs and survives.</li>
<li><strong>Ductile failure</strong> — the material yields, stretches, necks and then tears. It gives warning. PETG, ABS, nylon, TPU.</li>
<li><strong>Brittle failure</strong> — the material fractures suddenly at or near its elastic limit, with little permanent deformation and no warning. PLA, standard photopolymer resin, and most fibre-filled composites.</li>
<li><strong>Fatigue</strong> — failure from repeated cycles of load well below the static strength. A part that survives a 100 N pull once may fail after 10,000 cycles at 30 N.</li>
<li><strong>Creep</strong> — slow, continuous deformation under a <em>constant</em> load over time, without any increase in load. It accelerates dramatically as temperature approaches Tg.</li>
<li><strong>Stress concentration</strong> — a local multiplication of stress at a geometric discontinuity (a sharp internal corner, a notch, a hole edge, a sudden change of section).</li>
<li><strong>Neutral axis</strong> — in a beam under bending, the surface where the stress is zero; material on one side is in tension, on the other in compression.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-distinction-beginners-get-wrong-most-often"><a href="#The-distinction-beginners-get-wrong-most-often" class="headerlink" title="The distinction beginners get wrong most often"></a>The distinction beginners get wrong most often</h2>
<p><strong>Stiff ≠ strong ≠ tough.</strong> These are three different questions and different materials win each:</p>

<p>This is why PLA feels great on the bench (stiff, holds shape, precise) and disappoints in service (brittle, notch-sensitive, creeps). It is not a bad material. It is a material with a specific and well-known profile.</p>
<h2 id="Creep-—-the-quiet-killer"><a href="#Creep-—-the-quiet-killer" class="headerlink" title="Creep — the quiet killer"></a>Creep — the quiet killer</h2>
<p>Creep deserves its own treatment because it is the failure mode most often misdiagnosed as &quot;the print was weak&quot;.</p>
<p>Polymers are <strong>viscoelastic</strong>: under sustained load they behave partly like solids and partly like very stiff liquids. Hold a constant load on a printed bracket and it will slowly, continuously deform. Not fail — deform. Over hours, days, months, depending on the material, the stress level and the temperature.</p>
<p>The temperature dependence is the sting. Creep rate rises sharply as the material approaches Tg (Post 04). A PLA part at 20 °C creeps slowly enough to be irrelevant for many uses. The <em>same</em> part at 50 °C — a closed car in summer, a sunlit window sill, a shelf above a radiator, next to a stepper motor — is within striking distance of PLA's Tg of roughly 55–65 °C, and creeps fast enough to be visible in a day.</p>
<p>The canonical failure: a PLA phone mount, wall hook, or shelf bracket that was perfectly fine for weeks and one day is simply drooping. Nothing broke. It flowed.</p>
<p>Design responses, in order of effectiveness:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Don't use PLA for a sustained load in a warm place.</strong> PETG is a large improvement, ABS/ASA more, PC more again, and a fibre-filled grade of any of them better still.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce the stress.</strong> Creep rate is strongly stress-dependent; halving the stress helps disproportionately. Thicker sections, more perimeters, a shorter moment arm.</li>
<li><strong>Put the load into compression rather than bending.</strong> Compressive creep in a well-supported column is far less troublesome than bending creep in a cantilever.</li>
<li><strong>Carry the sustained load in metal.</strong> A printed body with a steel screw, rod or bracket taking the actual load is a robust pattern.</li>
<li><strong>Anneal</strong> (Post 04) if the part is PLA and must be warm — increased crystallinity meaningfully raises creep resistance. Then re-check the fit, because the dimensions changed.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> static strength tests tell you nothing about creep. Hanging a weight on a bracket for thirty seconds and declaring it strong is not a test of a load it will carry for a year. If the part carries a sustained load, the only meaningful test is a sustained one.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Anisotropy-honestly"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Anisotropy-honestly" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Anisotropy, honestly"></a>Chapter 2 — Anisotropy, honestly</h1>
<h2 id="What-is-actually-going-on"><a href="#What-is-actually-going-on" class="headerlink" title="What is actually going on"></a>What is actually going on</h2>
<p>A moulded part is essentially <strong>isotropic</strong>: the polymer is a continuous mass and its properties are the same in every direction. A printed part is not. It is a stack of extruded beads bonded to their neighbours, and the bond between beads is not the same as the bulk material.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>In-plane (X/Y)</strong>, along and across the extrusion direction, load is carried mostly by continuous polymer within beads and by side-to-side welds between adjacent beads laid down while both were hot.</li>
<li><strong>Through-thickness (Z)</strong>, across layers, load is carried entirely by the <strong>interlayer weld</strong>: the region where a new bead was deposited onto a layer that had already partly cooled, and the polymer chains had a limited time and a limited temperature at which to diffuse across the interface and entangle.</li>
</ul>
<p>That diffusion process is the entire mechanism. The strength of the Z direction is the strength of that weld, and the weld is imperfect: it forms in a fraction of a second, at a temperature falling rapidly toward Tg, between two surfaces that also have geometric voids at the bead junctions.</p>
<p><strong>The honest statement of the magnitude:</strong> interlayer (Z) strength is <strong>substantially lower</strong> than in-plane strength for essentially every FDM material and profile. The size of the gap varies enormously — it depends on the polymer, the nozzle temperature, the layer height, the part cooling, the chamber temperature, the layer time, the bead geometry, and whether the part was annealed. Published studies find a wide range of ratios, and any single percentage quoted as a general fact about &quot;3D printing&quot; is not one.</p>
<p>You will see figures like &quot;Z is 50 % of XY&quot; repeated widely. Treat them the way Post 05 taught you to treat &quot;0.05 mm accuracy&quot;: as a number for someone's specific machine, material and profile, not as a property of the technology. <strong>Measure it on yours.</strong> Exercise 2 gives the procedure.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-What-actually-controls-strength-in-order"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-What-actually-controls-strength-in-order" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — What actually controls strength, in order"></a>Chapter 3 — What actually controls strength, in order</h1>
<p>Beginners tune this list in roughly reverse order. Here it is correctly ordered by leverage.</p>
<h2 id="1-Orientation-—-by-a-wide-margin-the-most-important"><a href="#1-Orientation-—-by-a-wide-margin-the-most-important" class="headerlink" title="1. Orientation — by a wide margin the most important"></a>1. Orientation — by a wide margin the most important</h2>
<p>Rotating the part on the plate can change its breaking load by a factor. Nothing else in the profile comes close.</p>
<p>The rule: <strong>never let the primary tensile load path cross a layer boundary.</strong> Identify how the part is loaded, identify where the tension is highest, and orient so the layers run <em>along</em> the load, not across it.</p>
<p>Concrete cases:</p>

<h2 id="2-Perimeter-wall-count"><a href="#2-Perimeter-wall-count" class="headerlink" title="2. Perimeter (wall) count"></a>2. Perimeter (wall) count</h2>
<p>Second-largest lever, and much larger than infill. Going from 2 to 4 perimeters typically makes a far bigger difference than going from 20 % to 50 % infill, at a fraction of the material and time cost.</p>
<h3 id="Why-where-bending-stress-actually-acts"><a href="#Why-where-bending-stress-actually-acts" class="headerlink" title="Why: where bending stress actually acts"></a>Why: where bending stress actually acts</h3>
<p>Most printed parts fail in <strong>bending</strong>, not pure tension. In a beam under bending:</p>
<ul>
<li>The material on the outside of the bend is in <strong>tension</strong>; on the inside, in <strong>compression</strong>; and somewhere between them, at the <strong>neutral axis</strong>, the stress is zero.</li>
<li>Stress varies <strong>linearly with distance from the neutral axis</strong>. The extreme fibres carry the maximum; material at the neutral axis carries nothing.</li>
<li>Bending stiffness is governed by the <strong>second moment of area</strong>, which weights material by the <em>square</em> of its distance from the neutral axis. Bending <em>strength</em> is governed by the section modulus, which likewise rewards distance from the axis.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Cross-section of a printed beam under bending:</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">   perimeters (outer shell) ----&gt; ####################   &lt;- TENSION, max stress</span><br><span class="line">                                  #··················#</span><br><span class="line">   infill (interior)      ------&gt; #··· · · · · · ·  ·#   &lt;- near neutral axis,</span><br><span class="line">                                  #··················#      near-zero stress</span><br><span class="line">                                  ####################   &lt;- COMPRESSION, max stress</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">   Perimeters sit exactly where the stress is highest.</span><br><span class="line">   Infill sits exactly where the stress is lowest.</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>That is the entire argument. Perimeters are the material at the extreme fibres — the material that is doing the work. Infill is the material near the neutral axis, where the stress is small. Adding infill adds mass and print time in the place where it contributes least.</p>
<p>Infill is not useless; it does three real jobs:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Supports the top surfaces</strong> so they can be printed at all (Post 02).</li>
<li><strong>Stabilises the perimeters against buckling</strong> — a thin shell under compression fails by buckling long before it fails by crushing, and infill braces it.</li>
<li><strong>Carries compression and shear</strong> in a part loaded through its bulk rather than in bending.</li>
</ol>
<p>But for the common case — a bracket, a hook, a mount, a lever, anything cantilevered — perimeters win decisively.</p>
<h2 id="3-Material"><a href="#3-Material" class="headerlink" title="3. Material"></a>3. Material</h2>
<p>Third. Choosing PETG over PLA for a part that gets knocked, or nylon over PETG for a part that wears, changes the outcome more than any slicer setting except the two above. Post 04's decision tree is the tool. Note that the material choice interacts with orientation: fibre-filled composites are dramatically stiffer in-plane and are usually <em>more</em> brittle across layers, so the anisotropy gets worse, not better.</p>
<h2 id="4-Temperature-and-cooling"><a href="#4-Temperature-and-cooling" class="headerlink" title="4. Temperature and cooling"></a>4. Temperature and cooling</h2>
<p>Fourth. Nozzle temperature is the strongest single lever on interlayer strength within a given material (Chapter 2). Reduced part cooling helps interlayer bonding and hurts overhangs. An enclosure helps materials that want one. These are real effects with real trade-offs — but they are refinements on top of the first three decisions, not substitutes for them.</p>
<h2 id="5-Infill-density-and-pattern"><a href="#5-Infill-density-and-pattern" class="headerlink" title="5. Infill density and pattern"></a>5. Infill density and pattern</h2>
<p>Fifth, and well below where beginners put it.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Density.</strong> Beyond roughly 40–50 %, additional infill buys little strength for a lot of time and material; the curve flattens. Below about 15 % the part becomes noticeably soft and top surfaces suffer. For most functional parts, 20–30 % infill with 4–6 perimeters outperforms 50 % infill with 2 perimeters, using less material.</li>
<li><strong>Pattern.</strong> Matters more than density in some load cases. <strong>Gyroid</strong> is isotropic, self-supporting, prints fast and is a strong general default. <strong>Cubic</strong> and <strong>triangular</strong> are stiff and good under multi-axis load. <strong>Grid</strong> creates crossing points where the nozzle collides with previously-laid material. <strong>Lines/rectilinear</strong> is weak in one direction. <strong>Concentric</strong> is right under flexible parts.</li>
<li><strong>Special case:</strong> a <em>fully solid</em> part (100 % infill) is not automatically the strongest thing you can print. Over-extrusion in a solid part introduces internal stress and can leave voids; and the part still fails at layer interfaces. Solid is heavy, slow and often not better than thick perimeters plus moderate infill.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="6-Layer-height"><a href="#6-Layer-height" class="headerlink" title="6. Layer height"></a>6. Layer height</h2>
<p>Sixth. Thicker layers bond somewhat better and print faster; thinner layers give finer detail and better surface. Its effect on strength is real but small next to the levers above.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> the reflex &quot;print it at 100 % infill so it's strong&quot;. It costs hours and grams, and it usually loses to reorienting the part and adding two perimeters — which costs nothing.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Stress-concentration-and-design-patterns"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Stress-concentration-and-design-patterns" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Stress concentration and design patterns"></a>Chapter 4 — Stress concentration and design patterns</h1>
<h2 id="The-notch-effect"><a href="#The-notch-effect" class="headerlink" title="The notch effect"></a>The notch effect</h2>
<p>A sharp internal corner is a stress concentrator. The local stress at the corner can be several times the nominal stress in the surrounding material, and it rises as the corner radius falls — approaching, in the idealised limit of a perfectly sharp corner, an unbounded value. Real materials do not see infinite stress, but the multiplication is large and real, and it is where cracks start.</p>
<p>Printed parts are unusually vulnerable to this for two reasons:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>PLA and filled composites are notch-sensitive</strong> — brittle materials do not locally yield to blunt the peak, so the concentration translates directly into a crack.</li>
<li><strong>A layer interface at a sharp internal corner combines a stress raiser with the weakest plane in the part.</strong> That combination is the single most common origin of a printed-part fracture.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="Fillets-the-cheapest-strength-upgrade-available"><a href="#Fillets-the-cheapest-strength-upgrade-available" class="headerlink" title="Fillets: the cheapest strength upgrade available"></a>Fillets: the cheapest strength upgrade available</h2>
<p>Adding a fillet to a loaded internal corner costs one CAD feature, a negligible amount of material, and essentially no print time. It reduces the peak stress substantially. There is no other change with that ratio of benefit to cost.</p>
<p>Practical guidance:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fillet every loaded internal corner.</strong> A radius comparable to the local wall thickness is a reasonable default; larger is better, up to what the geometry allows.</li>
<li><strong>A larger radius is better than a smaller one.</strong> The benefit is strongly nonlinear at small radii — going from sharp to a small fillet buys most of the improvement.</li>
<li><strong>Fillet transitions between sections too.</strong> A sudden change from thick to thin is a stress raiser even without a corner. Taper it.</li>
<li><strong>Watch the printing rules</strong> (Post 06): fillet vertical and top edges freely; <strong>chamfer</strong> bottom edges.</li>
<li><strong>Do not fillet a corner that needs to mate flush with something.</strong> Chamfer that one, or add a relief groove on the mating part.</li>
</ul>

<td>thicker boss wall; correct pocket dimensions per datasheet; proper installation temperature and slow, straight pressure</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Warped over time on the shelf</strong></td>
<td>no fracture; the part simply is not the shape it was</td>
<td>residual stress relaxing, or moisture absorption (nylon), or heat exposure</td>
<td>print with an appropriate thermal environment (Post 04); anneal to relieve stress deliberately; store away from heat</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Sagged under a sustained load</strong></td>
<td>no fracture; permanent bend, often at the highest-moment section</td>
<td><strong>creep</strong></td>
<td>Chapter 1. Change material, reduce stress, put the load in compression, or carry it in metal</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Failed after long use at a load it once held easily</strong></td>
<td>fracture surface with a smooth region (crack growth) and a rough final region</td>
<td><strong>fatigue</strong></td>
<td>reduce stress amplitude; eliminate stress raisers; a tougher material; more section</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Became brittle and chalky outdoors</strong></td>
<td>surface crazing, colour fade, snaps with no warning</td>
<td><strong>UV degradation</strong>, plus thermal cycling</td>
<td>ASA. Paint with a UV-stable coating. Never PLA outdoors</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Delaminated mid-print or shortly after</strong></td>
<td>horizontal split, often in a tall thin part</td>
<td>thermal gradient plus residual stress; poor interlayer bonding</td>
<td>enclosure; hotter nozzle; less cooling; shorter/wider geometry (Post 04)</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Layers separated at an overhang or bridge</strong></td>
<td>localised, at the geometry transition</td>
<td>poor support of the region; excessive cooling variation</td>
<td>supports or reorientation (Post 03); bridging settings</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Snapped where a section changed abruptly</strong></td>
<td>fracture right at the step</td>
<td>stress concentration at the section change</td>
<td>taper or fillet the transition</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Part dissolved / softened in service</strong></td>
<td>swelling, tackiness, dimensional growth</td>
<td>chemical incompatibility with a solvent, oil or cleaner</td>
<td>check chemical resistance (Post 04); change material or coat it</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Loosened at a press fit over time</strong></td>
<td>joint has play that was not there when assembled</td>
<td>polymer <strong>stress relaxation</strong> — the interference decayed</td>
<td>do not rely on a plastic press fit for a permanent joint; use a fastener, a snap with a positive lock, or adhesive</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Testing-and-when-not-to-print"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Testing-and-when-not-to-print" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Testing, and when not to print"></a>Chapter 7 — Testing, and when not to print</h1>
<h2 id="Test-the-part-not-a-coupon"><a href="#Test-the-part-not-a-coupon" class="headerlink" title="Test the part, not a coupon"></a>Test the part, not a coupon</h2>
<p>Standardised tensile coupons (ISO 527, ASTM D638) exist for a reason: they let laboratories compare materials on equal terms. They are the right tool for <em>material</em> comparison and the wrong tool for answering &quot;will my bracket hold&quot;.</p>
<p>A coupon tells you about a dogbone specimen printed in one orientation. Your part has a specific geometry, specific stress concentrations, a specific orientation, a specific load path, specific infill, and a specific set of fillets and section changes. Those interactions dominate. <strong>Test the actual part, loaded the way it will actually be loaded.</strong></p>
<h2 id="A-repeatable-comparative-test-rig"><a href="#A-repeatable-comparative-test-rig" class="headerlink" title="A repeatable comparative test rig"></a>A repeatable comparative test rig</h2>
<p>You are not trying to produce absolute engineering data. You are trying to answer &quot;is A better than B on my machine&quot;. That requires only <strong>repeatability and a controlled comparison</strong> — both achievable with cheap equipment.</p>
<p>The minimum viable rig:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>A fixture that holds the part exactly the same way every time.</strong> This is the most important part. Print a jig that clamps the part in one repeatable position. Variation in how the part is held will swamp everything else.</li>
<li><strong>A way to apply load progressively and measure it.</strong> Options in ascending order of cost: a bucket you add water or sand to and then weigh; a luggage scale or fish scale pulled by hand; a hanging digital scale with a peak-hold function; a lever arm with hanging weights; an inexpensive load cell with an amplifier logging to a microcontroller.</li>
<li><strong>A record.</strong> Load at first audible crack, load at failure, where it failed, a photograph of the fracture.</li>
</ol>
<p>The disciplines that make the results mean something:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Change exactly one variable at a time.</strong> Same spool, same day, same profile except the one thing being tested.</li>
<li><strong>Print all specimens in the same batch</strong>, on the same plate where possible, so filament and machine state are shared.</li>
<li><strong>n ≥ 3 per condition.</strong> Five is better. Printed parts scatter; a single specimen tells you nothing, exactly as in Post 05.</li>
<li><strong>Report the spread, not just the mean.</strong> If A's mean beats B's by less than the within-group spread, you have not shown anything.</li>
<li><strong>Note the temperature.</strong> Polymer strength is temperature-dependent, so a test in a cold garage is not comparable to one in a warm room.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="Anisotropy-test-procedure"><a href="#Anisotropy-test-procedure" class="headerlink" title="Anisotropy test procedure"></a>Anisotropy test procedure</h2>
<p>Print the <em>same</em> simple beam three times in each of three orientations — flat, on-edge, and upright — with everything else identical. Break each in three-point bending in your fixture. The upright specimens fail across layers; the others do not. The ratio of mean failure loads is <strong>your</strong> anisotropy figure, for that material and profile.</p>

<h2 id="When-not-to-3D-print"><a href="#When-not-to-3D-print" class="headerlink" title="When not to 3D print"></a>When not to 3D print</h2>
<p>This section is the honest one, and it is missing from most 3D printing material because it is not what the audience wants to hear. FDM is a superb process for a specific envelope: <strong>one-off or low-volume parts with complex geometry, needed quickly, at low cost, where moderate strength and moderate accuracy suffice.</strong> Outside that envelope, something else wins.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Instead consider</th>
<th>When it wins</th>
<th>Why</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Buying the part</strong></td>
<td>it exists as a standard component — brackets, hinges, enclosures, knobs, clamps, standoffs, gears, bearings, fasteners</td>
<td>a mass-produced part is stronger, cheaper, and available now. Printing a worse copy of a €3 bracket over six hours is a hobby, not engineering. <strong>Search first.</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>CNC machining</strong></td>
<td>precision, isotropic strength, metal, surface finish</td>
<td>milled aluminium is strong in every direction, dimensionally accurate to a far tighter tolerance, and holds up under heat and sustained load</td>
</tr>

<td>quantity — hundreds up, and certainly thousands</td>
<td>per-part cost collapses once tooling is amortised; parts are isotropic and dimensionally consistent. Print the <em>prototype</em>, mould the <em>product</em></td>

<p>The productive way to hold this: <strong>3D printing's genuine superpower is geometry, not material.</strong> It makes shapes that no other process can make at any price, in a single operation, in an afternoon, with no tooling. Use it for that. When a part's requirement is strength, precision, temperature resistance, or quantity — the things other processes are actually good at — the printer is not the answer, and knowing that is a mark of competence rather than defeat.</p>

<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span><br><span class="line">13</span><br><span class="line">14</span><br><span class="line">15</span><br><span class="line">16</span><br><span class="line">17</span><br><span class="line">18</span><br><span class="line">19</span><br><span class="line">20</span><br><span class="line">21</span><br><span class="line">22</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Expected outcome:</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">  6 perimeters / 15 % infill  -&gt;  higher failure load</span><br><span class="line">                              -&gt;  LESS material</span><br><span class="line">                              -&gt;  LESS print time</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">  2 perimeters / 50 % infill  -&gt;  lower failure load, more material, more time</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Why, in one sentence: in bending, stress varies linearly with distance from</span><br><span class="line">the neutral axis and bending stiffness scales with the SECOND MOMENT of that</span><br><span class="line">distance — so material in the perimeters (at the extreme fibres) is doing the</span><br><span class="line">work, and material in the infill (near the neutral axis) is mostly ballast.</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Caveats that keep the conclusion honest:</span><br><span class="line">- It is a BENDING result. <h2 id="Exercise-4-—-What-a-fillet-is-worth"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-What-a-fillet-is-worth" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — What a fillet is worth"></a>Exercise 4 — What a fillet is worth</h2>
<p>Print three L-brackets with a sharp internal corner and three identical ones with a fillet of roughly one wall thickness at that corner. Load them until failure in the same fixture. Note where each cracked. Then print three more with a gusset added and repeat.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span><br><span class="line">13</span><br><span class="line">14</span><br><span class="line">15</span><br><span class="line">16</span><br><span class="line">17</span><br><span class="line">18</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Expected pattern:</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">  sharp corner  -&gt; cracks initiate exactly AT the corner, at the lowest load</span><br><span class="line">  filleted      -&gt; higher failure load; the crack, when it comes, often starts</span><br><span class="line">                   somewhere other than the corner</span><br><span class="line">  gusseted      -&gt; highest failure load; failure mode frequently MOVES</span><br><span class="line">                   elsewhere entirely (the gusset, the fastener holes, or</span><br><span class="line">                   the arm itself)</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">The engineering reading: when a design change moves the failure to a</span><br><span class="line">different location, that change has done its job — the original weak point</span><br><span class="line">is no longer the weakest link. Iterate by chasing the failure around the</span><br><span class="line">part until it fails somewhere you are content with.</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Cost accounting: the fillet added a negligible amount of material and</span><br><span class="line">essentially no print time. Nothing else in this course has that ratio of</span><br><span class="line">benefit to cost.</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>

<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Creep-observed"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Creep-observed" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Creep, observed"></a>Exercise 5 — Creep, observed</h2>
<p>Print two identical PLA hooks. Hang the same modest weight on both — well below the load that breaks them. Leave one at room temperature and one somewhere warm (a sunny window, a warm cupboard, near a radiator). Photograph both against a printed reference scale at day 0, day 1, day 7 and day 30. Then repeat the whole experiment in PETG.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Build-the-fixture"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Build-the-fixture" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Build the fixture"></a>Exercise 6 — Build the fixture</h2>
<p>Design and print a test fixture that holds your chosen test coupon in exactly the same position every time, and that interfaces with whatever load-measuring device you have. Test its repeatability by breaking five identical specimens and reporting the standard deviation. If that spread is large, improve the fixture before trusting any comparison you make with it.</p>
<h2 id="Exercise-7-—-The-should-I-print-this-audit"><a href="#Exercise-7-—-The-should-I-print-this-audit" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 7 — The &quot;should I print this?&quot; audit"></a>Exercise 7 — The &quot;should I print this?&quot; audit</h2>
<p>List the last ten things you printed. For each, answer: could it have been bought, and for how much? Would another process have been better? Be honest. Then, for the next functional part you design, run the full Chapter 7 checklist before starting the print and note which items caught something.</p>

<h1 id="Course-base-complete-—-whats-next"><a href="#Course-base-complete-—-whats-next" class="headerlink" title="Course base complete — what's next"></a>Course base complete — what's next</h1>
<p>You now have the full base: how the process works, how to calibrate a machine, how slicing decisions map to physical outcomes, how orientation and supports shape a design, what materials do and why, how to reason honestly about dimensions and tolerances, how to model for the process, and how to make parts that survive being used.</p>
<p>The through-line of the whole course, and the thing worth carrying forward: <strong>every number in this series is either a physical constant, a published material property with a range, or a value you must measure on your own machine — and the third category is much larger than beginners expect.</strong> A course that hands you settings makes you dependent on someone else's setup. A course that hands you procedures makes you able to characterise any machine you sit down at. That was the goal.</p>
<p><strong>Planned future posts (extensions):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>08 — Resin printing deep dive.</strong> MSLA mechanics, exposure calibration and test artefacts, supports and orientation for resin, wash and post-cure workflows, resin chemistry families, and the safety and waste handling this post only touched on.</li>
<li><strong>09 — Multi-material and multi-colour printing.</strong> AMS/MMU/tool-changer architectures, purge towers and the real waste economics, interface layers and soluble supports in practice, and designing parts that exploit material combinations rather than just colour.</li>
<li><strong>10 — Klipper tuning, input shaping and speed.</strong> Moving from Marlin, resonance measurement with an accelerometer, input shaping and pressure advance, and where speed genuinely costs quality versus where it is free.</li>
<li><strong>11 — Printer modification, maintenance and repair.</strong> Preventive maintenance schedules, hotend and extruder rebuilds, bed levelling and probe systems, frame squaring, belts and linear motion, and diagnosing a machine that has drifted.</li>
<li><strong>12 — Engineering materials and high-temperature printing.</strong> PA-CF, PPS, PPA, PEI/ULTEM and PEEK; hot chambers, all-metal high-temperature hotends, hardened everything, and whether any of it is justified for your work.</li>
<li><strong>13 — Large-format printing and print farms.</strong> Scaling geometry and warp, segmenting and joining large parts, queue management and remote monitoring, and the economics of running several machines.</li>
<li><strong>14 — From print to product.</strong> Cost modelling per part, batching and plate optimisation, finishing at scale, packaging, liability and standards, and what changes when you sell what you print.</li>
<li><strong>15 — 3D printing meets furniture and CAD.</strong> Jigs, drilling templates, hardware fitting templates, and the millimetre-precision question — the direct bridge to the planned CAD/furniture series, which makes this course's Post 05 argument again in wood: <strong>CAD precision does not survive contact with the physical world.</strong></li>
</ul>
