3D Printing Course — 07 · Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts

Created2026-09-23|Updated2026-09-23|Engineering > 3D Printing
|Post View:

The base course closes where it should: with the question of whether the part actually works. Posts 00–03 covered the process, Post 04 the materials, Post 05 whether the part is the right size, and Post 06 how to model it. This post covers whether it survives being used. That requires a working vocabulary of mechanics, an honest treatment of the anisotropy that makes a printed part fundamentally different from a moulded one, a correctly-ordered list of which print settings actually matter for strength, a catalogue mapping observed failures to their causes, and — the section most 3D printing material omits — a clear-eyed account of when the right answer is not to print the part at all.

Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts (this post).

Learning Outcome

  • Use the mechanics vocabulary correctly: stress, strain, stiffness, strength, toughness, yield, ultimate, ductile, brittle, fatigue, creep.
  • Explain why creep — not strength — is what quietly destroys loaded PLA parts, and design around it.
  • Describe printed-part anisotropy correctly, and run a test that measures your Z-strength ratio rather than quoting someone else's number.
  • Rank the print-profile levers on strength in the correct order, and explain why infill density sits far lower than beginners assume.
  • Argue the perimeters-versus-infill case from where bending stress actually acts.
  • Design out stress concentrations, and use fillets as the cheapest strength upgrade available.
  • Diagnose a broken part from the fracture surface and map symptom to root cause to fix.
  • Build a repeatable comparative test rig and test the actual part rather than a coupon.
  • Decide when machining, moulding, sheet metal, or simply buying the part is the better answer.

Chapter 1 — The mechanics you actually need

Terminology

  • Stress (σ) — force per unit area, in pascals (N/m²) or MPa. What the material experiences, independent of the part's size.
  • Strain (ε) — deformation per unit length, dimensionless. How much the material stretched relative to its original length.
  • Stiffness (elastic modulus, E) — the slope of stress against strain in the elastic region. How much it deflects under load. A stiff material barely moves.
  • Strength — the stress at which the material fails, whether by yielding or by fracturing. How much load it takes before something goes wrong. Stiffness and strength are independent properties: a material can be stiff and weak (glass), or compliant and strong (many elastomers).
  • Yield strength — the stress at which deformation stops being elastic and becomes permanent. Past yield the part is bent, even after the load comes off.
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) — the maximum stress the material carries before fracture.
  • Toughness — the energy absorbed before fracture; the area under the stress-strain curve. How much abuse it takes before breaking. A tough material bends, absorbs and survives.
  • Ductile failure — the material yields, stretches, necks and then tears. It gives warning. PETG, ABS, nylon, TPU.
  • Brittle failure — the material fractures suddenly at or near its elastic limit, with little permanent deformation and no warning. PLA, standard photopolymer resin, and most fibre-filled composites.
  • Fatigue — failure from repeated cycles of load well below the static strength. A part that survives a 100 N pull once may fail after 10,000 cycles at 30 N.
  • Creep — slow, continuous deformation under a constant load over time, without any increase in load. It accelerates dramatically as temperature approaches Tg.
  • Stress concentration — a local multiplication of stress at a geometric discontinuity (a sharp internal corner, a notch, a hole edge, a sudden change of section).
  • Neutral axis — in a beam under bending, the surface where the stress is zero; material on one side is in tension, on the other in compression.

The distinction beginners get wrong most often

Stiff ≠ strong ≠ tough. These are three different questions and different materials win each:

Question Property The common filament that wins
How much does it bend under load? stiffness PLA is the stiffest common filament — genuinely
How much load until it breaks? strength depends heavily on direction; PLA is competitive in-plane
Does it survive being dropped or shocked? toughness PLA is poor; PETG, ABS, PC and nylon are far better

This is why PLA feels great on the bench (stiff, holds shape, precise) and disappoints in service (brittle, notch-sensitive, creeps). It is not a bad material. It is a material with a specific and well-known profile.

Creep — the quiet killer

Creep deserves its own treatment because it is the failure mode most often misdiagnosed as "the print was weak".

Polymers are viscoelastic: under sustained load they behave partly like solids and partly like very stiff liquids. Hold a constant load on a printed bracket and it will slowly, continuously deform. Not fail — deform. Over hours, days, months, depending on the material, the stress level and the temperature.

The temperature dependence is the sting. Creep rate rises sharply as the material approaches Tg (Post 04). A PLA part at 20 °C creeps slowly enough to be irrelevant for many uses. The same part at 50 °C — a closed car in summer, a sunlit window sill, a shelf above a radiator, next to a stepper motor — is within striking distance of PLA's Tg of roughly 55–65 °C, and creeps fast enough to be visible in a day.

The canonical failure: a PLA phone mount, wall hook, or shelf bracket that was perfectly fine for weeks and one day is simply drooping. Nothing broke. It flowed.

Design responses, in order of effectiveness:

  1. Don't use PLA for a sustained load in a warm place. PETG is a large improvement, ABS/ASA more, PC more again, and a fibre-filled grade of any of them better still.
  2. Reduce the stress. Creep rate is strongly stress-dependent; halving the stress helps disproportionately. Thicker sections, more perimeters, a shorter moment arm.
  3. Put the load into compression rather than bending. Compressive creep in a well-supported column is far less troublesome than bending creep in a cantilever.
  4. Carry the sustained load in metal. A printed body with a steel screw, rod or bracket taking the actual load is a robust pattern.
  5. Anneal (Post 04) if the part is PLA and must be warm — increased crystallinity meaningfully raises creep resistance. Then re-check the fit, because the dimensions changed.

Watch out: static strength tests tell you nothing about creep. Hanging a weight on a bracket for thirty seconds and declaring it strong is not a test of a load it will carry for a year. If the part carries a sustained load, the only meaningful test is a sustained one.

Chapter 2 — Anisotropy, honestly

What is actually going on

A moulded part is essentially isotropic: the polymer is a continuous mass and its properties are the same in every direction. A printed part is not. It is a stack of extruded beads bonded to their neighbours, and the bond between beads is not the same as the bulk material.

  • In-plane (X/Y), along and across the extrusion direction, load is carried mostly by continuous polymer within beads and by side-to-side welds between adjacent beads laid down while both were hot.
  • Through-thickness (Z), across layers, load is carried entirely by the interlayer weld: the region where a new bead was deposited onto a layer that had already partly cooled, and the polymer chains had a limited time and a limited temperature at which to diffuse across the interface and entangle.

That diffusion process is the entire mechanism. The strength of the Z direction is the strength of that weld, and the weld is imperfect: it forms in a fraction of a second, at a temperature falling rapidly toward Tg, between two surfaces that also have geometric voids at the bead junctions.

The honest statement of the magnitude: interlayer (Z) strength is substantially lower than in-plane strength for essentially every FDM material and profile. The size of the gap varies enormously — it depends on the polymer, the nozzle temperature, the layer height, the part cooling, the chamber temperature, the layer time, the bead geometry, and whether the part was annealed. Published studies find a wide range of ratios, and any single percentage quoted as a general fact about "3D printing" is not one.

You will see figures like "Z is 50 % of XY" repeated widely. Treat them the way Post 05 taught you to treat "0.05 mm accuracy": as a number for someone's specific machine, material and profile, not as a property of the technology. Measure it on yours. Exercise 2 gives the procedure.

Reducing the gap

Lever Effect on interlayer strength Cost
Higher nozzle temperature strong positive — more time above the diffusion temperature surface quality, stringing, oozing; bounded by material degradation
Enclosure / warm chamber strong positive — the previous layer is hotter when the new one lands equipment; not appropriate for PLA (Post 04)
Less part cooling positive overhangs and bridges degrade; a real trade-off
Thicker layers positive — more bonded area per interface and more retained heat resolution, surface finish
Wider extrusion / bigger nozzle positive — larger weld area, fewer voids resolution, detail
Slower printing mixed — slower gives more heat per unit length, but a longer layer time means the previous layer is cooler when you return to it time
Annealing positive — chains interdiffuse across interfaces given time above Tg dimensional change (Post 04)
Ironing / higher flow small positive — reduces interlayer voids
Orientation eliminates the problem — put the load in-plane usually free; occasionally costs support material

Watch out: every mitigation in that table trades something away — surface quality, overhang capability, resolution, or time — and none of them removes the weakness. Only orientation does.

The last row is the point of the whole chapter. Every other lever is a partial mitigation with a cost. Orientation makes the weakness irrelevant, and it usually costs nothing but a decision. That is why it heads the next chapter's list.

Chapter 3 — What actually controls strength, in order

Beginners tune this list in roughly reverse order. Here it is correctly ordered by leverage.

1. Orientation — by a wide margin the most important

Rotating the part on the plate can change its breaking load by a factor. Nothing else in the profile comes close.

The rule: never let the primary tensile load path cross a layer boundary. Identify how the part is loaded, identify where the tension is highest, and orient so the layers run along the load, not across it.

Concrete cases:

Part Wrong orientation Right orientation
Hook carrying a downward load printed upright — the bending tension at the root is across layers printed flat on its side (or standing in the load plane) so layers run along the hook
Bracket with an L-shape either leg vertical — the corner is a layer interface in tension flat, with both legs in the layer plane
Threaded boss taking a pull-out load axis vertical — the thread strips layer by layer axis horizontal, or use a heat-set insert (Post 06)
Long thin rod under tension vertical — every layer is a potential fracture plane horizontal
A gear either works for tooth bending; hub axis vertical is standard axis vertical, so tooth loads are in-plane

Post 03 covers orientation for surface quality and supports. This is the same decision viewed through strength, and when the two conflict, strength usually wins on a functional part.

2. Perimeter (wall) count

Second-largest lever, and much larger than infill. Going from 2 to 4 perimeters typically makes a far bigger difference than going from 20 % to 50 % infill, at a fraction of the material and time cost.

Why: where bending stress actually acts

Most printed parts fail in bending, not pure tension. In a beam under bending:

  • The material on the outside of the bend is in tension; on the inside, in compression; and somewhere between them, at the neutral axis, the stress is zero.
  • Stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis. The extreme fibres carry the maximum; material at the neutral axis carries nothing.
  • Bending stiffness is governed by the second moment of area, which weights material by the square of its distance from the neutral axis. Bending strength is governed by the section modulus, which likewise rewards distance from the axis.

That is the entire argument. Perimeters are the material at the extreme fibres — the material that is doing the work. Infill is the material near the neutral axis, where the stress is small. Adding infill adds mass and print time in the place where it contributes least.

Infill is not useless; it does three real jobs:

  1. Supports the top surfaces so they can be printed at all (Post 02).
  2. Stabilises the perimeters against buckling — a thin shell under compression fails by buckling long before it fails by crushing, and infill braces it.
  3. Carries compression and shear in a part loaded through its bulk rather than in bending.

But for the common case — a bracket, a hook, a mount, a lever, anything cantilevered — perimeters win decisively.

3. Material

Third. Choosing PETG over PLA for a part that gets knocked, or nylon over PETG for a part that wears, changes the outcome more than any slicer setting except the two above. Post 04's decision tree is the tool. Note that the material choice interacts with orientation: fibre-filled composites are dramatically stiffer in-plane and are usually more brittle across layers, so the anisotropy gets worse, not better.

4. Temperature and cooling

Fourth. Nozzle temperature is the strongest single lever on interlayer strength within a given material (Chapter 2). Reduced part cooling helps interlayer bonding and hurts overhangs. An enclosure helps materials that want one. These are real effects with real trade-offs — but they are refinements on top of the first three decisions, not substitutes for them.

5. Infill density and pattern

Fifth, and well below where beginners put it.

  • Density. Beyond roughly 40–50 %, additional infill buys little strength for a lot of time and material; the curve flattens. Below about 15 % the part becomes noticeably soft and top surfaces suffer. For most functional parts, 20–30 % infill with 4–6 perimeters outperforms 50 % infill with 2 perimeters, using less material.
  • Pattern. Matters more than density in some load cases. Gyroid is isotropic, self-supporting, prints fast and is a strong general default. Cubic and triangular are stiff and good under multi-axis load. Grid creates crossing points where the nozzle collides with previously-laid material. Lines/rectilinear is weak in one direction. Concentric is right under flexible parts.
  • Special case: a fully solid part (100 % infill) is not automatically the strongest thing you can print. Over-extrusion in a solid part introduces internal stress and can leave voids; and the part still fails at layer interfaces. Solid is heavy, slow and often not better than thick perimeters plus moderate infill.

6. Layer height

Sixth. Thicker layers bond somewhat better and print faster; thinner layers give finer detail and better surface. Its effect on strength is real but small next to the levers above.

Common pitfall: the reflex "print it at 100 % infill so it's strong". It costs hours and grams, and it usually loses to reorienting the part and adding two perimeters — which costs nothing.

Chapter 4 — Stress concentration and design patterns

The notch effect

A sharp internal corner is a stress concentrator. The local stress at the corner can be several times the nominal stress in the surrounding material, and it rises as the corner radius falls — approaching, in the idealised limit of a perfectly sharp corner, an unbounded value. Real materials do not see infinite stress, but the multiplication is large and real, and it is where cracks start.

Printed parts are unusually vulnerable to this for two reasons:

  1. PLA and filled composites are notch-sensitive — brittle materials do not locally yield to blunt the peak, so the concentration translates directly into a crack.
  2. A layer interface at a sharp internal corner combines a stress raiser with the weakest plane in the part. That combination is the single most common origin of a printed-part fracture.

Fillets: the cheapest strength upgrade available

Adding a fillet to a loaded internal corner costs one CAD feature, a negligible amount of material, and essentially no print time. It reduces the peak stress substantially. There is no other change with that ratio of benefit to cost.

Practical guidance:

  • Fillet every loaded internal corner. A radius comparable to the local wall thickness is a reasonable default; larger is better, up to what the geometry allows.
  • A larger radius is better than a smaller one. The benefit is strongly nonlinear at small radii — going from sharp to a small fillet buys most of the improvement.
  • Fillet transitions between sections too. A sudden change from thick to thin is a stress raiser even without a corner. Taper it.
  • Watch the printing rules (Post 06): fillet vertical and top edges freely; chamfer bottom edges.
  • Do not fillet a corner that needs to mate flush with something. Chamfer that one, or add a relief groove on the mating part.

Pattern Instead of Why

Trick: the single highest-value five-minute change to almost any printed bracket is: add a gusset, fillet the internal corner, and rotate it so the layers run along the load. Those three together frequently more than double the failure load, and cost no extra material worth mentioning.

Common pitfall: loading a part immediately after vapour smoothing or solvent gluing. The solvent is still in the polymer, the surface is soft, and the part is temporarily far weaker than it was before you improved it. Give it days, not hours, in a ventilated place before putting load on it.

Gluing, by material

Material Adhesive that works Notes

Universal rules for a glued joint: roughen both faces, degrease with IPA, maximise surface area (a lap joint beats a butt joint), and clamp during cure. A glued joint that relies on end-grain-equivalent contact between two flat printed faces will disappoint; design a scarf, a lap or a tongue.

Chapter 6 — Failure-mode catalogue

Read the fracture surface. It tells you what happened.

Symptom What the fracture looks like Root cause Fix
Snapped cleanly along a layer line flat, matching the layer plane exactly; you can see the bead pattern on the face tensile load crossed the layer interface reorient so the load runs in-plane. Then: hotter nozzle, less cooling, thicker layers, enclosure. Consider annealing
Cracked at a sharp internal corner crack initiates at the corner and runs outward stress concentration, often compounded by a layer interface at the corner fillet the corner. Add a gusset. Reorient so the corner is not a layer boundary
Split between perimeters and infill separation at the shell/infill boundary insufficient perimeter-to-infill overlap, under-extrusion, or too few perimeters increase perimeter count; check flow calibration (Post 01); increase infill/perimeter overlap
Stripped a printed thread thread crests sheared off, often in a layer-wise spiral printed threads are weak; the crest is layer-bonded plastic heat-set insert or captive nut (Post 06). If it must be a printed thread: coarser pitch, axis vertical, more engagement length
Screw pulled through the plastic conical crater under the head bearing stress under the head exceeded what the polymer takes washer or metal backing plate; larger head; spread across more fasteners; thicker section
Boss cracked around an insert radial cracks from the insert outward boss wall too thin, or the insert forced in cold / too fast / crooked thicker boss wall; correct pocket dimensions per datasheet; proper installation temperature and slow, straight pressure
Warped over time on the shelf no fracture; the part simply is not the shape it was residual stress relaxing, or moisture absorption (nylon), or heat exposure print with an appropriate thermal environment (Post 04); anneal to relieve stress deliberately; store away from heat
Sagged under a sustained load no fracture; permanent bend, often at the highest-moment section creep Chapter 1. Change material, reduce stress, put the load in compression, or carry it in metal
Failed after long use at a load it once held easily fracture surface with a smooth region (crack growth) and a rough final region fatigue reduce stress amplitude; eliminate stress raisers; a tougher material; more section
Became brittle and chalky outdoors surface crazing, colour fade, snaps with no warning UV degradation, plus thermal cycling ASA. Paint with a UV-stable coating. Never PLA outdoors
Delaminated mid-print or shortly after horizontal split, often in a tall thin part thermal gradient plus residual stress; poor interlayer bonding enclosure; hotter nozzle; less cooling; shorter/wider geometry (Post 04)
Layers separated at an overhang or bridge localised, at the geometry transition poor support of the region; excessive cooling variation supports or reorientation (Post 03); bridging settings
Snapped where a section changed abruptly fracture right at the step stress concentration at the section change taper or fillet the transition
Part dissolved / softened in service swelling, tackiness, dimensional growth chemical incompatibility with a solvent, oil or cleaner check chemical resistance (Post 04); change material or coat it
Loosened at a press fit over time joint has play that was not there when assembled polymer stress relaxation — the interference decayed do not rely on a plastic press fit for a permanent joint; use a fastener, a snap with a positive lock, or adhesive

Trick: photograph every fracture surface before you throw the part away, with the load direction noted. A folder of these is the most useful engineering reference you will build, because it is entirely about your machine, your materials and your designs.

Chapter 7 — Testing, and when not to print

Test the part, not a coupon

Standardised tensile coupons (ISO 527, ASTM D638) exist for a reason: they let laboratories compare materials on equal terms. They are the right tool for material comparison and the wrong tool for answering "will my bracket hold".

A coupon tells you about a dogbone specimen printed in one orientation. Your part has a specific geometry, specific stress concentrations, a specific orientation, a specific load path, specific infill, and a specific set of fillets and section changes. Those interactions dominate. Test the actual part, loaded the way it will actually be loaded.

A repeatable comparative test rig

You are not trying to produce absolute engineering data. You are trying to answer "is A better than B on my machine". That requires only repeatability and a controlled comparison — both achievable with cheap equipment.

The minimum viable rig:

  1. A fixture that holds the part exactly the same way every time. This is the most important part. Print a jig that clamps the part in one repeatable position. Variation in how the part is held will swamp everything else.
  2. A way to apply load progressively and measure it. Options in ascending order of cost: a bucket you add water or sand to and then weigh; a luggage scale or fish scale pulled by hand; a hanging digital scale with a peak-hold function; a lever arm with hanging weights; an inexpensive load cell with an amplifier logging to a microcontroller.
  3. A record. Load at first audible crack, load at failure, where it failed, a photograph of the fracture.

The disciplines that make the results mean something:

  • Change exactly one variable at a time. Same spool, same day, same profile except the one thing being tested.
  • Print all specimens in the same batch, on the same plate where possible, so filament and machine state are shared.
  • n ≥ 3 per condition. Five is better. Printed parts scatter; a single specimen tells you nothing, exactly as in Post 05.
  • Report the spread, not just the mean. If A's mean beats B's by less than the within-group spread, you have not shown anything.
  • Note the temperature. Polymer strength is temperature-dependent, so a test in a cold garage is not comparable to one in a warm room.

Anisotropy test procedure

Print the same simple beam three times in each of three orientations — flat, on-edge, and upright — with everything else identical. Break each in three-point bending in your fixture. The upright specimens fail across layers; the others do not. The ratio of mean failure loads is your anisotropy figure, for that material and profile.

Common pitfall: comparing A against B when the two batches were printed on different days, from different spools, or with the fixture reassembled in between. Any of those can shift the result by more than the effect you are trying to measure, and the comparison then tells you about the day rather than about the change.

When not to 3D print

This section is the honest one, and it is missing from most 3D printing material because it is not what the audience wants to hear. FDM is a superb process for a specific envelope: one-off or low-volume parts with complex geometry, needed quickly, at low cost, where moderate strength and moderate accuracy suffice. Outside that envelope, something else wins.

Instead consider When it wins Why
Buying the part it exists as a standard component — brackets, hinges, enclosures, knobs, clamps, standoffs, gears, bearings, fasteners a mass-produced part is stronger, cheaper, and available now. Printing a worse copy of a €3 bracket over six hours is a hobby, not engineering. Search first.
CNC machining precision, isotropic strength, metal, surface finish milled aluminium is strong in every direction, dimensionally accurate to a far tighter tolerance, and holds up under heat and sustained load
Injection moulding quantity — hundreds up, and certainly thousands per-part cost collapses once tooling is amortised; parts are isotropic and dimensionally consistent. Print the prototype, mould the product
Sheet metal flat panels, brackets, enclosures, chassis enormously stiff and strong per gram; cheap; laser-cut and bent quickly. Most bracket-shaped problems are sheet-metal problems
Laser cutting (acrylic, ply, MDF) flat parts and panels seconds per part instead of hours, dimensionally excellent in-plane, isotropic
Casting (silicone mould, resin or urethane) tens of copies of a complex shape print the master, mould it, cast in a material with better properties than any filament
Woodwork / metalwork large structures, furniture, anything over a few hundred millimetres a printed part at that scale is slow, expensive, weak and warped
Extruded profile + fasteners frames and structures aluminium extrusion is straight, strong and instant
Hybrid: print + bought parts almost always a printed body carrying bought bearings, shafts, fasteners and springs is the strongest, cheapest and fastest answer far more often than a fully printed one

The productive way to hold this: 3D printing's genuine superpower is geometry, not material. It makes shapes that no other process can make at any price, in a single operation, in an afternoon, with no tooling. Use it for that. When a part's requirement is strength, precision, temperature resistance, or quantity — the things other processes are actually good at — the printer is not the answer, and knowing that is a mark of competence rather than defeat.

The functional-part checklist

Run this before committing a functional part to a long print.

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1 — Feel the anisotropy

Print two identical small beams (Chapter 7's coupon), one flat and one upright. Break both by hand or in a fixture. Note how much force each needed and what the fracture surface looks like. Photograph both fractures.

Solution

Exercise 2 — Measure your Z-strength ratio

Extend Exercise 1 properly: three specimens in each of flat, on-edge and upright, all from one plate, one spool, one profile. Break each in the same fixture. Report the mean and standard deviation for each orientation and the flat-to-upright ratio. Then repeat with the nozzle 15 °C hotter and see how much the ratio moves.

Exercise 3 — Perimeters versus infill, settled empirically

Print six identical beams: three at 2 perimeters / 50 % infill and three at 6 perimeters / 15 % infill. Record the material used and print time for each set. Break all six in three-point bending. Run compare.py on the results.

Solution

Exercise 4 — What a fillet is worth

Print three L-brackets with a sharp internal corner and three identical ones with a fillet of roughly one wall thickness at that corner. Load them until failure in the same fixture. Note where each cracked. Then print three more with a gusset added and repeat.

Solution

Exercise 5 — Creep, observed

Print two identical PLA hooks. Hang the same modest weight on both — well below the load that breaks them. Leave one at room temperature and one somewhere warm (a sunny window, a warm cupboard, near a radiator). Photograph both against a printed reference scale at day 0, day 1, day 7 and day 30. Then repeat the whole experiment in PETG.

Exercise 6 — Build the fixture

Design and print a test fixture that holds your chosen test coupon in exactly the same position every time, and that interfaces with whatever load-measuring device you have. Test its repeatability by breaking five identical specimens and reporting the standard deviation. If that spread is large, improve the fixture before trusting any comparison you make with it.

Exercise 7 — The "should I print this?" audit

List the last ten things you printed. For each, answer: could it have been bought, and for how much? Would another process have been better? Be honest. Then, for the next functional part you design, run the full Chapter 7 checklist before starting the print and note which items caught something.

Instead of solid bulk ribs (0.5–1× wall thick, 2–3× tall, filleted at the root)
Threads heat-set inserts or captive nuts, not printed threads
Fastener heads washer or backing plate; spread the load over several fasteners
Sustained load carry it in metal, or in compression, or both
Vapour smoothing ABS/ASA only; acetone vapour is explosive — ventilate, no ignition sources
Snapped along a layer line reorient. Then hotter, less cooling, thicker layers, enclosure
Cracked at a corner fillet it; add a gusset
Sagged over time creep — change material, reduce stress, or use metal
Testing test the part, not a coupon; n ≥ 3; one variable at a time; report the spread
Don't print it when you can buy it, or when it needs precision, isotropic strength, heat resistance, or quantity
FDM's real superpower geometry — shapes nothing else can make, with no tooling, in an afternoon

Course base complete — what's next

You now have the full base: how the process works, how to calibrate a machine, how slicing decisions map to physical outcomes, how orientation and supports shape a design, what materials do and why, how to reason honestly about dimensions and tolerances, how to model for the process, and how to make parts that survive being used.

The through-line of the whole course, and the thing worth carrying forward: every number in this series is either a physical constant, a published material property with a range, or a value you must measure on your own machine — and the third category is much larger than beginners expect. A course that hands you settings makes you dependent on someone else's setup. A course that hands you procedures makes you able to characterise any machine you sit down at. That was the goal.
