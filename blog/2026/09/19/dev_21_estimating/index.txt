<h2 id="The-planning-fallacy"><a href="#The-planning-fallacy" class="headerlink" title="The planning fallacy"></a>The planning fallacy</h2>
<p>Kahneman and Tversky described the <strong>planning fallacy</strong> in 1979 (&quot;Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures&quot;): people predict how long their own tasks will take by imagining how the work will go, and they underweight what usually happens to tasks like it. Their forecasts come out too optimistic.</p>
<p>Buehler, Griffin and Ross tested it in 1994 (&quot;Exploring the 'planning fallacy'&quot;, <em>Journal of Personality and Social Psychology</em> 67(3), 366-381). In Study 1, students predicted when they would finish their honours thesis:</p>

<h2 id="Hofstadters-law"><a href="#Hofstadters-law" class="headerlink" title="Hofstadter's law"></a>Hofstadter's law</h2>
<p>An aphorism, not a research finding, from Douglas Hofstadter's <em>Gödel, Escher, Bach</em> (1979). It says a task always takes longer than you expect — even when you allow for Hofstadter's law. It is a joke with a true point — knowing about the bias does not remove it. You need a <strong>method</strong>, not willpower.</p>
<h2 id="The-cone-of-uncertainty-a-model"><a href="#The-cone-of-uncertainty-a-model" class="headerlink" title="The cone of uncertainty (a model)"></a>The cone of uncertainty (a model)</h2>
<p>Barry Boehm drew a &quot;funnel curve&quot; in <em>Software Engineering Economics</em> (1981); Steve McConnell later popularised it as the <strong>cone of uncertainty</strong>. The idea: at the initial concept stage, the real effort can be anywhere from about <strong>0.25×</strong> to <strong>4×</strong> the estimate (a 16× range). The range narrows as decisions are made — requirements, design, code.</p>
<p>Treat it as a <strong>model</strong>, with two caveats:</p>
<ul>
<li>McConnell himself says the cone shows the <strong>best</strong> accuracy skilled estimators can get. It is easy to do worse.</li>
<li>The cone does not narrow by itself. Todd Little (IEEE Software, 2006) studied projects at Landmark Graphics and found estimate errors followed a right-skewed (lognormal) distribution, and the uncertainty range stayed nearly the same through the project — against the popular reading of the cone. Uncertainty shrinks only when you actively remove it.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="What-people-forget"><a href="#What-people-forget" class="headerlink" title="What people forget"></a>What people forget</h2>
<p>Most slips are not bad maths. They are missing work:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unknown unknowns</strong> — the library that does not support your case; the API that is rate-limited.</li>
<li><strong>Integration</strong> — each part works alone; wiring them together takes days.</li>
<li><strong>Review and rework</strong> — code review, QA feedback, security review.</li>
<li><strong>Tests, docs, deploy, config, migrations, monitoring.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Everything else</strong> — meetings, support, on-call, holidays, sick days, context switching.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Break-the-work-down"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Break-the-work-down" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Break the work down"></a>Chapter 2 — Break the work down</h1>
<p>Big tasks hide work. Small tasks expose it. Keep splitting until each task is <strong>one day or less</strong> and has a clear &quot;done&quot;.</p>
<p>A bad task: &quot;Add user sign-up&quot; (5 days?). A breakdown:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Add user sign-up</span><br><span class="line">├── DB: users table + migration + unique email index          0.5 d</span><br><span class="line">├── API: POST /signup (validation, password hashing)          1   d</span><br><span class="line">├── API: send verification email (template, provider setup)   1   d</span><br><span class="line">├── API: GET /verify?token=... (expiry, reuse)                0.5 d</span><br><span class="line">├── UI: sign-up form + error states                           1   d</span><br><span class="line">├── UI: &quot;check your email&quot; + verified pages                   0.5 d</span><br><span class="line">├── Tests: unit + one end-to-end happy path                   1   d</span><br><span class="line">├── Rate limit + audit log on /signup                         0.5 d</span><br><span class="line">└── Review fixes, docs, deploy, monitoring                    1   d</span><br><span class="line">                                                   total      7   d</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The first guess of &quot;5 days&quot; became 7 once the work was visible, and there is still no buffer.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> keep a personal checklist of forgotten work (tests, review, migration, config, docs, deploy, monitoring, feature flag, rollback plan) and run through it for every estimate.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> if you cannot break a task down, you do not understand it yet. That is a signal for a <strong>spike</strong> (Chapter 4), not for a bigger number.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Three-point-PERT-estimates"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Three-point-PERT-estimates" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Three-point (PERT) estimates"></a>Chapter 3 — Three-point (PERT) estimates</h1>
<p>A single number hides uncertainty. Ask three questions for each task:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>O — optimistic:</strong> if everything goes well.</li>
<li><strong>M — most likely:</strong> the usual case.</li>
<li><strong>P — pessimistic:</strong> if things go badly (but not a disaster).</li>
</ul>
<p>The PERT formula, from the US Navy's Polaris programme in 1958, weights the most likely value four times:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Expected  E  = (O + 4M + P) / 6</span><br><span class="line">Spread    SD ≈ (P - O) / 6</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>

<p>Two things to notice:</p>
<ol>
<li>The sum of &quot;most likely&quot; values is 8.5 days, but the expected total is 9.92. Tasks can run long by much more than they can run short, so the mean sits to the right of the mode.</li>
<li>The total SD is <strong>not</strong> the sum of SDs. If tasks are independent, variances add: <code>sqrt(0.50² + 0.42² + 0.67² + 0.83² + 0.33²) ≈ 1.29</code>. Real tasks are often linked (one bad week hits all of them), so treat this as a lower bound on the spread.</li>
</ol>
<p>A rough 80% figure with a normal approximation: <code>9.92 + 0.84 × 1.29 ≈ 11</code> days.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Ranges-outside-view-spikes-and-buffers"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Ranges-outside-view-spikes-and-buffers" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Ranges, outside view, spikes and buffers"></a>Chapter 4 — Ranges, outside view, spikes and buffers</h1>
<h2 id="Give-a-range-with-a-confidence-level"><a href="#Give-a-range-with-a-confidence-level" class="headerlink" title="Give a range with a confidence level"></a>Give a range with a confidence level</h2>
<p>A single date sounds precise but carries no information about risk. Say instead:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>&quot;Most likely around 10-11 working days. I am 80% confident it is done within 13, assuming the email provider approves our account this week.&quot;</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Use the same words every time — P50, P80, P95 — so people learn what they mean. A P80 date is missed one time in five; say so up front.</p>
<h2 id="Reference-class-forecasting-the-outside-view"><a href="#Reference-class-forecasting-the-outside-view" class="headerlink" title="Reference-class forecasting (the outside view)"></a>Reference-class forecasting (the outside view)</h2>
<p>Your plan is the <strong>inside view</strong>. The <strong>outside view</strong> asks: how long did similar work take before? Bent Flyvbjerg built this into <strong>reference-class forecasting</strong> (&quot;From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right&quot;, <em>Project Management Journal</em>, 2006), based on Kahneman and Tversky's work. Three steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Pick a reference class of past, similar projects — broad enough to have data, narrow enough to be comparable.</li>
<li>Find the distribution of outcomes in that class (for example, actual ÷ estimate).</li>
<li>Place your project in that distribution, and adjust your estimate with it.</li>
</ol>
<p>For a developer, the reference class is often your own history: &quot;The last four API integrations took 1.4× to 2× my estimate.&quot;</p>
<h2 id="Spikes-for-unknowns"><a href="#Spikes-for-unknowns" class="headerlink" title="Spikes for unknowns"></a>Spikes for unknowns</h2>
<p>A <strong>spike</strong> comes from Extreme Programming: a small, throw-away experiment to reduce a technical risk or make an estimate more reliable. Time-box it (&quot;half a day to prove the PDF library can do Chinese fonts&quot;), then estimate with the answer in hand.</p>
<h2 id="Explicit-buffers"><a href="#Explicit-buffers" class="headerlink" title="Explicit buffers"></a>Explicit buffers</h2>
<p>Do not pad every task in secret. Padding gets spent (work expands to fill the time) and nobody can see the risk. Instead, estimate tasks honestly and add <strong>one visible buffer line</strong> at the project level, sized from the P50-P80 gap or from named risks. Report how much buffer is left as the project goes on.</p>
<hr>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Communicating-estimates"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Communicating-estimates" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Communicating estimates"></a>Chapter 7 — Communicating estimates</h1>
<h2 id="Keep-estimate-target-and-commitment-apart"><a href="#Keep-estimate-target-and-commitment-apart" class="headerlink" title="Keep estimate, target and commitment apart"></a>Keep estimate, target and commitment apart</h2>
<p>McConnell's <em>Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art</em> (2006) separates three things that people often mix up:</p>
<ul>
<li>an <strong>estimate</strong> is a prediction (&quot;P80 is 13 days&quot;);</li>
<li>a <strong>target</strong> is a business wish (&quot;we want it before the conference&quot;);</li>
<li>a <strong>commitment</strong> is a promise (&quot;we will ship the core flow by Oct 21&quot;).</li>
</ul>
<p>When a target is shorter than the estimate, do not shrink the estimate. Change the scope, the people, or the date — and say which.</p>
<h2 id="A-good-estimate-message"><a href="#A-good-estimate-message" class="headerlink" title="A good estimate message"></a>A good estimate message</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>The range</strong> and its confidence: &quot;P50 Oct 19, P80 Oct 21.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>The assumptions</strong>: &quot;Assumes the email provider approves us by Friday; excludes the admin screen.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>The biggest risks</strong>: &quot;Email approval (30% chance of +3 days).&quot;</li>
<li><strong>When you will update it</strong>: &quot;I will re-estimate after the spike on Wednesday.&quot;</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="Re-estimate-when-the-facts-change"><a href="#Re-estimate-when-the-facts-change" class="headerlink" title="Re-estimate when the facts change"></a>Re-estimate when the facts change</h2>
<p>An estimate is a forecast, not a vow. When scope grows or a risk happens, update the range at once and say why. Bad news early is useful; bad news on the due date is not.</p>
<h2 id="Story-points-vs-hours"><a href="#Story-points-vs-hours" class="headerlink" title="Story points vs hours"></a>Story points vs hours</h2>
<p>Story points are a <strong>team convention</strong>, not a law. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention story points at all; it only says the Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it. Ron Jeffries, who says he may have invented story points, wrote in 2019 that &quot;if I did, I'm sorry now&quot;. He explains they started as &quot;ideal days&quot; and recommends slicing stories small instead. Use points or hours as your team prefers — the ideas in this post (breakdown, ranges, tracking actuals) work the same with both.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Practice-Exercises"><a href="#Practice-Exercises" class="headerlink" title="Practice Exercises"></a>Practice Exercises</h1>
<h2 id="Exercise-1-—-One-PERT-estimate"><a href="#Exercise-1-—-One-PERT-estimate" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 1 — One PERT estimate"></a>Exercise 1 — One PERT estimate</h2>
<p>A task has O = 2, M = 3, P = 10 days. Compute E and SD. Why is E so far above M?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>E = (2 + 4 × 3 + 10) / 6 = 24 / 6 = <strong>4.0 days</strong>. SD = (10 − 2) / 6 ≈ <strong>1.33 days</strong>. The pessimistic value is 7 days above M but the optimistic is only 1 day below, so the long right tail pulls the mean up.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-2-—-Add-up-three-tasks"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-Add-up-three-tasks" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — Add up three tasks"></a>Exercise 2 — Add up three tasks</h2>
<p>Tasks (O, M, P): A (1, 2, 3), B (2, 3, 7), C (1, 1, 4). Give the total expected days, the total SD (assume independence), and a rough P80.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>E: A = 12/6 = 2.0, B = 21/6 = 3.5, C = 9/6 = 1.5. Total <strong>7.0 days</strong>.<br>
SD: A = 0.33, B = 0.83, C = 0.5. Total = sqrt(0.111 + 0.694 + 0.25) = sqrt(1.055) ≈ <strong>1.03 days</strong>.<br>
Rough P80 = 7.0 + 0.84 × 1.03 ≈ <strong>7.9 days</strong>, so &quot;about 8 days, 80% confident&quot;.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-3-—-Use-your-own-history"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Use-your-own-history" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — Use your own history"></a>Exercise 3 — Use your own history</h2>
<p>Your log shows a median actual/estimate ratio of 1.5, with 80% of ratios at or below 1.9. Your gut says a new task takes 6 days. What range do you give?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Median: 6 × 1.5 = <strong>9 days</strong>. 80% level: 6 × 1.9 ≈ <strong>11.4 days</strong>. Say &quot;about 9 days, 80% confident within 12&quot;, and state the assumptions.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Break-it-down"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Break-it-down" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Break it down"></a>Exercise 4 — Break it down</h2>
<p>Break &quot;Add CSV export to the orders page&quot; into tasks of one day or less. Include the work people forget.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>One possible list: define columns and format with the product owner (0.25 d); backend endpoint that streams rows (1 d); handle large exports — paging or a background job (1 d); encoding — UTF-8 with BOM if users open it in Excel (0.25 d, see Dev Essentials 01 · Text Encoding); date and number formats (0.25 d); UI button, loading state, errors (0.5 d); permission check (0.25 d); tests (0.5 d); review fixes, docs, deploy (0.5 d). Total ≈ 4.5 days before buffer.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Add-a-risk-to-the-simulation"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Add-a-risk-to-the-simulation" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Add a risk to the simulation"></a>Exercise 5 — Add a risk to the simulation</h2>
<p>Add <code>(&quot;Security review asks for changes&quot;, 0.20, 2.0)</code> to <code>RISKS</code> and run the script again. What happens to P50 and P80?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>With seed 42 the output becomes P50 ≈ 11.0 and P80 ≈ 13.0 working days (was 10.6 and 12.6), and the chance of meeting the &quot;most likely&quot; sum drops to about 9%. A 20% chance of 2 extra days adds about 0.4 days on average, but it also widens the tail.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Rewrite-the-message"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Rewrite-the-message" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Rewrite the message"></a>Exercise 6 — Rewrite the message</h2>
<p>Rewrite this status update: &quot;It'll be done Friday.&quot;</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>&quot;Most likely Thursday (P50); 80% confident by next Tuesday. This assumes the payments sandbox is back up tomorrow and excludes the refund screen. Biggest risk: the provider's webhook format — I am running a half-day spike on it today and will update the estimate tomorrow noon.&quot;</p>
</details>

<h1 id="Cheat-sheet"><a href="#Cheat-sheet" class="headerlink" title="Cheat-sheet"></a>Cheat-sheet</h1>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Idea</th>
<th>Rule of thumb</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Planning fallacy</td>
<td>Gut estimates are optimistic — even &quot;worst case&quot; ones (Buehler et al., 1994)</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Hofstadter's law</td>
<td>An aphorism: knowing the bias does not remove it</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Cone of uncertainty</td>
<td>A model: ~0.25×-4× at concept stage; narrows only as decisions remove uncertainty</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Breakdown</td>
<td>Split until each task ≤ 1 day with a clear &quot;done&quot;</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Forgotten work</td>
<td>Integration, tests, review, docs, deploy, migrations, meetings</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>PERT</td>
<td><code>E = (O + 4M + P) / 6</code>, <code>SD ≈ (P − O) / 6</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Summing</td>
<td>Means add; variances add (if independent); SDs do <strong>not</strong> add</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Ranges</td>
<td>Say P50 / P80 / P95, not one date</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Outside view</td>
<td>Compare with similar past work; use your actual/estimate ratio</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Spike</td>
<td>Time-boxed experiment before estimating an unknown</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Buffer</td>
<td>One visible project-level line, not hidden padding</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Monte Carlo</td>
<td>Sample each task 10,000+ times; read percentiles</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Communicate</td>
<td>Range + confidence + assumptions + risks + next update</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Estimate ≠ target ≠ commitment</td>
<td>Change scope/people/date, not the estimate</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Story points</td>
<td>A team convention; not required by the Scrum Guide</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
