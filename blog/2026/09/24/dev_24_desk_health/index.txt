<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Digital-eye-strain"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Digital-eye-strain" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Digital eye strain"></a>Chapter 2 — Digital eye strain</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Digital eye strain</strong> (also called computer vision syndrome) — tired, dry, sore or blurry eyes and headaches after long screen use.</li>
<li><strong>Accommodation</strong> — the eye's focusing effort. Near work keeps the focusing muscles working.</li>
<li><strong>Glare</strong> — bright light reflecting off the screen or shining into your eyes.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Causes"><a href="#Causes" class="headerlink" title="Causes"></a>Causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Less blinking.</strong> The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) says people normally blink about 15 times a minute, but only about 5 to 7 times a minute while using computers. The eye surface dries.</li>
<li><strong>Long near focus</strong> without a break.</li>
<li><strong>Glare and poor lighting.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Screen too close, too high or too far.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Uncorrected vision problems.</strong> The American Optometric Association (AOA) lists them as a cause. An eye test can fix what no desk setup can.</li>
</ul>
<p>The AAO states that eye strain does not injure the eye and does not cause permanent damage. It is still worth fixing: it is uncomfortable and makes work harder.</p>
<h2 id="The-20-20-20-rule-—-expert-guidance-one-small-trial"><a href="#The-20-20-20-rule-—-expert-guidance-one-small-trial" class="headerlink" title="The 20-20-20 rule — expert guidance; one small trial"></a>The 20-20-20 rule — <em>expert guidance; one small trial</em></h2>
<p><strong>Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet (about 6 m) away for at least 20 seconds.</strong> Both the AAO and the AOA recommend it. The AOA also suggests resting the eyes for 15 minutes after two hours of continuous computer use (<em>expert guidance</em>).</p>
<p>What does research say? A 2023 study in <em>Contact Lens and Anterior Eye</em> (Talens-Estarelles and colleagues) gave 29 computer users break-reminder software for two weeks. Eye strain and dry-eye symptoms improved while they used it, but the gains were gone one week after they stopped. Binocular vision and tear-film measures did not change. The study is small and short, so treat it as <em>limited evidence</em> that the habit helps <strong>while you keep it up</strong>.</p>

<h2 id="Lighting-and-glare-—-expert-guidance"><a href="#Lighting-and-glare-—-expert-guidance" class="headerlink" title="Lighting and glare — expert guidance"></a>Lighting and glare — <em>expert guidance</em></h2>
<ul>
<li>Place the monitor <strong>side-on to windows</strong> (OSHA: perpendicular to the window), not facing them or with them behind it. Use blinds (AOA).</li>
<li>Match screen brightness to the room (AAO). A bright screen in a dark room, or a dim screen in a bright room, both strain.</li>
<li>Tilting the screen back too far can catch overhead lights (OSHA). Tilt it so it faces your line of sight.</li>
<li>A matte screen filter can cut glare (AAO).</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Text-size-—-rule-of-thumb"><a href="#Text-size-—-rule-of-thumb" class="headerlink" title="Text size — rule of thumb"></a>Text size — <em>rule of thumb</em></h2>
<p>If you lean in to read, the text is too small. Increase the zoom or the system scaling instead of moving closer. OSHA notes text may need to be bigger on smaller monitors.</p>
<h2 id="Blue-light-and-dry-eyes"><a href="#Blue-light-and-dry-eyes" class="headerlink" title="Blue light and dry eyes"></a>Blue light and dry eyes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Blue-light glasses</strong> — <em>moderate evidence against</em>. The AAO does not recommend them and says there is no scientific evidence that light from computer screens damages the eyes. A 2023 Cochrane review (17 studies, 619 people) found blue-light filtering lenses may not reduce eye strain from computer use in the short term.</li>
<li><strong>Dry eyes</strong> — blink on purpose during breaks, and the AAO suggests artificial tears when eyes feel dry (<em>expert guidance</em>).</li>
<li><strong>Dark mode</strong> — <em>anecdotal</em>. Many people find it comfortable, especially at night. There is no strong reason to choose one theme over the other; use what feels better.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> buying gadgets before booking an eye test. An out-of-date prescription, or none at all, is a common cause that no filter can fix.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-3-—-Posture-and-setup"><a href="#Chapter-3-—-Posture-and-setup" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 3 — Posture and setup"></a>Chapter 3 — Posture and setup</h1>
<p><img src="/blog/assets/dev/diagram_24_workstation.svg" alt="Side view of a workstation with the typical targets for screen height, angle and distance, elbows, back and feet"></p>
<h2 id="OSHAs-neutral-posture-—-expert-guidance"><a href="#OSHAs-neutral-posture-—-expert-guidance" class="headerlink" title="OSHA's neutral posture — expert guidance"></a>OSHA's neutral posture — <em>expert guidance</em></h2>
<p>OSHA's eTool describes a neutral working posture:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hands, wrists and forearms</strong> straight, in line, roughly parallel to the floor.</li>
<li><strong>Elbows</strong> close to the body, bent <strong>between 90 and 120 degrees</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Shoulders</strong> relaxed, upper arms hanging normally.</li>
<li><strong>Feet</strong> fully supported by the floor, or by a footrest if the desk is not adjustable.</li>
<li><strong>Thighs</strong> supported and roughly parallel to the floor; <strong>knees</strong> about level with the hips.</li>
<li><strong>Back</strong> fully supported, with lumbar (lower-back) support, upright or leaning back slightly.</li>
<li><strong>Head</strong> level, facing forward, balanced over the torso.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Set-up-in-this-order"><a href="#Set-up-in-this-order" class="headerlink" title="Set up in this order"></a>Set up in this order</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Chair first.</strong> Set the height so your whole foot rests on the floor and the back of the knee is slightly higher than the seat (OSHA). The seat should support most of your thigh without pressing into the back of the knee. Move the lumbar support to fit the small of your back.</li>
<li><strong>Keyboard at elbow height.</strong> OSHA: elbows about the same height as the keyboard, wrists straight, not bent up, down or sideways. Put the mouse right next to the keyboard to avoid reaching.</li>
<li><strong>If the desk is too high</strong>, raise the chair and add a footrest. If it is too low, raise the desk.</li>
<li><strong>Screen last</strong>, using Chapter 2's targets.</li>
</ol>
<p>Cornell University's ergonomics guide adds that a slightly <strong>reclined</strong> seated posture of about 100-110 degrees is better than a bolt-upright 90 degrees (<em>expert guidance</em>).</p>
<h2 id="Laptops-—-expert-guidance"><a href="#Laptops-—-expert-guidance" class="headerlink" title="Laptops — expert guidance"></a>Laptops — <em>expert guidance</em></h2>
<p>Cornell's guide calls laptop design &quot;inherently&quot; problematic: when the screen is at a good height, the keyboard is not, and the other way round. For regular long sessions:</p>
<ul>
<li>Raise the laptop on a <strong>stand</strong> so the top of the screen is near eye level.</li>
<li>Use an <strong>external keyboard and mouse</strong> at elbow height.</li>
<li>Or use an external monitor and keep the laptop as a second screen.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-best-posture-is-the-next-posture-—-expert-guidance"><a href="#The-best-posture-is-the-next-posture-—-expert-guidance" class="headerlink" title="The best posture is the next posture — expert guidance"></a>The best posture is the next posture — <em>expert guidance</em></h2>
<p>No single position is safe for eight hours. Workplace-safety bodies such as WorkSafeNB (Canada) use the phrase &quot;the best posture is your next posture&quot;, and OSHA advises changing position through small adjustments, stretching, standing and walking. A 2019 viewpoint in the <em>Journal of Orthopaedic &amp; Sports Physical Therapy</em> (Slater and colleagues, <em>expert opinion</em>) argues there is little strong evidence that &quot;sitting up straight&quot; prevents back pain. The practical message: set up a comfortable, supported base position, then <strong>move often</strong>.</p>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Sitting-time-breaks-and-standing-desks"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Sitting-time-breaks-and-standing-desks" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Sitting time, breaks and standing desks"></a>Chapter 4 — Sitting time, breaks and standing desks</h1>
<h2 id="What-the-Cochrane-review-found-—-limited-evidence"><a href="#What-the-Cochrane-review-found-—-limited-evidence" class="headerlink" title="What the Cochrane review found — limited evidence"></a>What the Cochrane review found — <em>limited evidence</em></h2>
<p>The Cochrane review &quot;Workplace interventions for reducing sitting at work&quot; (Shrestha and colleagues, 2018; searches up to August 2017) is the latest version found for this post. It included <strong>34 studies with 3,397 office workers</strong> from high-income countries.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sit-stand desks</strong> reduced workplace sitting by about <strong>84 to 116 minutes per workday</strong> in the short term, and about 57 minutes a day at 3-12 months.</li>
<li>The <strong>quality of evidence was low to very low</strong> for most interventions, mainly due to study design limits and small samples.</li>
<li>The effects of policies, information and counselling were mostly inconsistent.</li>
<li>There was no evidence on longer-term effects, and the review did not show health benefits such as less disease.</li>
</ul>
<p>So: a sit-stand desk probably helps you sit less, at least at first. Whether that improves long-term health is not proven.</p>
<h2 id="Break-patterns"><a href="#Break-patterns" class="headerlink" title="Break patterns"></a>Break patterns</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cornell's 20-8-2 pattern</strong> — <em>rule of thumb</em>. About 20 minutes sitting, 8 minutes standing, 2 minutes moving and stretching. Cornell presents it as a &quot;ball park goal&quot;, not an exact prescription.</li>
<li><strong>Cornell's break types</strong> — <em>expert guidance</em>: short eye breaks, micro-breaks (under 2 minutes), and a rest break every 30-60 minutes where you stand up and move.</li>
<li><strong>Standing all day is not the goal.</strong> Long, still standing has its own discomfort. Alternate.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-RSI-warning-signs-—-expert-guidance"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-RSI-warning-signs-—-expert-guidance" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — RSI warning signs — expert guidance"></a>Chapter 6 — RSI warning signs — <em>expert guidance</em></h1>
<p>Repetitive strain injury (RSI) is pain in muscles, nerves and tendons from repeated movement and overuse. Typing is one of the listed causes, along with poor posture at work. The NHS lists symptoms that usually start gradually:</p>
<ul>
<li>Pain that may feel <strong>burning, aching or throbbing</strong></li>
<li><strong>Stiffness</strong> and <strong>weakness</strong></li>
<li><strong>Tingling, pins and needles, or numbness</strong></li>
<li>Muscle <strong>cramps</strong></li>
<li><strong>Swelling</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>The NHS advises seeing a GP (a family doctor) if symptoms <strong>do not go away or get worse</strong>. Numbness, tingling or weakness deserve a professional look sooner rather than later.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> pushing through wrist or forearm pain to finish a sprint. Early, mild symptoms are the cheap time to change your setup, take breaks and get advice. Do not self-diagnose from the internet.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Sleep-and-learning"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Sleep-and-learning" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Sleep and learning"></a>Chapter 7 — Sleep and learning</h1>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sleep helps memory</strong> — <em>strong evidence (mainly lab studies)</em>. A major review (Rasch and Born, <em>Physiological Reviews</em>, 2013) sums up over a century of research: sleep after learning helps you keep what you learned, and memories are actively consolidated during sleep.</li>
<li><strong>How much</strong> — <em>expert guidance</em>. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society (2015 consensus) recommend <strong>7 or more hours per night</strong> on a regular basis for adults aged 18 to 60.</li>
<li><strong>Practical rule</strong> — <em>rule of thumb</em>. Study or learn a new tool earlier in the day, then sleep, instead of cutting sleep to cram late at night. A tired brain also writes more bugs (see Dev Essentials 10 · Debugging).</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Practice-Exercises"><a href="#Practice-Exercises" class="headerlink" title="Practice Exercises"></a>Practice Exercises</h1>
<h2 id="Exercise-1-—-Measure-and-check"><a href="#Exercise-1-—-Measure-and-check" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 1 — Measure and check"></a>Exercise 1 — Measure and check</h2>
<p>Measure the eight values in <code>desk_check.py</code> for your own desk and run it.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Measure while sitting normally, not sitting up extra straight for the tape measure. Fix items in the order of Chapter 3: chair, then keyboard, then screen. Changing the chair height changes every other number, so re-measure after each change.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-2-—-Screen-height-from-the-angle"><a href="#Exercise-2-—-Screen-height-from-the-angle" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 2 — Screen height from the angle"></a>Exercise 2 — Screen height from the angle</h2>
<p>Your eyes are 120 cm from the floor and the screen is 60 cm away. At what heights should the screen centre be for a 15° and a 20° downward gaze?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> math</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">for</span> deg <span class="hljs-keyword">in</span> (<span class="hljs-number">15</span>, <span class="hljs-number">20</span>):</span><br><span class="line">    drop = <span class="hljs-number">60</span> * math.tan(math.radians(deg))</span><br><span class="line">    <span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(deg, <span class="hljs-built_in">round</span>(<span class="hljs-number">120</span> - drop, <span class="hljs-number">1</span>))   <span class="hljs-comment"># 15 -&gt; 103.9 cm, 20 -&gt; 98.2 cm</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The screen centre belongs about 98-104 cm from the floor, which is 16-22 cm below the eyes.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-3-—-Label-the-evidence"><a href="#Exercise-3-—-Label-the-evidence" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 3 — Label the evidence"></a>Exercise 3 — Label the evidence</h2>
<p>Label each claim: (a) &quot;a sit-stand desk makes you sit less&quot;, (b) &quot;blue-light glasses cure eye strain&quot;, (c) &quot;look 6 m away every 20 minutes&quot;, (d) &quot;20 minutes sitting, 8 standing, 2 moving&quot;.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>(a) <em>Limited evidence</em> for less sitting in the short term (Cochrane: low to very low quality); health benefits unproven. (b) The evidence points the other way: the AAO does not recommend them, and a Cochrane review found they may not reduce eye strain. (c) <em>Expert guidance</em> from the AAO and AOA, plus one small trial that found benefits only while people kept doing it. (d) <em>Rule of thumb</em> (Cornell calls it a ball-park goal).</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-4-—-Glare-audit"><a href="#Exercise-4-—-Glare-audit" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 4 — Glare audit"></a>Exercise 4 — Glare audit</h2>
<p>Sit at your desk at the time of day you work most. Turn the screen off and look at the black glass. What do you see reflected?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>Windows or ceiling lights in the reflection are glare sources. Turn the desk so the screen is side-on to windows, close blinds, reduce the backward tilt, or move the light. Turn the screen on again and match its brightness to the room.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-5-—-Break-routine"><a href="#Exercise-5-—-Break-routine" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 5 — Break routine"></a>Exercise 5 — Break routine</h2>
<p>Run <code>breaks.py</code> for one working day. Count how many reminders you actually followed.</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>If you ignored most, the interval or the action is wrong for you, not the idea. Try linking breaks to natural pauses instead: after each commit, each test run, or each meeting. The 2023 trial suggests the benefit lasts only while the habit does, so choose something you can keep.</p>
</details>
<h2 id="Exercise-6-—-Laptop-fix-plan"><a href="#Exercise-6-—-Laptop-fix-plan" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 6 — Laptop fix plan"></a>Exercise 6 — Laptop fix plan</h2>
<p>Using the laptop output in Chapter 8, what is the smallest change that fixes all three <code>FIX</code> lines?</p>
<details>
<summary>Solution</summary>
<p>A laptop stand that raises the screen by about 13-18 cm, plus an external keyboard and mouse placed at elbow height. Check it with the script: raising by 15 cm gives a screen top 8 cm below the eyes and a centre about 18° down. Raising by 23 cm would put the top at eye level but the centre only about 10° down, which is too high. Raising the chair instead would fix the keyboard line but make the screen angle worse.</p>
</details>
