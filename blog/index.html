Dev Essentials — 24 · Desk Health — Eye Strain, Posture, Movement and Setup
Created2026-09-24|Dev Essentials
Developers spend thousands of hours a year at a screen. Dry, tired eyes, a stiff neck and sore wrists are common, and mostly avoidable. This last post of the series covers eye strain, desk and chair setup, movement and breaks, simple stretches, warning signs, and sleep. Each recommendation carries an evidence label, because some advice rests on solid research and some is just sensible habit. It ends with a checklist and a small script to measure your own setup, then looks back over the whole series. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics → 24 Desk Health (this post). Part E · Growth outside code. > Watch out: this post is educational, not medical advice. See a doctor, physiotherapist or eye-care professional if you have pain that does not go away, numbness or tingling, weakness, or any change in your vision. Do not wait for a checklist to tell you something is wrong. Learning Outcome - Read the evidence labels and know what each one means.- Explain what causes digital eye strain and apply the 20-20-20 rule.- Set screen distance, screen height, lighting and text size to published targets.- Set chair, desk, keyboard and mouse for a neutral posture, including on a laptop.- Explain why movement matters more than one "perfect" posture.- Say what the Cochrane review found about sit-stand desks.- Recognise the warning signs of repetitive strain injury (RSI).- Measure your own setup and check it with a short script. Chapter 1 — Evidence labels | Label | Meaning ||-------|---------|| Strong evidence | Many good studies agree; systematic reviews support it || Moderate evidence | Some good studies or a review, but with limits (short follow-up, mixed results) || Limited evidence | Few, small or low-quality studies; could change with better research || Expert guidance | Recommended by official or professional bodies (OSHA, AOA, AAO, NHS), mostly on ergonomic principles rather than trials || Rule of thumb | A handy simplification; useful, but the exact numbers are not research results || Anecdotal | People report it helps; no good evidence either way | Most desk advice is expert guidance. That is not a weakness; it just means you should adjust it to your body instead of treating the numbers as exact. Chapter 2 — Digital eye strain Terminology - Digital eye strain (also called computer vision syndrome) — tired, dry, sore or blurry eyes and headaches after long screen use.- Accommodation — the eye's focusing effort. Near work keeps the focusing muscles working.- Glare — bright light reflecting off the screen or shining into your eyes. Causes - Less blinking. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) says people normally blink about 15 times a minute, but only about 5 to 7 times a minute while using computers. The eye surface dries.- Long near focus without a break.- Glare and poor lighting.- Screen too close, too high or too far.- Uncorrected vision problems. The American Optometric Association (AOA) lists them as a cause. An eye test can fix what no desk setup can. The AAO states that eye strain does not injure the eye and does not cause permanent damage. It is still worth fixing: it is uncomfortable and makes work harder. The 20-20-20 rule — expert guidance; one small trial Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet (about 6 m) away for at least 20 seconds. Both the AAO and the AOA recommend it. The AOA also suggests resting the eyes for 15 minutes after two hours of continuous computer use (expert guidance). What does research say? A 2023 study in Contact Lens and Anterior Eye (Talens-Estarelles and colleagues) gave 29 computer users break-reminder software for two weeks. Eye strain and dry-eye symptoms improved while they used it, but the gains were gone one week after they stopped. Binocular vision and tear-film measures did not change. The study is small and short, so treat it as limited evidence that the habit helps while you keep it up. Screen distance and height — expert guidance | Source | Distance | Height ||--------|----------|--------|| OSHA computer workstations eTool | 20-40 in (50-100 cm), eye to screen | Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level; centre of the screen 15-20° below horizontal eye level || AOA | 20-28 in (about 51-71 cm) | Centre of the screen 15-20° below eye level (about 4-5 in, 10-13 cm) || AAO | About 25 in (about 64 cm), roughly arm's length | Gaze slightly downward | They agree: about an arm's length away, top edge near eye level, and you look slightly down. OSHA adds that people with bifocals may need the screen lower, so they do not tilt their head back to read through the bottom of the lens. Lighting and glare — expert guidance - Place the monitor side-on to windows (OSHA: perpendicular to the window), not facing them or with them behind it. Use blinds (AOA).- Match screen brightness to the room (AAO). A bright screen in a dark room, or a dim screen in a bright room, both strain.- Tilting the screen back too far can catch overhead lights (OSHA). Tilt it so it faces your line of sight.- A matte screen filter can cut glare (AAO). Text size — rule of thumb If you lean in to read, the text is too small. Increase the zoom or the system scaling instead of moving closer. OSHA notes text may need to be bigger on smaller monitors. Blue light and dry eyes - Blue-light glasses — moderate evidence against. The AAO does not recommend them and says there is no scientific evidence that light from computer screens damages the eyes. A 2023 Cochrane review (17 studies, 619 people) found blue-light filtering lenses may not reduce eye strain from computer use in the short term.- Dry eyes — blink on purpose during breaks, and the AAO suggests artificial tears when eyes feel dry (expert guidance).- Dark mode — anecdotal. Many people find it comfortable, especially at night. There is no strong reason to choose one theme over the other; use what feels better. > Common pitfall: buying gadgets before booking an eye test. An out-of-date prescription, or none at all, is a common cause that no filter can fix. Chapter 3 — Posture and setup OSHA's neutral posture — expert guidance OSHA's eTool describes a neutral working posture: - Hands, wrists and forearms straight, in line, roughly parallel to the floor.- Elbows close to the body, bent between 90 and 120 degrees.- Shoulders relaxed, upper arms hanging normally.- Feet fully supported by the floor, or by a footrest if the desk is not adjustable.- Thighs supported and roughly parallel to the floor; knees about level with the hips.- Back fully supported, with lumbar (lower-back) support, upright or leaning back slightly.- Head level, facing forward, balanced over the torso. Set up in this order 1. Chair first. Set the height so your whole foot rests on the floor and the back of the knee is slightly higher than the seat (OSHA). The seat should support most of your thigh without pressing into the back of the knee. Move the lumbar support to fit the small of your back.2. Keyboard at elbow height. OSHA: elbows about the same height as the keyboard, wrists straight, not bent up, down or sideways. Put the mouse right next to the keyboard to avoid reaching.3. If the desk is too high, raise the chair and add a footrest. If it is too low, raise the desk.4. Screen last, using Chapter 2's targets. Cornell University's ergonomics guide adds that a slightly reclined seated posture of about 100-110 degrees is better than a bolt-upright 90 degrees (expert guidance). Laptops — expert guidance Cornell's guide calls laptop design "inherently" problematic: when the screen is at a good height, the keyboard is not, and the other way round. For regular long sessions: - Raise the laptop on a stand so the top of the screen is near eye level.- Use an external keyboard and mouse at elbow height.- Or use an external monitor and keep the laptop as a second screen. The best posture is the next posture — expert guidance No single position is safe for eight hours. Workplace-safety bodies such as WorkSafeNB (Canada) use the phrase "the best posture is your next posture", and OSHA advises changing position through small adjustments, stretching, standing and walking. A 2019 viewpoint in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy (Slater and colleagues, expert opinion) argues there is little strong evidence that "sitting up straight" prevents back pain. The practical message: set up a comfortable, supported base position, then move often. Chapter 4 — Sitting time, breaks and standing desks What the Cochrane review found — limited evidence The Cochrane review "Workplace interventions for reducing sitting at work" (Shrestha and colleagues, 2018; searches up to August 2017) is the latest version found for this post. It included 34 studies with 3,397 office workers from high-income countries. - Sit-stand desks reduced workplace sitting by about 84 to 116 minutes per workday in the short term, and about 57 minutes a day at 3-12 months.- The quality of evidence was low to very low for most interventions, mainly due to study design limits and small samples.- The effects of policies, information and counselling were mostly inconsistent.- There was no evidence on longer-term effects, and the review did not show health benefits such as less disease. So: a sit-stand desk probably helps you sit less, at least at first. Whether that improves long-term health is not proven. Break patterns - Cornell's 20-8-2 pattern — rule of thumb. About 20 minutes sitting, 8 minutes standing, 2 minutes moving and stretching. Cornell presents it as a "ball park goal", not an exact prescription.- Cornell's break types — expert guidance: short eye breaks, micro-breaks (under 2 minutes), and a rest break every 30-60 minutes where you stand up and move.- Standing all day is not the goal. Long, still standing has its own discomfort. Alternate. A tiny reminder script (stop with Ctrl+C): rings the terminal bell where the terminal supports it. A timer app or an OS focus feature does the same job. Chapter 5 — Simple stretches — expert guidance These are from the NHS "sitting exercises" guide, adapted for a desk break. Move slowly, stay within a comfortable range, and stop if anything hurts. Chapter 6 — RSI warning signs — expert guidance Repetitive strain injury (RSI) is pain in muscles, nerves and tendons from repeated movement and overuse. Typing is one of the listed causes, along with poor posture at work. The NHS lists symptoms that usually start gradually: - Pain that may feel burning, aching or throbbing- Stiffness and weakness- Tingling, pins and needles, or numbness- Muscle cramps- Swelling The NHS advises seeing a GP (a family doctor) if symptoms do not go away or get worse. Numbness, tingling or weakness deserve a professional look sooner rather than later. > Common pitfall: pushing through wrist or forearm pain to finish a sprint. Early, mild symptoms are the cheap time to change your setup, take breaks and get advice. Do not self-diagnose from the internet. Chapter 7 — Sleep and learning - Sleep helps memory — strong evidence (mainly lab studies). A major review (Rasch and Born, Physiological Reviews, 2013) sums up over a century of research: sleep after learning helps you keep what you learned, and memories are actively consolidated during sleep.- How much — expert guidance. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society (2015 consensus) recommend 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis for adults aged 18 to 60.- Practical rule — rule of thumb. Study or learn a new tool earlier in the day, then sleep, instead of cutting sleep to cram late at night. A tired brain also writes more bugs (see Dev Essentials 10 · Debugging). 3D Printing Course — 07 · Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts
Created2026-09-23|Engineering 3D Printing
The base course closes where it should: with the question of whether the part actually works. Posts 00–03 covered the process, Post 04 the materials, Post 05 whether the part is the right size, and Post 06 how to model it. This post covers whether it survives being used. That requires a working vocabulary of mechanics, an honest treatment of the anisotropy that makes a printed part fundamentally different from a moulded one, a correctly-ordered list of which print settings actually matter for strength, a catalogue mapping observed failures to their causes, and — the section most 3D printing material omits — a clear-eyed account of when the right answer is not to print the part at all. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts (this post). Learning Outcome - Use the mechanics vocabulary correctly: stress, strain, stiffness, strength, toughness, yield, ultimate, ductile, brittle, fatigue, creep.- Explain why creep — not strength — is what quietly destroys loaded PLA parts, and design around it.- Describe printed-part anisotropy correctly, and run a test that measures your Z-strength ratio rather than quoting someone else's number.- Rank the print-profile levers on strength in the correct order, and explain why infill density sits far lower than beginners assume.- Argue the perimeters-versus-infill case from where bending stress actually acts.- Design out stress concentrations, and use fillets as the cheapest strength upgrade available.- Diagnose a broken part from the fracture surface and map symptom to root cause to fix.- Build a repeatable comparative test rig and test the actual part rather than a coupon.- Decide when machining, moulding, sheet metal, or simply buying the part is the better answer. Chapter 1 — The mechanics you actually need Terminology - Stress (σ) — force per unit area, in pascals (N/m²) or MPa. What the material experiences, independent of the part's size.- Strain (ε) — deformation per unit length, dimensionless. How much the material stretched relative to its original length.- Stiffness (elastic modulus, E) — the slope of stress against strain in the elastic region. How much it deflects under load. A stiff material barely moves.- Strength — the stress at which the material fails, whether by yielding or by fracturing. How much load it takes before something goes wrong. Stiffness and strength are independent properties: a material can be stiff and weak (glass), or compliant and strong (many elastomers).- Yield strength — the stress at which deformation stops being elastic and becomes permanent. Past yield the part is bent, even after the load comes off.- Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) — the maximum stress the material carries before fracture.- Toughness — the energy absorbed before fracture; the area under the stress-strain curve. How much abuse it takes before breaking. A tough material bends, absorbs and survives.- Ductile failure — the material yields, stretches, necks and then tears. It gives warning. PETG, ABS, nylon, TPU.- Brittle failure — the material fractures suddenly at or near its elastic limit, with little permanent deformation and no warning. PLA, standard photopolymer resin, and most fibre-filled composites.- Fatigue — failure from repeated cycles of load well below the static strength. A part that survives a 100 N pull once may fail after 10,000 cycles at 30 N.- Creep — slow, continuous deformation under a constant load over time, without any increase in load. It accelerates dramatically as temperature approaches Tg.- Stress concentration — a local multiplication of stress at a geometric discontinuity (a sharp internal corner, a notch, a hole edge, a sudden change of section).- Neutral axis — in a beam under bending, the surface where the stress is zero; material on one side is in tension, on the other in compression. The distinction beginners get wrong most often Stiff ≠ strong ≠ tough. These are three different questions and different materials win each: | Question | Property | The common filament that wins ||---|---|---|| How much does it bend under load? | stiffness | PLA is the stiffest common filament — genuinely || How much load until it breaks? | strength | depends heavily on direction; PLA is competitive in-plane || Does it survive being dropped or shocked? | toughness | PLA is poor; PETG, ABS, PC and nylon are far better | This is why PLA feels great on the bench (stiff, holds shape, precise) and disappoints in service (brittle, notch-sensitive, creeps). It is not a bad material. It is a material with a specific and well-known profile. Creep — the quiet killer Creep deserves its own treatment because it is the failure mode most often misdiagnosed as "the print was weak". Polymers are viscoelastic: under sustained load they behave partly like solids and partly like very stiff liquids. Hold a constant load on a printed bracket and it will slowly, continuously deform. Not fail — deform. Over hours, days, months, depending on the material, the stress level and the temperature. The temperature dependence is the sting. Creep rate rises sharply as the material approaches Tg (Post 04). A PLA part at 20 °C creeps slowly enough to be irrelevant for many uses. The same part at 50 °C — a closed car in summer, a sunlit window sill, a shelf above a radiator, next to a stepper motor — is within striking distance of PLA's Tg of roughly 55–65 °C, and creeps fast enough to be visible in a day. The canonical failure: a PLA phone mount, wall hook, or shelf bracket that was perfectly fine for weeks and one day is simply drooping. Nothing broke. It flowed. Design responses, in order of effectiveness: 1. Don't use PLA for a sustained load in a warm place. PETG is a large improvement, ABS/ASA more, PC more again, and a fibre-filled grade of any of them better still.2. Reduce the stress. Creep rate is strongly stress-dependent; halving the stress helps disproportionately. Thicker sections, more perimeters, a shorter moment arm.3. Put the load into compression rather than bending. Compressive creep in a well-supported column is far less troublesome than bending creep in a cantilever.4. Carry the sustained load in metal. A printed body with a steel screw, rod or bracket taking the actual load is a robust pattern.5. Anneal (Post 04) if the part is PLA and must be warm — increased crystallinity meaningfully raises creep resistance. Then re-check the fit, because the dimensions changed. > Watch out: static strength tests tell you nothing about creep. Hanging a weight on a bracket for thirty seconds and declaring it strong is not a test of a load it will carry for a year. If the part carries a sustained load, the only meaningful test is a sustained one. Chapter 2 — Anisotropy, honestly What is actually going on A moulded part is essentially isotropic: the polymer is a continuous mass and its properties are the same in every direction. A printed part is not. It is a stack of extruded beads bonded to their neighbours, and the bond between beads is not the same as the bulk material. - In-plane (X/Y), along and across the extrusion direction, load is carried mostly by continuous polymer within beads and by side-to-side welds between adjacent beads laid down while both were hot.- Through-thickness (Z), across layers, load is carried entirely by the interlayer weld: the region where a new bead was deposited onto a layer that had already partly cooled, and the polymer chains had a limited time and a limited temperature at which to diffuse across the interface and entangle. That diffusion process is the entire mechanism. The strength of the Z direction is the strength of that weld, and the weld is imperfect: it forms in a fraction of a second, at a temperature falling rapidly toward Tg, between two surfaces that also have geometric voids at the bead junctions. The honest statement of the magnitude: interlayer (Z) strength is substantially lower than in-plane strength for essentially every FDM material and profile. The size of the gap varies enormously — it depends on the polymer, the nozzle temperature, the layer height, the part cooling, the chamber temperature, the layer time, the bead geometry, and whether the part was annealed. Published studies find a wide range of ratios, and any single percentage quoted as a general fact about "3D printing" is not one. You will see figures like "Z is 50 % of XY" repeated widely. Treat them the way Post 05 taught you to treat "0.05 mm accuracy": as a number for someone's specific machine, material and profile, not as a property of the technology. Measure it on yours. Exercise 2 gives the procedure. Reducing the gap | Lever | Effect on interlayer strength | Cost ||---|---|---|| Higher nozzle temperature | strong positive — more time above the diffusion temperature | surface quality, stringing, oozing; bounded by material degradation || Enclosure / warm chamber | strong positive — the previous layer is hotter when the new one lands | equipment; not appropriate for PLA (Post 04) || Less part cooling | positive | overhangs and bridges degrade; a real trade-off || Thicker layers | positive — more bonded area per interface and more retained heat | resolution, surface finish || Wider extrusion / bigger nozzle | positive — larger weld area, fewer voids | resolution, detail || Slower printing | mixed — slower gives more heat per unit length, but a longer layer time means the previous layer is cooler when you return to it | time || Annealing | positive — chains interdiffuse across interfaces given time above Tg | dimensional change (Post 04) || Ironing / higher flow | small positive — reduces interlayer voids | || Orientation | eliminates the problem — put the load in-plane | usually free; occasionally costs support material | > Watch out: every mitigation in that table trades something away — surface quality, overhang capability, resolution, or time — and none of them removes the weakness. Only orientation does. The last row is the point of the whole chapter. Every other lever is a partial mitigation with a cost. Orientation makes the weakness irrelevant, and it usually costs nothing but a decision. That is why it heads the next chapter's list. Chapter 3 — What actually controls strength, in order Beginners tune this list in roughly reverse order. Here it is correctly ordered by leverage. 1. Orientation — by a wide margin the most important Rotating the part on the plate can change its breaking load by a factor. Nothing else in the profile comes close. The rule: never let the primary tensile load path cross a layer boundary. Identify how the part is loaded, identify where the tension is highest, and orient so the layers run along the load, not across it. Concrete cases: | Part | Wrong orientation | Right orientation ||---|---|---|| Hook carrying a downward load | printed upright — the bending tension at the root is across layers | printed flat on its side (or standing in the load plane) so layers run along the hook || Bracket with an L-shape | either leg vertical — the corner is a layer interface in tension | flat, with both legs in the layer plane || Threaded boss taking a pull-out load | axis vertical — the thread strips layer by layer | axis horizontal, or use a heat-set insert (Post 06) || Long thin rod under tension | vertical — every layer is a potential fracture plane | horizontal || A gear | either works for tooth bending; hub axis vertical is standard | axis vertical, so tooth loads are in-plane | Post 03 covers orientation for surface quality and supports. This is the same decision viewed through strength, and when the two conflict, strength usually wins on a functional part. 2. Perimeter (wall) count Second-largest lever, and much larger than infill. Going from 2 to 4 perimeters typically makes a far bigger difference than going from 20 % to 50 % infill, at a fraction of the material and time cost. Why: where bending stress actually acts Most printed parts fail in bending, not pure tension. In a beam under bending: - The material on the outside of the bend is in tension; on the inside, in compression; and somewhere between them, at the neutral axis, the stress is zero.- Stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis. The extreme fibres carry the maximum; material at the neutral axis carries nothing.- Bending stiffness is governed by the second moment of area, which weights material by the square of its distance from the neutral axis. Bending strength is governed by the section modulus, which likewise rewards distance from the axis. That is the entire argument. Perimeters are the material at the extreme fibres — the material that is doing the work. Infill is the material near the neutral axis, where the stress is small. Adding infill adds mass and print time in the place where it contributes least. Infill is not useless; it does three real jobs: 1. Supports the top surfaces so they can be printed at all (Post 02).2. Stabilises the perimeters against buckling — a thin shell under compression fails by buckling long before it fails by crushing, and infill braces it.3. Carries compression and shear in a part loaded through its bulk rather than in bending. But for the common case — a bracket, a hook, a mount, a lever, anything cantilevered — perimeters win decisively. 3. Material Third. Choosing PETG over PLA for a part that gets knocked, or nylon over PETG for a part that wears, changes the outcome more than any slicer setting except the two above. Post 04's decision tree is the tool. Note that the material choice interacts with orientation: fibre-filled composites are dramatically stiffer in-plane and are usually more brittle across layers, so the anisotropy gets worse, not better. 4. Temperature and cooling Fourth. Nozzle temperature is the strongest single lever on interlayer strength within a given material (Chapter 2). Reduced part cooling helps interlayer bonding and hurts overhangs. An enclosure helps materials that want one. These are real effects with real trade-offs — but they are refinements on top of the first three decisions, not substitutes for them. 5. Infill density and pattern Fifth, and well below where beginners put it. - Density. Beyond roughly 40–50 %, additional infill buys little strength for a lot of time and material; the curve flattens. Below about 15 % the part becomes noticeably soft and top surfaces suffer. For most functional parts, 20–30 % infill with 4–6 perimeters outperforms 50 % infill with 2 perimeters, using less material.- Pattern. Matters more than density in some load cases. Gyroid is isotropic, self-supporting, prints fast and is a strong general default. Cubic and triangular are stiff and good under multi-axis load. Grid creates crossing points where the nozzle collides with previously-laid material. Lines/rectilinear is weak in one direction. Concentric is right under flexible parts.- Special case: a fully solid part (100 % infill) is not automatically the strongest thing you can print. Over-extrusion in a solid part introduces internal stress and can leave voids; and the part still fails at layer interfaces. Solid is heavy, slow and often not better than thick perimeters plus moderate infill. 6. Layer height Sixth. Thicker layers bond somewhat better and print faster; thinner layers give finer detail and better surface. Its effect on strength is real but small next to the levers above. > Common pitfall: the reflex "print it at 100 % infill so it's strong". It costs hours and grams, and it usually loses to reorienting the part and adding two perimeters — which costs nothing. Chapter 4 — Stress concentration and design patterns The notch effect A sharp internal corner is a stress concentrator. The local stress at the corner can be several times the nominal stress in the surrounding material, and it rises as the corner radius falls — approaching, in the idealised limit of a perfectly sharp corner, an unbounded value. Real materials do not see infinite stress, but the multiplication is large and real, and it is where cracks start. Printed parts are unusually vulnerable to this for two reasons: 1. PLA and filled composites are notch-sensitive — brittle materials do not locally yield to blunt the peak, so the concentration translates directly into a crack.2. A layer interface at a sharp internal corner combines a stress raiser with the weakest plane in the part. That combination is the single most common origin of a printed-part fracture. Fillets: the cheapest strength upgrade available Adding a fillet to a loaded internal corner costs one CAD feature, a negligible amount of material, and essentially no print time. It reduces the peak stress substantially. There is no other change with that ratio of benefit to cost. Practical guidance: - Fillet every loaded internal corner. A radius comparable to the local wall thickness is a reasonable default; larger is better, up to what the geometry allows.- A larger radius is better than a smaller one. The benefit is strongly nonlinear at small radii — going from sharp to a small fillet buys most of the improvement.- Fillet transitions between sections too. A sudden change from thick to thin is a stress raiser even without a corner. Taper it.- Watch the printing rules (Post 06): fillet vertical and top edges freely; chamfer bottom edges.- Do not fillet a corner that needs to mate flush with something. Chamfer that one, or add a relief groove on the mating part. Chapter 7 — Testing, and when not to print Test the part, not a coupon Standardised tensile coupons (ISO 527, ASTM D638) exist for a reason: they let laboratories compare materials on equal terms. They are the right tool for material comparison and the wrong tool for answering "will my bracket hold". A coupon tells you about a dogbone specimen printed in one orientation. Your part has a specific geometry, specific stress concentrations, a specific orientation, a specific load path, specific infill, and a specific set of fillets and section changes. Those interactions dominate. Test the actual part, loaded the way it will actually be loaded. A repeatable comparative test rig You are not trying to produce absolute engineering data. You are trying to answer "is A better than B on my machine". That requires only repeatability and a controlled comparison — both achievable with cheap equipment. The minimum viable rig: 1. A fixture that holds the part exactly the same way every time. This is the most important part. Print a jig that clamps the part in one repeatable position. Variation in how the part is held will swamp everything else.2. A way to apply load progressively and measure it. Options in ascending order of cost: a bucket you add water or sand to and then weigh; a luggage scale or fish scale pulled by hand; a hanging digital scale with a peak-hold function; a lever arm with hanging weights; an inexpensive load cell with an amplifier logging to a microcontroller.3. A record. Load at first audible crack, load at failure, where it failed, a photograph of the fracture. The disciplines that make the results mean something: - Change exactly one variable at a time. Same spool, same day, same profile except the one thing being tested.- Print all specimens in the same batch, on the same plate where possible, so filament and machine state are shared.- n ≥ 3 per condition. Five is better. Printed parts scatter; a single specimen tells you nothing, exactly as in Post 05.- Report the spread, not just the mean. If A's mean beats B's by less than the within-group spread, you have not shown anything.- Note the temperature. Polymer strength is temperature-dependent, so a test in a cold garage is not comparable to one in a warm room. Anisotropy test procedure Print the same simple beam three times in each of three orientations — flat, on-edge, and upright — with everything else identical. Break each in three-point bending in your fixture. The upright specimens fail across layers; the others do not. The ratio of mean failure loads is your anisotropy figure, for that material and profile. > Common pitfall: comparing A against B when the two batches were printed on different days, from different spools, or with the fixture reassembled in between. Any of those can shift the result by more than the effect you are trying to measure, and the comparison then tells you about the day rather than about the change. FDM is a superb process for a specific envelope: one-off or low-volume parts with complex geometry, needed quickly, at low cost, where moderate strength and moderate accuracy suffice. Chapter 1 — Track spending, then budget You cannot plan what you do not measure. Track every outflow for one full month. Most banks let you export a CSV. Put each line into one of three buckets: - Needs — you would face real trouble if you did not pay: housing, basic food, transport to work, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.- Wants — everything else you spend on.- Savings — money moved to savings or investments, plus debt payments above the minimum. The 50/30/20 split is a rule of thumb from Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book All Your Worth. It says to aim for about 50% of after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings. It is a starting point, not a law. In a high-rent city, needs may be well above 50%. That tells you where the pressure is; it does not mean you failed. To use your own data, replace with and add a column. The 470 "left over" is money with no job. Unplanned money tends to disappear, so give it one (often: more savings). > Watch out: these calculators use for teaching. For a real ledger, store money as integer cents or (see Dev Essentials 08 · Floating Point). Chapter 2 — Pay yourself first "Pay yourself first" means you move money to savings on payday, before you spend, instead of saving whatever is left at month end. Leftovers are usually close to zero. The easiest way is an automatic transfer that runs the day your salary arrives. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) calls saving automatically "one of the easiest ways" to make saving consistent. It turns a daily choice into a one-time setup. > Trick: raise the automatic transfer each time your pay goes up. You never get used to spending the extra. Chapter 3 — The emergency fund An emergency fund is cash for surprises: job loss, a medical bill, a broken laptop. It stops a bad month from turning into high-interest debt. - Size. US regulator FINRA says financial planners often recommend three to six months of living expenses. This is common guidance, not a rule. People with variable income, one income in the household, or a job that is hard to replace often aim higher.- Where. Somewhere safe and easy to reach quickly, kept apart from spending money. It is not an investment; its job is to be there.- Start small. If six months feels impossible, the CFPB suggests thinking about the unexpected costs you have actually had before and what they cost. A first target of one month is still a real safety net. Size the fund on needs, not total spending. In a real emergency, the wants stop. Chapter 4 — High-interest debt: compounding against you Credit card rates are high. The US Federal Reserve's G.19 release (September 2026) puts the average rate on card accounts that were charged interest at 22.15% APR for Q2 2026. Rates differ by country and by card; this number is only for scale. APR is the yearly rate. A card with 22% APR charges roughly $22\% / 12 \approx 1.83\%$ per month on the unpaid balance. On a 5,000 balance, that is about 92 of interest in the first month. If you pay 100, only about 8 reduces the debt. Paying 100 a month takes over 11 years and costs more in interest than the original debt. Paying 300 takes under 2 years. FINRA calls paying down high-rate debt, such as credit card debt, "one of the best ways" to improve your financial base. The reason is simple: clearing a 22% debt is like earning a sure 22% return, which almost no investment can promise. > Common pitfall: paying only the minimum. The minimum is designed to keep the account in good standing, not to clear the debt quickly. Chapter 5 — Compound interest and the rule of 72 With compound interest, you earn interest on past interest. For a principal $P$, yearly rate $r$, compounding $n$ times a year, for $t$ years: $$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$ As $n$ grows without limit, this becomes continuous compounding, $A = Pe^{rt}$. The compounding frequency matters a little. The rate and the time matter a lot. The rule of 72 (an approximation) To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the yearly rate in percent: at 6%, about $72 / 6 = 12$ years. A card balance at 24% with no payments doubles in about 3 years. Chapter 6 — Inflation: real versus nominal Inflation means prices rise, so each unit of money buys less. The nominal return is the number on your statement. The real return is what you gain in buying power: $$1 + r{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi}\qquad\text{so}\qquadr{\text{real}} \approx r{\text{nominal}} - \pi$$ where $\pi$ is the inflation rate. - A 5% return with 3% inflation is a real return of about 1.94%.- With 3% inflation, 1,000 held as cash for 10 years buys what 744 buys today.- Cash earning 1% during 3% inflation still loses buying power: 1,000 becomes 822 in today's money. This is why long-term money is often invested rather than held as cash, and why an emergency fund (short-term money) is kept as cash anyway: for it, being available matters more than growth. Chapter 7 — Stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification Terminology - Stock (share, equity) — a small piece of ownership in a company. Value goes up and down with the company and the market. Historically higher long-run returns, with large swings.- Bond — a loan to a government or company, which pays interest and returns the loan amount at the end. Usually smaller swings than stocks; can still lose value, for example when interest rates rise or the borrower fails.- Fund — a pool of many investors' money that buys many stocks and/or bonds.- Mutual fund / ETF — two common fund structures. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) trades on an exchange during the day like a stock; a traditional mutual fund is bought and sold at one price per day.- Index fund — the US SEC's investor site defines it as a mutual fund, ETF or unit investment trust that follows a passive strategy, aiming to match the return of a market index (such as the S&P 500) before fees.- Expense ratio — a fund's yearly running cost as a percentage of your money in it.- Asset allocation — how you split money between asset types (stocks, bonds, cash).- Diversification — spreading money across many investments so that one failure does not sink you. Why diversification helps A single company can fail. A broad index holds hundreds or thousands of companies, so no single one decides your result. The SEC's beginners' guide explains that the right mix of investments may reduce losses and smooth returns without giving up too much potential gain. Diversification does not remove market risk: when the whole market falls, a diversified portfolio falls too, just less wildly than one bad stock. > Common pitfall: "diversifying" into many funds that hold the same companies. Five large-company funds from the same market may be one bet with five fees. Chapter 8 — Risk versus time horizon Time horizon is how long until you need the money. The SEC's guide says the right asset allocation depends largely on your time horizon and your ability to take risk. A toy model shows why. It assumes each year's return is random with a mean of 6% and a standard deviation of 15%, years independent. These are made-up numbers, not a forecast. Real markets have crashes bigger than this model allows, and bad years can cluster. In this model, the yearly average over a long period swings much less than a single year does, and the chance of ending below where you started falls. That is the logic behind a common guideline: money you need within a few years is kept out of volatile assets, while money for decades can ride out bad years. (Note also that the median is below 6%: big swings lower the typical compound result. This is often called volatility drag.) Chapter 9 — Why fees matter A fee is taken every year, from a balance that keeps growing. The loss compounds, just like returns do. The gap (90,800) is much bigger than the extra fees paid (about 48,000). The rest is the growth those fees would have earned. Returns are uncertain; fees are not. Chapter 10 — Lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging Suppose you have a large sum to invest at once (a bonus, an inheritance). You can invest it all now (lump sum) or feed it in over months (dollar-cost averaging, DCA). One well-known study: Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later" (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos and Wimmer, July 2012). Setup and findings: - Markets: United States (1926–2011), United Kingdom (1976–2011), Australia (1984–2011).- Portfolio: 60% stocks / 40% bonds. In US 12-month windows, lump sum lost money in 22.4% of periods and DCA in 17.6%. Chapter 11 — Behaviour: the costly mistakes The biggest risk is often the investor, not the market. - Chasing performance — buying what went up most recently, often near its peak.- Panic selling — FINRA describes it as selling large amounts quickly out of fear of further falls, without careful analysis. It turns a paper loss into a real one and can miss the recovery.- Overconfidence and over-trading — frequent changes add costs and timing mistakes.- Checking too often — daily prices are mostly noise, and noise makes people act. The damage is measurable. Chapter 1 — Why estimates slip The planning fallacy Kahneman and Tversky described the planning fallacy in 1979 ("Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures"): people predict how long their own tasks will take by imagining how the work will go, and they underweight what usually happens to tasks like it. Their forecasts come out too optimistic. Buehler, Griffin and Ross tested it in 1994 ("Exploring the 'planning fallacy'", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 67(3), 366-381). In Study 1, students predicted when they would finish their honours thesis: | Prediction asked for | Average predicted days | Average actual days | Finished by that prediction ||----------------------|------------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------|| Best guess (as accurate as possible) | 33.9 | 55.5 | 29.7% || Optimistic (everything goes as well as possible) | 27.4 | 55.5 | 10.8% || Pessimistic (everything goes as badly as possible) | 48.6 | 55.5 | 48.7% | (33 students had finished when the records were closed, so the averages are over those 33.) Even the worst-case guess was too short on average. This is the core lesson: your gut estimate is a best case in disguise. Hofstadter's law An aphorism, not a research finding, from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979). It says a task always takes longer than you expect — even when you allow for Hofstadter's law. It is a joke with a true point — knowing about the bias does not remove it. You need a method, not willpower. The cone of uncertainty (a model) Barry Boehm drew a "funnel curve" in Software Engineering Economics (1981); Steve McConnell later popularised it as the cone of uncertainty. The idea: at the initial concept stage, the real effort can be anywhere from about 0.25× to 4× the estimate (a 16× range). The range narrows as decisions are made — requirements, design, code. Treat it as a model, with two caveats: - McConnell himself says the cone shows the best accuracy skilled estimators can get. It is easy to do worse.- The cone does not narrow by itself. Todd Little (IEEE Software, 2006) studied projects at Landmark Graphics and found estimate errors followed a right-skewed (lognormal) distribution, and the uncertainty range stayed nearly the same through the project — against the popular reading of the cone. Uncertainty shrinks only when you actively remove it. What people forget Most slips are not bad maths. They are missing work: - Unknown unknowns — the library that does not support your case; the API that is rate-limited.- Integration — each part works alone; wiring them together takes days.- Review and rework — code review, QA feedback, security review.- Tests, docs, deploy, config, migrations, monitoring.- Everything else — meetings, support, on-call, holidays, sick days, context switching. Chapter 4 — Ranges, outside view, spikes and buffers Give a range with a confidence level A single date sounds precise but carries no information about risk. Say instead: > "Most likely around 10-11 working days. I am 80% confident it is done within 13, assuming the email provider approves our account this week." Reference-class forecasting (the outside view) Your plan is the inside view. The outside view asks: how long did similar work take before? Bent Flyvbjerg built this into reference-class forecasting ("From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right", Project Management Journal, 2006), based on Kahneman and Tversky's work. Three steps: 1. Pick a reference class of past, similar projects — broad enough to have data, narrow enough to be comparable.2. Find the distribution of outcomes in that class (for example, actual ÷ estimate).3. Place your project in that distribution, and adjust your estimate with it. For a developer, the reference class is often your own history: "The last four API integrations took 1.4× to 2× my estimate." Spikes for unknowns A spike comes from Extreme Programming: a small, throw-away experiment to reduce a technical risk or make an estimate more reliable. This is reference-class forecasting on your own data. After 20-30 tasks, the spread of ratios is also a good guide for your range. Chapter 7 — Communicating estimates Keep estimate, target and commitment apart McConnell's Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (2006) separates three things that people often mix up: - an estimate is a prediction ("P80 is 13 days");- a target is a business wish ("we want it before the conference");- a commitment is a promise ("we will ship the core flow by Oct 21"). When a target is shorter than the estimate, do not shrink the estimate. Change the scope, the people, or the date — and say which. A good estimate message 1. The range and its confidence: "P50 Oct 19, P80 Oct 21."2. The assumptions: "Assumes the email provider approves us by Friday; excludes the admin screen."3. The biggest risks: "Email approval (30% chance of +3 days)."4. When you will update it: "I will re-estimate after the spike on Wednesday." Re-estimate when the facts change An estimate is a forecast, not a vow. When scope grows or a risk happens, update the range at once and say why. Bad news early is useful; bad news on the due date is not. Story points vs hours Story points are a team convention, not a law. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not mention story points at all; it only says the Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it. Ron Jeffries, who says he may have invented story points, wrote in 2019 that "if I did, I'm sorry now". He explains they started as "ideal days" and recommends slicing stories small instead. Use points or hours as your team prefers — the ideas in this post (breakdown, ranges, tracking actuals) work the same with both. Gold for a newcomer. Chapter 2 — The three-pass method The source is S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review 37(3), July 2007, pages 83-84. It is two pages long. Keshav has updated it over the years; later versions (for example, one dated February 17, 2016) keep the same method. The key idea: read a paper in up to three passes, not once from start to end. Each pass has its own goal. You can stop after any pass. First pass — the bird's-eye view (about 5-10 minutes) Keshav's steps: 1. Carefully read the title, abstract and introduction.2. Read the section and sub-section headings, and ignore everything else.3. Glance at the math (if any) to see what theory it rests on.4. Read the conclusions.5. Glance over the references and tick off the ones you have already read. At the end, you should be able to answer the five Cs: | C | Question ||---|----------|| Category | What type of paper is this? A measurement paper? An analysis of an existing system? A description of a research prototype? || Context | Which other papers is it related to? What theory does it use? || Correctness | Do the assumptions seem valid? || Contributions | What are the main contributions? || Clarity | Is it well written? | Now you decide. Maybe the paper does not interest you. Maybe you lack the background. Maybe the assumptions look wrong. Stopping here is fine. Keshav says the first pass is enough for papers outside your area that may matter some day. > Trick: the same logic works in reverse when you write. Most readers make only one pass. Clear headings and a tight abstract decide whether anyone reads further. (See Dev Essentials 22 · Design Docs.) Second pass — the content (up to about an hour) Read with more care, but skip details such as proofs. Write key points and questions in the margin as you go. Two special jobs: 1. Look hard at the figures, diagrams and graphs. Are the axes labelled? Are there error bars, so the result is statistically meaningful? Keshav notes that mistakes like these separate rushed work from excellent work.2. Mark relevant references you have not read. They are your reading list for background. After this pass you should be able to summarise the main idea, with evidence, to someone else. Keshav says this pass takes up to an hour for an experienced reader. Sometimes you still will not understand the paper. The topic may be new, the terms unfamiliar, or the writing poor. You then have three choices: (a) put it aside, (b) come back after reading background material, or (c) push on to the third pass. Third pass — virtual re-implementation (hours) The key is to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work in your head (or on paper, or in code). Then compare your version with theirs. The gaps show you both the new ideas and the hidden weak points. - Challenge every assumption in every statement.- Ask how you would present each idea.- Write down ideas for future work. Time: the 2007 version says about four or five hours for beginners and about an hour for an experienced reader. The 2016 version is less optimistic: many hours for beginners and more than an hour or two even for experienced readers. At the end, you should be able to rebuild the paper's structure from memory, and point out implicit assumptions, missing citations and problems with the experiments or analysis. Post 05 established that a printed part's dimensions differ from its nominal dimensions in ways you can measure and partly compensate. This post is about the tool where those decisions get encoded. Choosing a CAD paradigm, understanding what happens when a solid becomes a triangle mesh, picking an interchange format that carries units, and writing a model whose clearances are named parameters rather than typed numbers — these are the things that determine whether a design can be revised in five minutes or has to be rebuilt from scratch. Chapter 1 — The paradigms, and what each is for Terminology - Parametric, history-based modelling — the model is a recorded sequence of operations (a feature tree) driven by named dimensions. Editing a dimension replays the tree. Fusion 360, Onshape, SolidWorks, FreeCAD, Solid Edge.- Direct (explicit) modelling — you push and pull faces of the geometry directly; there is no history to replay. Fast for edits to imported geometry that has no tree. Plasticity, SpaceClaim, Fusion's direct-edit mode, most of FreeCAD's Part workbench.- Code-CAD (script-CAD) — the model is source code that evaluates to geometry. "This hole is centred on that boss" is design intent; "this hole is at X = 17.5" is a coincidence that happens to be true today.- Feature tree — the ordered list of operations that builds a parametric model.- Constraint — a geometric or dimensional relationship in a sketch (coincident, tangent, horizontal, equal, or a driving dimension) that removes a degree of freedom. The reasons are structural, not stylistic: 1. No dimensions. A mesh stores vertex coordinates. It does not store "this face is 20 mm from that face because a bearing is 20 mm wide". Change one thing and you re-derive everything by hand.2. No constraints. Nothing enforces that two faces stay parallel or that a hole stays centred. Move a vertex and the design intent is silently gone.3. No solid guarantee. A mesh can be non-manifold, self-intersecting or open, and Blender will happily let you build and export one. Blender's bevel modifier is a mesh operation; a CAD fillet is an exact tangent surface. Where mesh tools are right: sculpting an organic shape, retopologising a scan, or artistic detailing. Chapter 2 — BREP solids and triangle meshes This is the single most important technical idea in the post, because every accuracy problem in Post 05's Chapter 4 traces back to it. Two ways to describe a shape BREP (boundary representation) describes a solid by its boundary, as a topological structure of faces, edges and vertices, where each face carries an exact analytic surface — a plane, a cylinder, a cone, a sphere, a torus, or a NURBS patch — and each edge an exact curve. A cylindrical hole is stored as "cylindrical surface, axis here, radius 3.0". That radius is exact at every scale. Zoom in a million times and it is still a perfect circle. Triangle mesh describes a surface as a list of vertices and a list of triangles. There are no analytic surfaces. A cylindrical hole is stored as some number of flat facets approximating a circle. If you find yourself repairing an STL, the real fix is upstream. > Watch out: this is why "I downloaded an STL and want to change the hole size" is a hard problem and "I downloaded a STEP and want to change the hole size" is a two-minute job. Chapter 3 — A parametric workflow that survives editing Sketch discipline A sketch is the 2D profile that features are built from, and it is where most parametric models go wrong. - Fully constrain every sketch. A fully-constrained sketch has zero degrees of freedom; every entity is locked by constraints and dimensions. Most CAD packages colour it differently when it is. An under-constrained sketch will silently change shape when you edit something else — the single largest source of "my model exploded when I changed one number".- Prefer geometric constraints over dimensions. Coincident, collinear, tangent, symmetric, equal. These express intent; a dimension expresses a value. A hole constrained symmetric about the origin stays centred when the part gets wider. A hole dimensioned at 17.5 mm from the left edge does not.- Sketch on planes and on datums, not on model faces, where you can. Sketching on a face creates a dependency on that face's identity; if a later edit removes or splits the face, the sketch loses its reference. This is topological naming, and it is why parametric trees break.- Keep sketches small. One sketch per feature is easier to debug than one heroic sketch containing everything.- Sketch the design intent, not the picture. If two features must always be the same size, constrain them equal rather than dimensioning both to the same number. Mesh export settings — where Post 05's chord error is decided Every tessellator is controlled by two tolerances, whatever the UI calls them: - Chord / linear / deviation tolerance — the maximum distance between the flat facet and the true surface. 3D Printing Course — 05 · Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances
Created2026-09-12|Engineering 3D Printing
This is the post the rest of the series is built around. A CAD model contains a number — 20.000 mm — that is exact in the sense that a mathematical object is exact. The part that comes off the plate is not that number. It is a physical object with a size that depends on the filament batch, the flow calibration, the extrusion width, the first-layer squish, the cooling history, the motion system, the geometry itself, and the temperature of the room when you measured it. The gap between those two things is not a defect to be eliminated; it is the normal condition of manufacturing, and the entire discipline of tolerancing exists because of it. The goal of this post is not to hand you numbers. It is to give you the vocabulary to describe the problem precisely, an honest accounting of where the error comes from, and a reproducible procedure that produces numbers for your machine, your material and your profile — because those are the only numbers that are true. > Series map: 00 How 3D Printing Works → 01 First Print and Calibration → 02 Slicing in Depth → 03 Orientation, Supports and Design for Printing → 04 Materials → 05 Dimensional Accuracy and Tolerances (this post) → 06 CAD for Printing → 07 Strength, Failure Modes and Functional Parts. Learning Outcome - State the difference between accuracy, precision (repeatability and reproducibility) and resolution, and explain why an advertised "0.05 mm resolution" says essentially nothing about accuracy.- Measure a printed part defensibly: right instrument, controlled force, controlled temperature, repeated readings, recorded uncertainty.- Build an error budget for a printed dimension and name which contributions are systematic and which are random.- Recognise and correct the standard systematic errors: elephant foot, hole undersizing, external-large/internal-small, and X/Y versus Z asymmetry.- Run a test-artefact procedure that produces a machine-and-material-specific offset table you fill in yourself.- Design fits using clearance/transition/interference as a concept, with a printed sweep gauge determining the actual value.- Choose between compensating in CAD and compensating in the slicer, and know which is correct when.- Design so that tolerance stack-up is absorbed by an adjustable dimension rather than demanded of every dimension. Chapter 1 — Saying what you mean Most confusion about printer accuracy is vocabulary confusion. These terms are not synonyms and the rest of the post keeps them strictly distinct. Terminology - Nominal size — the number in the CAD model. A design intent, not a prediction.- Actual size — what a specific physical feature measures, on a specific instrument, at a specific time and temperature.- Deviation (error) — actual minus nominal, signed. Sign matters: a hole 0.15 mm undersize and a hole 0.15 mm oversize are opposite problems with opposite fixes.- Accuracy (formally, trueness) — how close the mean of many measurements is to the true value. Accuracy is about the centre of the distribution.- Precision — how tightly repeated results cluster, independent of whether they cluster in the right place. Precision is about the spread.- Repeatability — spread when the same operator measures the same feature with the same instrument under the same conditions, or when the same machine prints the same part repeatedly without changes.- Reproducibility — spread when something legitimately changes: a different operator, a different instrument, a different day, a new spool of the same filament, the same model on a different printer.- Resolution — the smallest increment an instrument can display, or the smallest increment a machine can command. It is a property of the readout or the motion system, and it bounds nothing else.- Uncertainty — the interval within which the true value plausibly lies, given the instrument, the method and the operator. Always larger than the resolution.- Tolerance — the permitted deviation, decided by the designer from the function of the part. A tolerance is a requirement, not a measurement.- Systematic error (bias) — a repeatable, signed offset. Can be measured and compensated.- Random error — scatter with no fixed sign. Can be characterised statistically and reduced by averaging, but not compensated away. The four-quadrant picture, stated plainly | | Precise | Not precise ||---|---|---|| Accurate | every part right, every time — the goal | on average right, individually unpredictable || Not accurate | every part wrong by the same amount — compensable | every part wrong by a different amount — the hard case | The bottom-left quadrant is the most important thing in this post. A machine that is precise but not accurate is a good machine: the error is systematic, you measure it once, and you compensate for it. A machine that is imprecise cannot be fixed by compensation, because there is no single number to compensate by. So the first diagnostic question is never "is it accurate?" — it is "is it repeatable?" Why "0.05 mm resolution" is a meaningless accuracy claim Marketing copy quotes numbers like "0.0125 mm XY resolution". That figure is a motion increment: the linear distance corresponding to one microstep of a stepper motor, derived from the motor's step angle, the microstepping setting, and the belt pitch or lead screw pitch. It is arithmetic on the kinematics. It says nothing about: - whether the belt is tensioned identically in both directions (backlash),- whether the frame is square,- whether the extruder delivers the commanded volume of plastic (flow),- whether the extrusion width matches what the slicer assumed,- whether the filament is the diameter the slicer was told,- how much the part shrinks as it cools,- whether the motor actually reaches every commanded microstep under load, which at high microstepping ratios it frequently does not. A stepper motor's ability to command a 0.0125 mm move does not mean the toolhead goes there, and even if it did, the plastic that ends up on the plate is not the toolhead path. Treat advertised resolution as a specification of the controller, not of the part. > Watch out: the same confusion appears on the CAD side. A modeller displaying 20.0000 mm is showing you the precision of a floating-point representation, not a claim about anything physical. Digits in a CAD field are free; micrometres on a plate are not. Chapter 2 — The instrument is part of the experiment Before measuring error, establish that the measurement itself is not the largest source of error. On printed polymer parts, it very often is. Calipers versus micrometers | | Digital calipers | Micrometer ||---|---|---|| Typical display resolution | 0.01 mm | 0.001 mm || Realistic uncertainty in normal use | far worse than the display — commonly a few hundredths of a mm | roughly an order of magnitude better || Measuring force | uncontrolled, entirely operator-dependent | controlled by a ratchet or friction thimble || Reach | external, internal, depth, step | external only (for a standard outside micrometer) || Cost | low | moderate || Right for | almost everything in this post | verifying a critical dimension, or calibrating your calipers' bias | The decisive difference is measuring force. Calipers have no force control at all: how hard you squeeze determines the reading. On steel this barely matters. On a printed polymer part — a material with a modulus orders of magnitude below steel, and a surface made of rounded extrusion beads rather than a plane — it matters enormously. Squeezing calipers hard on a PLA cube can easily change the reading by more than the deviation you are trying to measure, and on TPU it is hopeless. A micrometer's ratchet applies a repeatable force. That is why a micrometer is more precise, not the extra digit. > Trick: to find out how much your grip is worth, measure the same feature ten times deliberately gently and ten times deliberately firmly. The difference between the two means is your personal measuring-force error. Everyone should know this number about themselves. It is usually larger than expected. The other things that move the reading - Zero. Close the jaws, check zero, re-zero, before every measurement session and again if you drop the tool. A caliper that drifted 0.03 mm off zero produces a beautifully repeatable, entirely wrong dataset.- Temperature. Polymers have a coefficient of thermal expansion roughly an order of magnitude larger than steel. A part measured warm off the plate and the same part measured cold are different sizes, and your steel calipers are not expanding with it. Let parts equilibrate to room temperature — a couple of hours for anything substantial — and record the room temperature with the data.- Where on the feature. Printed surfaces are not planes. A measurement across the middle of a face, across a corner, at the first layer, and at the top of a 40 mm pillar are four different measurements of the same nominal dimension. Decide a location convention and record it.- Seam and blob. The layer seam is a local bulge. Measuring across a seam gives a systematically larger reading. Either measure away from the seam and say so, or measure across it deliberately and say that.- Bead geometry. The outer surface is a stack of rounded beads, not a flat face. Calipers contact bead crowns; a flat contact averages differently than a knife-edge contact.- Internal features are harder. Caliper internal jaws are stubby and imprecise, and on a hole they measure a chord, not necessarily the diameter. Pin gauges (or, adequately, drill bit shanks of known size, or ground dowel pins) measure a hole far better: the largest pin that enters is a real, functional, repeatable answer. The measurement protocol The whole of the rest of this post depends on measurements taken like this: 1. Let the part reach room temperature. Record the temperature.2. Zero the instrument. Verify against a known reference if you have one (a gauge block, a ground dowel pin, a new drill bit shank).3. Deburr nothing and clean nothing that changes the dimension; do remove obvious stringing and the brim.4. Take at least five readings of each feature, re-seating the instrument each time. Rotate the part between readings where the feature allows.5. Record every reading, not just the one you like.6. Report the mean and the spread (range or standard deviation). The mean estimates the bias; the spread estimates your precision.7. If the spread is comparable to the deviation you are chasing, stop — improve the measurement before drawing any conclusion about the printer. > Common pitfall: measuring once, getting 19.86 on a 20 mm nominal, and concluding "my printer prints 0.14 mm small". One reading contains no information about its own reliability. It could be bias, it could be grip, it could be a seam, it could be the caliper's zero. Five readings tell you which. Chapter 3 — The error budget Here is the honest thesis of this post: the deviation of a printed dimension is a sum of contributions, most of which you can name, several of which you can measure, and a few of which you cannot control at all. Chapter 4 — The systematic errors, and their actual causes These four account for the large majority of real-world fit failures. Elephant foot Symptom: the bottom one to three layers bulge outward. A part that measures correctly at mid-height is oversize at the base, and it will not sit flat in a recess or slide into a slot. Cause: two things at once. The first layer is deliberately squashed into the plate for adhesion, which displaces material sideways. And the first layers cool against a heated bed that keeps them soft, under the weight and pressure of everything printed above. Fixes, in the order to try them: 1. Get the first-layer Z offset right (Post 01). Over-squish is the root cause; everything else is compensation.2. Chamfer the bottom edge in CAD — a 0.4–0.6 mm chamfer on the bottom perimeter. This is the most robust fix because it survives moving the model to a different printer, a different slicer or a different profile. It costs one CAD feature.3. Slicer elephant-foot compensation — shrinks the first layer's perimeter by a set amount. Effective, but it is a profile setting that does not travel with the model.4. Reduce bed temperature slightly if the material allows. Holes come out undersize Symptom: a 5 mm hole measures under 5 mm, and a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse. This is the single most reported dimensional complaint in FDM, and it has two independent causes that add. Cause A — polygonal chord error. The slicer works on a triangle mesh. A cylindrical hole in CAD becomes an N-sided polygon on export, and the toolpath follows that polygon. Because the mesh vertices sit on the true circle and the polygon edges cut inside it, the printed hole is inscribed in the nominal circle and is therefore smaller. The error grows as the facet count falls and as the hole gets smaller relative to the facet size. This error is created in CAD export, not in the printer — see Post 06 on chord/deviation tolerance. Cause B — extrusion overlap on a concave path. When the nozzle traverses a concave (inward-curving) path, the deposited bead's outer material is compressed toward the inside of the curve. The bead does not simply lie along the commanded centreline; it bulges into the hole. Small holes have tighter curvature, so the effect is larger — which is why a 3 mm hole is proportionally worse than a 12 mm one. This is a real physical effect of extruding a finite-width bead along a curve, and it cannot be fixed in CAD export. Fixes: 1. Export with a tight chord tolerance so the facets are fine (Post 06). This removes Cause A entirely and costs nothing but file size.2. Print, measure, and compensate. Because Cause B depends on hole size, the compensation is a curve, not a constant — hence the sweep in Chapter 5.3. For holes that must fit a fastener or a shaft, drill or ream to size after printing. A printed hole reamed with a standard drill or reamer is dramatically more accurate than any compensation scheme, and this is a legitimate professional answer, not a cop-out.4. Slicer hole compensation where available (Cura's Hole Horizontal Expansion, OrcaSlicer's X-Y hole compensation). Note that PrusaSlicer's XY size compensation is not hole-specific: it grows or shrinks all perimeters, so a positive value that enlarges the outside simultaneously shrinks holes. > Watch out: never design a hole at exactly the nominal shaft size and expect it to fit. A 3 mm hole for a 3 mm shaft will not accept the shaft on any FDM printer without help. Clearance is designed in, not hoped for — Chapter 6. External dimensions large, internal features small Symptom: the outside of the box is 20.15 on a 20 nominal, and the pocket inside it is 19.85 on a 20 nominal. Both errors are the same sign of material excess. Cause: any excess deposited material grows the part outward from the toolpath in both cases. On an external wall that means larger; on an internal wall it means the cavity is smaller. Over-extrusion, an extrusion width larger than the slicer assumed, or a too-hot first layer all produce this signature. This is the diagnostic signature of over-extrusion, and it is why both an external and an internal feature must be in the test artefact. Measuring only external features and compensating them makes internal features worse. Squish the first layer 0.05 mm extra and every Z dimension is 0.05 mm short. That is a systematic, easily measured, easily corrected error — and it is a completely different animal from the size-proportional errors in X and Y. > Trick: measure a tall feature's height and a short feature's height on the same print. If both are short by the same amount, it is first-layer offset. If the tall one is short by proportionally more, it is steps/mm or thermal contraction. The pattern of the error tells you the cause. Chapter 5 — The calibration-cube fallacy, and what to do instead Why the 20 mm cube proves almost nothing The 20 mm calibration cube is the most-printed object in the hobby and one of the least informative. Consider what it can and cannot tell you. What it does test: whether a 20 mm external dimension in X and in Y comes out near 20 mm, once. What it cannot tell you: - Anything about internal features. It has no holes. It therefore cannot detect the over-extrusion signature above, which is the most common real cause of fit failure.- Anything about size dependence. One nominal size cannot separate the offset term from the scale term of the error model. Chapter 6 — Designing fits The concept, borrowed correctly Mechanical engineering has a mature vocabulary for how two mating features relate. ISO 286 ("Geometrical product specifications (GPS) — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes") defines it: a hole and a shaft each get a tolerance zone, described by a letter (position of the zone relative to nominal) and a grade number (width of the zone, IT01 through IT18). The classic designations — H7/g6, H7/p6 — come from that system, and fits fall into three classes: - Clearance fit — the hole is always larger than the shaft. There is always a gap. Parts assemble by hand and can move or rotate.- Transition fit — the tolerance zones overlap. Depending where each part lands within its zone, the assembly may be slightly loose or slightly tight. Used for accurate location with hand assembly.- Interference (press) fit — the shaft is always larger than the hole. Assembly requires force, heating or cooling, and the joint transmits torque by friction. Now the honest part: ISO 286 fit classes are not directly applicable to FDM printing. Borrow the three concepts; do not copy the values. The reasons are specific: 1. The IT grades assume machining processes with characterised, stable capability. FDM's capability is not that, and it is different for every machine, material and profile.2. The system assumes tolerance zones roughly symmetric about a predictable process centre. FDM's bias is strongly feature-dependent — external and internal features err in opposite directions, and small holes err differently from large ones.3. FDM surfaces are made of rounded beads with layer-scale texture. "Size" on such a surface is less well-defined than on a machined one, which undermines the measurement the system is built on.4. The material is compliant and viscoelastic. A polymer press fit relaxes over time (creep — Post 07); a steel one does not. So: use "I want a clearance fit here and an interference fit there" as design language. Then determine the actual clearance experimentally. The fit-test gauge The right tool is a printed gauge that sweeps clearance in known steps against a known reference. Use a real reference object rather than another printed part, so you are measuring one unknown instead of two: a ground dowel pin, a drill bit shank, an M3 screw, a 608 bearing, a specific connector. Record the result as a judgement, not a number, because the judgement is what you actually want to reproduce: | Clearance | Result | Classification ||---|---|---|| 0.00 | | won't enter / needs force / press || 0.05 | | || 0.10 | | || 0.15 | | || 0.20 | | || 0.25 | | || 0.30 | | || 0.40 | | || 0.50 | | free / sloppy | From one gauge you get your machine's four working numbers: press fit, tight locating fit, sliding fit, free-running fit. Write them into your CAD parameter file as named variables (Post 06) and stop guessing. Three things that change the answer and must be held constant or re-tested: - Material. PETG and PLA do not give the same clearances. Neither do two colours (Post 04).- Engagement depth. A 2 mm-deep hole and a 20 mm-deep hole with identical diameters behave completely differently. Test at a realistic depth.- Orientation. A hole printed with its axis vertical (a circle in the layer plane) and one printed horizontally (an arch, with bridging at the top) are different features with different errors. Test the orientation you will use. > Trick: design a deliberately adjustable interface where you can — a slot instead of a hole, a screw-clamped collar instead of a press fit, a shim pocket. One adjustable feature absorbs the whole tolerance stack of the assembly and turns a metrology problem into a five-second assembly step. Compensating in CAD versus in the slicer Both are legitimate. They belong in different places, and mixing them without noticing is how people end up double-compensating. | Compensate in CAD | Compensate in the slicer ||---|---|| The correction is a design decision: a functional clearance, a bottom chamfer, a wall thickness set to a multiple of extrusion width | The correction is a machine/process characteristic: your printer's systematic offset for this material and profile || Travels with the model to any printer, slicer or person | Lives in the profile; correctly re-applied to every model you print with that profile || Correct for: fit clearances, chamfers, thread reliefs, features sized to nozzle geometry | Correct for: global XY size compensation, hole compensation, elephant-foot compensation || Wrong for: your machine's private bias — you would bake a local defect into a shared file | Wrong for: functional clearances — the slicer setting applies to every feature, including the ones that were already fine | The rule: design intent goes in CAD, machine bias goes in the slicer. A clearance of 0.2 mm because a shaft must rotate is design intent — it belongs in the model and should be a named parameter. A −0.06 mm XY compensation because your extruder runs slightly hot is machine bias — it belongs in the profile, and it should not follow the model to someone else's printer. > Watch out: if you compensate in the slicer and then adjust the model because parts still do not fit, you now have two corrections that must be maintained together, and neither is documented. Pick one place per cause, and write down what you did. Chapter 7 — Stack-up, standards, and the argument Tolerance stack-up Errors add along an assembly path. Three parts stacked, each within ±0.15 mm, can be ±0.45 mm out at the end in the worst case. Design a mechanism as a chain of five tightly-toleranced printed features and you have designed something that will not assemble. Two ways to add up the contributions: - Worst case (arithmetic sum). Add all the tolerances. Guarantees assembly, and is conservative — it assumes every part is simultaneously at its extreme, which is unlikely.- Statistical (root-sum-square). If the individual errors are independent and roughly centred, the combined spread grows like the square root of the sum of squares — much smaller than the arithmetic sum. Valid only when the errors really are independent and centred, and FDM errors often are neither: a whole batch printed from one spool on one day shares the same bias, which is precisely the correlated case RSS is invalid for. For hobby and small-batch printing, the honest position is: use worst-case reasoning, and design so that the worst case is absorbed rather than resisted. Techniques that absorb stack-up, roughly in order of usefulness: 1. Make one dimension in the chain adjustable. A slot, an oversized clearance hole with a washer, a screw-adjusted stop, a shim. One adjustable feature is worth ten tight tolerances.2. Shorten the chain. Print two mating features as one part rather than three parts that must align. This is FDM's genuine superiority over machining — arbitrary geometry is free, so consolidate.3. Locate on a datum, not on an accumulation. Reference every feature to one face rather than each to the previous one, so errors do not accumulate down the chain.4. Use compliance deliberately. A printed snap-fit, a flexure, a slightly springy tab tolerates variation that a rigid interface will not.5. Post-process the one critical feature. Ream the bearing hole. Tap the thread. Face the mating surface flat with sandpaper on a granite tile. One machined feature on an otherwise printed part is cheap and effective. Where the standards fit | Standard | What it is | Applicability to FDM ||---|---|---|| ISO/ASTM 52900:2021 | Additive manufacturing — general principles, fundamentals and vocabulary | Directly applicable. The terminology standard for the field; use its vocabulary in anything you write for others || ISO 286-1 / -2:2010 | GPS — ISO code system for tolerances on linear sizes; the H7/g6 system | Concepts applicable, values not. Borrow clearance/transition/interference as language; determine values experimentally || ISO 2768-1 | General tolerances for linear and angular dimensions — the "f / m / c / v" classes on a title block | A useful comparison point, not a specification you can claim. It was written for machined and formed parts; a printed part is not automatically within any of its classes, and claiming a class on a drawing means you have demonstrated capability || ISO 22081:2021 | General geometrical specifications; supersedes ISO 2768-2 (geometrical part), and supplies rules rather than values | Relevant if you produce drawings to a modern GPS framework || ISO 10303 (STEP) AP242 | Product data interchange | Post 06 — the format for exchanging solid geometry with units intact | The reason ISO 2768's "medium" class is worth looking at is calibration of expectations: it gives a sense of what a workshop-grade general tolerance looks like at each size range. Compare your measured spread against it and you learn something honest about where your machine sits. What you must not do is print the class in a title block and imply conformance you have not demonstrated. > Watch out: none of these standards will make your printer more accurate. Their value is in giving you a precise, shared way to say what you require and what you achieved. That is not nothing — most disagreement about printed part quality is people using the same words to mean different things. The closing argument Everything above converges on one position, and it is the same position the planned furniture-CAD series will argue about woodworking: CAD precision does not survive contact with the physical world. The number in the model is exact. The part is not, and the material is not, and the machine is not, and the measurement is not. Every physical process has a distribution of outcomes, and the designer's job is not to eliminate that distribution — that is impossible — but to design something that works across it. Three consequences follow, and they are the practical content of the whole post: 1. A design that only works if a tolerance is met is a fragile design. If the assembly requires a 0.1 mm clearance and the process delivers ±0.15 mm, it will fail, and no amount of care changes that. Redesign the interface — make it adjustable, make it compliant, or consolidate the parts — rather than demanding a capability the process does not have.2. Measure your own process, then design inside what it can do. The offset and scale you fit in Chapter 5, and the clearances you found in Chapter 6, are your process capability. They are the design constraint. Someone else's numbers are not data about your machine.3. Where precision is genuinely required, get it from a process that has it. Ream the hole. Tap the thread. Press in a heat-set insert. Use a bought bearing, a bought shaft, a bought fastener. FDM's strength is arbitrary geometry produced quickly and cheaply, not precision — and combining it with a precise process at the two or three points that need precision is a better engineering answer than fighting the printer for a capability it does not have. The most valuable skill in this entire course is not making an accurate print. It is knowing which dimensions need to be accurate — usually far fewer than beginners assume — and designing so the rest do not have to be.
