Dev Essentials — 24 · Desk Health — Eye Strain, Posture, Movement and Setup
Created2026-09-24|Dev Essentials
Developers spend thousands of hours a year at a screen. Dry, tired eyes, a stiff neck and sore wrists are common, and mostly avoidable. This last post of the series covers eye strain, desk and chair setup, movement and breaks, simple stretches, warning signs, and sleep. Each recommendation carries an evidence label, because some advice rests on solid research and some is just sensible habit. It ends with a checklist and a small script to measure your own setup, then looks back over the whole series. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics → 24 Desk Health (this post). Part E · Growth outside code. > Watch out: this post is educational, not medical advice. See a doctor, physiotherapist or eye-care professional if you have pain that does not go away, numbness or tingling, weakness, or any change in your vision. Do not wait for a checklist to tell you something is wrong. Learning Outcome - Read the evidence labels and know what each one means.- Explain what causes digital eye strain and apply the 20-20-20 rule.- Set screen distance, screen height, lighting and text size to published targets.- Set chair, desk, keyboard and mouse for a neutral posture, including on a laptop.- Explain why movement matters more than one "perfect" posture.- Say what the Cochrane review found about sit-stand desks.- Recognise the warning signs of repetitive strain injury (RSI).- Measure your own setup and check it with a short script. Chapter 1 — Evidence labels | Label | Meaning ||-------|---------|| Strong evidence | Many good studies agree; systematic reviews support it || Moderate evidence | Some good studies or a review, but with limits (short follow-up, mixed results) || Limited evidence | Few, small or low-quality studies; could change with better research || Expert guidance | Recommended by official or professional bodies (OSHA, AOA, AAO, NHS), mostly on ergonomic principles rather than trials || Rule of thumb | A handy simplification; useful, but the exact numbers are not research results || Anecdotal | People report it helps; no good evidence either way | Most desk advice is expert guidance. That is not a weakness; it just means you should adjust it to your body instead of treating the numbers as exact. Chapter 2 — Digital eye strain Terminology - Digital eye strain (also called computer vision syndrome) — tired, dry, sore or blurry eyes and headaches after long screen use.- Accommodation — the eye's focusing effort. Near work keeps the focusing muscles working.- Glare — bright light reflecting off the screen or shining into your eyes. Causes - Less blinking. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) says people normally blink about 15 times a minute, but only about 5 to 7 times a minute while using computers. The eye surface dries.- Long near focus without a break.- Glare and poor lighting.- Screen too close, too high or too far.- Uncorrected vision problems. The American Optometric Association (AOA) lists them as a cause. An eye test can fix what no desk setup can. The AAO states that eye strain does not injure the eye and does not cause permanent damage. It is still worth fixing: it is uncomfortable and makes work harder. The 20-20-20 rule — expert guidance; one small trial Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet (about 6 m) away for at least 20 seconds. Both the AAO and the AOA recommend it. The AOA also suggests resting the eyes for 15 minutes after two hours of continuous computer use (expert guidance). What does research say? A 2023 study in Contact Lens and Anterior Eye (Talens-Estarelles and colleagues) gave 29 computer users break-reminder software for two weeks. Eye strain and dry-eye symptoms improved while they used it, but the gains were gone one week after they stopped. Binocular vision and tear-film measures did not change. The study is small and short, so treat it as limited evidence that the habit helps while you keep it up. Screen distance and height — expert guidance | Source | Distance | Height ||--------|----------|--------|| OSHA computer workstations eTool | 20-40 in (50-100 cm), eye to screen | Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level; centre of the screen 15-20° below horizontal eye level || AOA | 20-28 in (about 51-71 cm) | Centre of the screen 15-20° below eye level (about 4-5 in, 10-13 cm) || AAO | About 25 in (about 64 cm), roughly arm's length | Gaze slightly downward | They agree: about an arm's length away, top edge near eye level, and you look slightly down. OSHA adds that people with bifocals may need the screen lower, so they do not tilt their head back to read through the bottom of the lens. Lighting and glare — expert guidance - Place the monitor side-on to windows (OSHA: perpendicular to the window), not facing them or with them behind it. Use blinds (AOA).- Match screen brightness to the room (AAO). A bright screen in a dark room, or a dim screen in a bright room, both strain.- Tilting the screen back too far can catch overhead lights (OSHA). Tilt it so it faces your line of sight.- A matte screen filter can cut glare (AAO). Text size — rule of thumb If you lean in to read, the text is too small. Increase the zoom or the system scaling instead of moving closer. OSHA notes text may need to be bigger on smaller monitors. Blue light and dry eyes - Blue-light glasses — moderate evidence against. The AAO does not recommend them and says there is no scientific evidence that light from computer screens damages the eyes. A 2023 Cochrane review (17 studies, 619 people) found blue-light filtering lenses may not reduce eye strain from computer use in the short term.- Dry eyes — blink on purpose during breaks, and the AAO suggests artificial tears when eyes feel dry (expert guidance).- Dark mode — anecdotal. Many people find it comfortable, especially at night. There is no strong reason to choose one theme over the other; use what feels better. > Common pitfall: buying gadgets before booking an eye test. An out-of-date prescription, or none at all, is a common cause that no filter can fix. Chapter 3 — Posture and setup OSHA's neutral posture — expert guidance OSHA's eTool describes a neutral working posture: - Hands, wrists and forearms straight, in line, roughly parallel to the floor.- Elbows close to the body, bent between 90 and 120 degrees.- Shoulders relaxed, upper arms hanging normally.- Feet fully supported by the floor, or by a footrest if the desk is not adjustable.- Thighs supported and roughly parallel to the floor; knees about level with the hips.- Back fully supported, with lumbar (lower-back) support, upright or leaning back slightly.- Head level, facing forward, balanced over the torso. Set up in this order 1. Chair first. Set the height so your whole foot rests on the floor and the back of the knee is slightly higher than the seat (OSHA). The seat should support most of your thigh without pressing into the back of the knee. Move the lumbar support to fit the small of your back.2. Keyboard at elbow height. OSHA: elbows about the same height as the keyboard, wrists straight, not bent up, down or sideways. Put the mouse right next to the keyboard to avoid reaching.3. If the desk is too high, raise the chair and add a footrest. If it is too low, raise the desk.4. Screen last, using Chapter 2's targets. Cornell University's ergonomics guide adds that a slightly reclined seated posture of about 100-110 degrees is better than a bolt-upright 90 degrees (expert guidance). Laptops — expert guidance Cornell's guide calls laptop design "inherently" problematic: when the screen is at a good height, the keyboard is not, and the other way round. For regular long sessions: - Raise the laptop on a stand so the top of the screen is near eye level.- Use an external keyboard and mouse at elbow height.- Or use an external monitor and keep the laptop as a second screen. The best posture is the next posture — expert guidance No single position is safe for eight hours. Workplace-safety bodies such as WorkSafeNB (Canada) use the phrase "the best posture is your next posture", and OSHA advises changing position through small adjustments, stretching, standing and walking. A 2019 viewpoint in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy (Slater and colleagues, expert opinion) argues there is little strong evidence that "sitting up straight" prevents back pain. The practical message: set up a comfortable, supported base position, then move often. Chapter 4 — Sitting time, breaks and standing desks What the Cochrane review found — limited evidence The Cochrane review "Workplace interventions for reducing sitting at work" (Shrestha and colleagues, 2018; searches up to August 2017) is the latest version found for this post. It included 34 studies with 3,397 office workers from high-income countries. - Sit-stand desks reduced workplace sitting by about 84 to 116 minutes per workday in the short term, and about 57 minutes a day at 3-12 months.- The quality of evidence was low to very low for most interventions, mainly due to study design limits and small samples.- The effects of policies, information and counselling were mostly inconsistent.- There was no evidence on longer-term effects, and the review did not show health benefits such as less disease. So: a sit-stand desk probably helps you sit less, at least at first. Whether that improves long-term health is not proven. Break patterns - Cornell's 20-8-2 pattern — rule of thumb. About 20 minutes sitting, 8 minutes standing, 2 minutes moving and stretching. Cornell presents it as a "ball park goal", not an exact prescription.- Cornell's break types — expert guidance: short eye breaks, micro-breaks (under 2 minutes), and a rest break every 30-60 minutes where you stand up and move.- Standing all day is not the goal. Long, still standing has its own discomfort. Alternate. A tiny reminder script (stop with Ctrl+C): rings the terminal bell where the terminal supports it. A timer app or an OS focus feature does the same job. Chapter 5 — Simple stretches — expert guidance These are from the NHS "sitting exercises" guide, adapted for a desk break. Move slowly, stay within a comfortable range, and stop if anything hurts. The NHS advises a stable chair without wheels for these, so push your office chair against a wall or use another chair. | Stretch | How | Dose (NHS) ||---------|-----|-----------|| Chest stretch | Sit tall, away from the backrest. Chapter 6 — RSI warning signs — expert guidance Repetitive strain injury (RSI) is pain in muscles, nerves and tendons from repeated movement and overuse. Typing is one of the listed causes, along with poor posture at work. The NHS lists symptoms that usually start gradually: - Pain that may feel burning, aching or throbbing- Stiffness and weakness- Tingling, pins and needles, or numbness- Muscle cramps- Swelling The NHS advises seeing a GP (a family doctor) if symptoms do not go away or get worse. Numbness, tingling or weakness deserve a professional look sooner rather than later. > Common pitfall: pushing through wrist or forearm pain to finish a sprint. Early, mild symptoms are the cheap time to change your setup, take breaks and get advice. Do not self-diagnose from the internet. Chapter 7 — Sleep and learning - Sleep helps memory — strong evidence (mainly lab studies). A major review (Rasch and Born, Physiological Reviews, 2013) sums up over a century of research: sleep after learning helps you keep what you learned, and memories are actively consolidated during sleep.- How much — expert guidance. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society (2015 consensus) recommend 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis for adults aged 18 to 60.- Practical rule — rule of thumb. Study or learn a new tool earlier in the day, then sleep, instead of cutting sleep to cram late at night. A tired brain also writes more bugs (see Dev Essentials 10 · Debugging). Dev Essentials — 23 · Money Basics for Engineers — Budget, Emergency Fund, Compound Interest and Index Funds
Created2026-09-22|Dev Essentials
Engineers learn exponential growth for algorithms, then often ignore it for their own money. Compound interest works for you in savings and against you in debt. Small fees grow into large sums over decades. This post teaches the basic ideas with formulas and small Python calculators you can run on your own numbers: budgeting, an emergency fund, debt, compound interest, inflation, index funds, fees, and the mistakes people make under stress. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 21 Estimating → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics (this post) → 24 Desk Health. Part E · Growth outside code. > Watch out: this post is general education only, not personal financial advice. It does not recommend any product, fund, provider or asset. Account types, tax rules and pension schemes differ by country, so check your own country's regulator or government money-guidance service. For decisions about your own situation, talk to a qualified, regulated adviser. Learning Outcome - Track spending and test it against the 50/30/20 rule of thumb.- Explain "pay yourself first" and size an emergency fund.- Show with code how card interest makes a small debt last for years.- Use $A = P(1 + r/n)^{nt}$ and know how wrong the rule of 72 is.- Turn a nominal return into a real (after-inflation) return.- Explain stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification in plain words.- Measure how a 1.0% fee compares with a 0.1% fee over 30 years.- Describe what one Vanguard study found about lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging.- Name the behaviour mistakes that cost investors the most. Chapter 1 — Track spending, then budget You cannot plan what you do not measure. Track every outflow for one full month. Most banks let you export a CSV. Put each line into one of three buckets: - Needs — you would face real trouble if you did not pay: housing, basic food, transport to work, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.- Wants — everything else you spend on.- Savings — money moved to savings or investments, plus debt payments above the minimum. The 50/30/20 split is a rule of thumb from Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book All Your Worth. It says to aim for about 50% of after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings. It is a starting point, not a law. In a high-rent city, needs may be well above 50%. That tells you where the pressure is; it does not mean you failed. To use your own data, replace with and add a column. The 470 "left over" is money with no job. Unplanned money tends to disappear, so give it one (often: more savings). > Watch out: these calculators use for teaching. For a real ledger, store money as integer cents or (see Dev Essentials 08 · Floating Point). Chapter 2 — Pay yourself first "Pay yourself first" means you move money to savings on payday, before you spend, instead of saving whatever is left at month end. Leftovers are usually close to zero. The easiest way is an automatic transfer that runs the day your salary arrives. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) calls saving automatically "one of the easiest ways" to make saving consistent. It turns a daily choice into a one-time setup. > Trick: raise the automatic transfer each time your pay goes up. You never get used to spending the extra. Chapter 3 — The emergency fund An emergency fund is cash for surprises: job loss, a medical bill, a broken laptop. It stops a bad month from turning into high-interest debt. - Size. US regulator FINRA says financial planners often recommend three to six months of living expenses. This is common guidance, not a rule. People with variable income, one income in the household, or a job that is hard to replace often aim higher.- Where. Somewhere safe and easy to reach quickly, kept apart from spending money. It is not an investment; its job is to be there.- Start small. If six months feels impossible, the CFPB suggests thinking about the unexpected costs you have actually had before and what they cost. A first target of one month is still a real safety net. Size the fund on needs, not total spending. In a real emergency, the wants stop. Chapter 4 — High-interest debt: compounding against you Credit card rates are high. The US Federal Reserve's G.19 release (September 2026) puts the average rate on card accounts that were charged interest at 22.15% APR for Q2 2026. Rates differ by country and by card; this number is only for scale. APR is the yearly rate. A card with 22% APR charges roughly $22\% / 12 \approx 1.83\%$ per month on the unpaid balance. On a 5,000 balance, that is about 92 of interest in the first month. If you pay 100, only about 8 reduces the debt. Paying 100 a month takes over 11 years and costs more in interest than the original debt. Paying 300 takes under 2 years. FINRA calls paying down high-rate debt, such as credit card debt, "one of the best ways" to improve your financial base. The reason is simple: clearing a 22% debt is like earning a sure 22% return, which almost no investment can promise. > Common pitfall: paying only the minimum. The minimum is designed to keep the account in good standing, not to clear the debt quickly. Chapter 5 — Compound interest and the rule of 72 With compound interest, you earn interest on past interest. For a principal $P$, yearly rate $r$, compounding $n$ times a year, for $t$ years: $$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$ As $n$ grows without limit, this becomes continuous compounding, $A = Pe^{rt}$. The compounding frequency matters a little. The rate and the time matter a lot. The rule of 72 (an approximation) To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the yearly rate in percent: at 6%, about $72 / 6 = 12$ years. The exact answer for yearly compounding is $$t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1 + r)}$$ | Rate | Rule of 72 (years) | Exact (years) | Error ||------|------|------|------|| 1% | 72.00 | 69.66 | +2.34 || 2% | 36.00 | 35.00 | +1.00 || 4% | 18.00 | 17.67 | +0.33 || 6% | 12.00 | 11.90 | +0.10 || 8% | 9.00 | 9.01 | −0.01 || 10% | 7.20 | 7.27 | −0.07 || 12% | 6.00 | 6.12 | −0.12 || 18% | 4.00 | 4.19 | −0.19 || 24% | 3.00 | 3.22 | −0.22 | It is almost exact near 8%. At the edges of the table it is off by about 3% (at 1%) to 7% (at 24%) of the true answer. Why 72? For small $r$, $\ln(1+r) \approx r$, which gives $69.3 / \text{rate}$. Since $\ln(1+r)$ is a bit less than $r$, a slightly bigger number fits better for common rates, and 72 divides easily by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12. > Trick: the rule works for debt too. A card balance at 24% with no payments doubles in about 3 years. Chapter 6 — Inflation: real versus nominal Inflation means prices rise, so each unit of money buys less. The nominal return is the number on your statement. The real return is what you gain in buying power: $$1 + r{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi}\qquad\text{so}\qquadr{\text{real}} \approx r{\text{nominal}} - \pi$$ where $\pi$ is the inflation rate. - A 5% return with 3% inflation is a real return of about 1.94%.- With 3% inflation, 1,000 held as cash for 10 years buys what 744 buys today.- Cash earning 1% during 3% inflation still loses buying power: 1,000 becomes 822 in today's money. This is why long-term money is often invested rather than held as cash, and why an emergency fund (short-term money) is kept as cash anyway: for it, being available matters more than growth. Chapter 7 — Stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification Terminology - Stock (share, equity) — a small piece of ownership in a company. Value goes up and down with the company and the market. Historically higher long-run returns, with large swings.- Bond — a loan to a government or company, which pays interest and returns the loan amount at the end. Usually smaller swings than stocks; can still lose value, for example when interest rates rise or the borrower fails.- Fund — a pool of many investors' money that buys many stocks and/or bonds.- Mutual fund / ETF — two common fund structures. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) trades on an exchange during the day like a stock; a traditional mutual fund is bought and sold at one price per day.- Index fund — the US SEC's investor site defines it as a mutual fund, ETF or unit investment trust that follows a passive strategy, aiming to match the return of a market index (such as the S&P 500) before fees.- Expense ratio — a fund's yearly running cost as a percentage of your money in it.- Asset allocation — how you split money between asset types (stocks, bonds, cash).- Diversification — spreading money across many investments so that one failure does not sink you. Why diversification helps A single company can fail. A broad index holds hundreds or thousands of companies, so no single one decides your result. The SEC's beginners' guide explains that the right mix of investments may reduce losses and smooth returns without giving up too much potential gain. Diversification does not remove market risk: when the whole market falls, a diversified portfolio falls too, just less wildly than one bad stock. > Common pitfall: "diversifying" into many funds that hold the same companies. Five large-company funds from the same market may be one bet with five fees. Chapter 8 — Risk versus time horizon Time horizon is how long until you need the money. The SEC's guide says the right asset allocation depends largely on your time horizon and your ability to take risk. A toy model shows why. It assumes each year's return is random with a mean of 6% and a standard deviation of 15%, years independent. These are made-up numbers, not a forecast. Real markets have crashes bigger than this model allows, and bad years can cluster. In this model, the yearly average over a long period swings much less than a single year does, and the chance of ending below where you started falls. That is the logic behind a common guideline: money you need within a few years is kept out of volatile assets, while money for decades can ride out bad years. (Note also that the median is below 6%: big swings lower the typical compound result. This is often called volatility drag.) Chapter 9 — Why fees matter A fee is taken every year, from a balance that keeps growing. The loss compounds, just like returns do. The US SEC's investor bulletin on fees shows 100,000 invested for 20 years at 4% a year: with a 1% yearly fee, the result is nearly 30,000 lower than with a 0.25% fee. Now a longer case. Assumptions (made up for illustration): start with 10,000, add 500 every month for 30 years, earn 6.0% a year before fees every year, fee charged monthly as a share of the balance, no taxes or inflation. The gap (90,800) is much bigger than the extra fees paid (about 48,000). The rest is the growth those fees would have earned. The SEC check reproduces the bulletin: 0.50% costs about 10,000 and 1.00% costs about 28,000 compared with 0.25%. > Trick: when comparing funds that track the same index, the expense ratio is one of the few things you know for certain in advance. Returns are uncertain; fees are not. Chapter 10 — Lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging Suppose you have a large sum to invest at once (a bonus, an inheritance). You can invest it all now (lump sum) or feed it in over months (dollar-cost averaging, DCA). One well-known study: Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later" (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos and Wimmer, July 2012). Setup and findings: - Markets: United States (1926–2011), United Kingdom (1976–2011), Australia (1984–2011).- Portfolio: 60% stocks / 40% bonds. DCA: equal monthly amounts over 12 months, the rest held in cash. Results compared over rolling 10-year periods.- Lump sum ended ahead in about two-thirds of periods: 67% (US), 67% (UK), 66% (Australia).- On average the lump-sum portfolio ended 2.3% higher in the US (2.2% UK, 1.3% Australia).- Longer DCA made it worse: in the US, lump sum beat 36-month DCA in about 90% of periods.- DCA did better in falling markets. In US 12-month windows, lump sum lost money in 22.4% of periods and DCA in 17.6%. The authors' reading: lump sum wins more often because stocks and bonds usually beat cash; DCA can make sense for someone mainly worried about short-term losses and regret. Note this is one study of past data; it does not predict any future period. Vanguard published a follow-up on the same question in February 2023. > Watch out: this study is about a sum you already have. Investing part of each paycheck as it arrives is also called DCA, but it is a different situation: the money did not exist earlier. The same paper calls that a prudent way to invest. Chapter 11 — Behaviour: the costly mistakes The biggest risk is often the investor, not the market. - Chasing performance — buying what went up most recently, often near its peak.- Panic selling — FINRA describes it as selling large amounts quickly out of fear of further falls, without careful analysis. It turns a paper loss into a real one and can miss the recovery.- Overconfidence and over-trading — frequent changes add costs and timing mistakes.- Checking too often — daily prices are mostly noise, and noise makes people act. The damage is measurable. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" 2025 study (one annual industry study) estimated that over the 10 years to December 2024, the average dollar in US mutual funds and ETFs earned about 7.0% a year, while the funds themselves returned about 8.2%. The 1.2-point gap, about 15% of the total return, came mostly from the timing of investors' buys and sells. Defences that work because they remove in-the-moment decisions: 1. Chapter 1 — Why estimates slip The planning fallacy Kahneman and Tversky described the planning fallacy in 1979 ("Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures"): people predict how long their own tasks will take by imagining how the work will go, and they underweight what usually happens to tasks like it. Their forecasts come out too optimistic. Buehler, Griffin and Ross tested it in 1994 ("Exploring the 'planning fallacy'", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 67(3), 366-381). In Study 1, students predicted when they would finish their honours thesis: | Prediction asked for | Average predicted days | Average actual days | Finished by that prediction ||----------------------|------------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------|| Best guess (as accurate as possible) | 33.9 | 55.5 | 29.7% || Optimistic (everything goes as well as possible) | 27.4 | 55.5 | 10.8% || Pessimistic (everything goes as badly as possible) | 48.6 | 55.5 | 48.7% | (33 students had finished when the records were closed, so the averages are over those 33.) Even the worst-case guess was too short on average. This is the core lesson: your gut estimate is a best case in disguise. Hofstadter's law An aphorism, not a research finding, from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979). It says a task always takes longer than you expect — even when you allow for Hofstadter's law. It is a joke with a true point — knowing about the bias does not remove it. You need a method, not willpower. The cone of uncertainty (a model) Barry Boehm drew a "funnel curve" in Software Engineering Economics (1981); Steve McConnell later popularised it as the cone of uncertainty. The idea: at the initial concept stage, the real effort can be anywhere from about 0.25× to 4× the estimate (a 16× range). The range narrows as decisions are made — requirements, design, code. Treat it as a model, with two caveats: - McConnell himself says the cone shows the best accuracy skilled estimators can get. It is easy to do worse.- The cone does not narrow by itself. Todd Little (IEEE Software, 2006) studied projects at Landmark Graphics and found estimate errors followed a right-skewed (lognormal) distribution, and the uncertainty range stayed nearly the same through the project — against the popular reading of the cone. Uncertainty shrinks only when you actively remove it. What people forget Most slips are not bad maths. They are missing work: - Unknown unknowns — the library that does not support your case; the API that is rate-limited.- Integration — each part works alone; wiring them together takes days.- Review and rework — code review, QA feedback, security review.- Tests, docs, deploy, config, migrations, monitoring.- Everything else — meetings, support, on-call, holidays, sick days, context switching. Chapter 4 — Ranges, outside view, spikes and buffers Give a range with a confidence level A single date sounds precise but carries no information about risk. Say instead: > "Most likely around 10-11 working days. I am 80% confident it is done within 13, assuming the email provider approves our account this week." Use the same words every time — P50, P80, P95 — so people learn what they mean. A P80 date is missed one time in five; say so up front. Reference-class forecasting (the outside view) Your plan is the inside view. The outside view asks: how long did similar work take before? Bent Flyvbjerg built this into reference-class forecasting ("From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right", Project Management Journal, 2006), based on Kahneman and Tversky's work. Three steps: 1. Pick a reference class of past, similar projects — broad enough to have data, narrow enough to be comparable.2. Find the distribution of outcomes in that class (for example, actual ÷ estimate).3. Place your project in that distribution, and adjust your estimate with it. For a developer, the reference class is often your own history: "The last four API integrations took 1.4× to 2× my estimate." Spikes for unknowns A spike comes from Extreme Programming: a small, throw-away experiment to reduce a technical risk or make an estimate more reliable. Time-box it ("half a day to prove the PDF library can do Chinese fonts"), then estimate with the answer in hand. Explicit buffers Do not pad every task in secret. Padding gets spent (work expands to fill the time) and nobody can see the risk. Instead, estimate tasks honestly and add one visible buffer line at the project level, sized from the P50-P80 gap or from named risks. Report how much buffer is left as the project goes on. Gold for a newcomer. Chapter 2 — The three-pass method The source is S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review 37(3), July 2007, pages 83-84. It is two pages long. Keshav has updated it over the years; later versions (for example, one dated February 17, 2016) keep the same method. The key idea: read a paper in up to three passes, not once from start to end. Each pass has its own goal. You can stop after any pass. First pass — the bird's-eye view (about 5-10 minutes) Keshav's steps: 1. Carefully read the title, abstract and introduction.2. Read the section and sub-section headings, and ignore everything else.3. Glance at the math (if any) to see what theory it rests on.4. Read the conclusions.5. Glance over the references and tick off the ones you have already read. At the end, you should be able to answer the five Cs: | C | Question ||---|----------|| Category | What type of paper is this? A measurement paper? An analysis of an existing system? A description of a research prototype? || Context | Which other papers is it related to? What theory does it use? || Correctness | Do the assumptions seem valid? || Contributions | What are the main contributions? || Clarity | Is it well written? | Now you decide. Maybe the paper does not interest you. Maybe you lack the background. Maybe the assumptions look wrong. Stopping here is fine. Keshav says the first pass is enough for papers outside your area that may matter some day. > Trick: the same logic works in reverse when you write. Most readers make only one pass. Clear headings and a tight abstract decide whether anyone reads further. (See Dev Essentials 22 · Design Docs.) Second pass — the content (up to about an hour) Read with more care, but skip details such as proofs. Write key points and questions in the margin as you go. Two special jobs: 1. Look hard at the figures, diagrams and graphs. Are the axes labelled? Are there error bars, so the result is statistically meaningful? Keshav notes that mistakes like these separate rushed work from excellent work.2. Mark relevant references you have not read. They are your reading list for background. After this pass you should be able to summarise the main idea, with evidence, to someone else. Keshav says this pass takes up to an hour for an experienced reader. Sometimes you still will not understand the paper. The topic may be new, the terms unfamiliar, or the writing poor. You then have three choices: (a) put it aside, (b) come back after reading background material, or (c) push on to the third pass. Third pass — virtual re-implementation (hours) The key is to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work in your head (or on paper, or in code). Then compare your version with theirs. The gaps show you both the new ideas and the hidden weak points. - Challenge every assumption in every statement.- Ask how you would present each idea.- Write down ideas for future work. Time: the 2007 version says about four or five hours for beginners and about an hour for an experienced reader. The 2016 version is less optimistic: many hours for beginners and more than an hour or two even for experienced readers. At the end, you should be able to rebuild the paper's structure from memory, and point out implicit assumptions, missing citations and problems with the experiments or analysis. Attention, at intuition level Almost every modern LLM is a transformer, from "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017). Its key part is self-attention: for each token, the model decides how much each earlier token matters right now. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired", attention lets "it" pull in information from "animal". Each token makes a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?): $$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{dk}}\right)V$$ For text generation, a causal mask stops each token from looking at later tokens. Real models stack many such layers, each with several attention "heads". What this means for you: - Output is written one token at a time, which is why APIs offer streaming. On Anthropic's current price list, output tokens cost five times as much as input tokens.- The model cannot go back. An early wrong token shapes everything after it. Reasoning before answering helps (Chapter 8).- Long inputs cost more compute. The paper lists self-attention cost per layer as $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ for sequence length $n$.
