Piano Notes — 04 · Grade 4 Theory & Repertoire
Created2026-05-19|Music
Grade 4 is the bridge between "can read music" and "understands music". The syllabus expands keys to ±4 sharps/flats, finalises melodic minor, introduces all interval qualities including augmented and diminished, formalises cadences, and asks you to write primary triads in any key from the syllabus. Repertoire jumps too — full Bach inventions, longer sonatina movements, intermediate Romantic miniatures. > Series map: 00 → 01 → 02 → 03 → 04 Grade 4 (this post). Grades 5–8 to follow. Learning Outcome - All simple and compound time signatures up to 12/8, with duplets and triplets in compound time.- All major and minor keys up to ±4 sharps/flats.- Full melodic minor (ascending raised 6 + 7, descending revert).- Identify and write all interval qualities including augmented and diminished (not only the tritone).- Identify and write primary triads (I, IV, V/i, iv, V) in root position in any key from the syllabus.- Write a perfect, plagal, imperfect, or interrupted cadence in any of those keys.- Transpose a short passage up or down an octave, possibly with a clef change.- Recognise broader Italian, French, and German musical terms.- Play 3–5 Grade 4 pieces hands together at fair tempo. Chapter 1 — Keys Up to ±4 Sharps/Flats Adding E major (4 sharps) and A♭ major (4 flats) to the Grade 3 list. | Key | Sharps/Flats | Relative minor ||-----|--------------|----------------|| C | — | A m || G | F♯ | E m || D | F♯ C♯ | B m || A | F♯ C♯ G♯ | F♯ m || E | F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ | C♯ m || F | B♭ | D m || B♭ | B♭ E♭ | G m || E♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ | C m || A♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ | F m | That's 9 major keys + 9 minor keys = 18 to know fluently by Grade 4. > Trick: the circle of fifths is your best friend. Memorise the order clockwise from C: G D A E (each adds a sharp), counter-clockwise: F B♭ E♭ A♭ (each adds a flat). The relative minor lives 3 semitones below — so the same circle holds the minors too, offset by a third. A small mental tool: "key family" Group the Grade 4 keys into three families by sharps/flats. When you can derive any scale within a family fluently, the family acts as a single unit: - Sharp family: G, D, A, E.- Flat family: F, B♭, E♭, A♭.- Neutral: C. Each family has the same major-key triad pattern (M m m M M m d°). Practise one family per week; rotate; by month four you own all 9 keys. Chapter 2 — Melodic Minor Finalised Reminder from Grade 3: three minor flavours sharing the same key signature. Melodic minor is the one Grade 4 wants from you in writing and playing. Ascending raises the 6 and 7. Descending reverts to natural (lowers the 6 and 7 back). It's the only scale in Western theory that's deliberately asymmetric between up and down. > Where I got stuck: I kept playing the ascending shape on the way down too. Fix: in slow practice, name the sharps aloud as you ascend ("F-sharp, G-sharp"), and name the naturals aloud as you descend ("G-natural, F-natural"). The voice catches the descent change before the fingers do. Writing a melodic minor in any key 1. Write the natural minor (the key signature's normal scale).2. Mark the 6th and 7th degrees.3. Ascending: raise both with an accidental (♯ or ♮ depending on whether the natural is flat).4. Descending: revert — write naturals/flats explicitly to cancel the ascending raises. They sound identical (both 3 semitones), and on the piano they're the same key. But the staff number is different (C–D = 2nd, C–E♭ = 3rd). Always read the letter names first, count positions, then count semitones. Two questions, two answers, two checks. Chapter 4 — Primary Triads, Reinforced Now in all 9 major + 9 minor keys, written out: Major-key triads pattern (always the same) | Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° ||--------|---|----|-----|----|----|----|------|| Quality | M | m | m | M | M | m | dim | Minor-key triads (harmonic minor) | Degree | i | ii° | III+ | iv | V | VI | vii° ||--------|---|-----|------|----|----|----|------|| Quality | m | dim | Aug | m | M | M | dim | (III+ = augmented; only applies in harmonic minor because the raised 7 alters that triad.) > Trick: memorise the major pattern first (M m m M M m d°). Then commit just this fact about minor: the V in harmonic minor is major (because of the raised 7th). For Grade 4 written work, primary triads (i, iv, V) are what you'll be asked. i and iv are minor (because they include the natural 3rd and 5th). V is major. Worked example: write i, iv, V in F♯ minor F♯ minor's relative major is A (3 sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯). Harmonic minor raises the 7th of natural F♯ minor → raises E to E♯. Notice E♯ (not F natural). The spelling matters — F♯ minor uses the letters F-G-A-B-C-D-E in order, so the raised 7 is E♯ (not F natural), even though they're the same key on the piano. Chapter 5 — Cadences A cadence (終止式) is a two-chord progression that ends a musical phrase. The four Grade-4 cadences: | Cadence | Roman | Sound | Where ||---------|-------|-------|-------|| Perfect (Authentic) | V → I | Full closure | End of major sections || Plagal | IV → I | "Amen" / gentler close | End of hymns, gospel || Imperfect (Half) | ? → V | Pause; more to come | Mid-phrase question || Interrupted (Deceptive) | V → vi | Surprise — expected V→I doesn't arrive | Romantic music loves this | Writing a cadence For Grade 4, you'll typically be asked to write two block-chord triads in root position in a given key. Voicings are open here — the syllabus doesn't yet require correct four-part voice leading. Example — plagal cadence in G major: > Trick: perfect cadences are the period at the end of a sentence. Plagal cadences are the "and we're done" exhale. Imperfect is the question mark mid-phrase. Interrupted is the comma-where-you-expected-a-period — the listener feels the rug pulled. Naming the emotional function makes them memorable. Detection by ear - Perfect ends on the tonic with a strong "thud" of finality.- Plagal ends on tonic but softer, "Amen"-shaped.- Imperfect doesn't end on tonic — it leaves you hanging on V.- Interrupted sets you up for tonic, then jolts to vi. > Where I got stuck: I confused plagal and perfect by ear because both end on the tonic. The fix: listen to the chord before the tonic. If it's a bright, leading chord with a sharpened 7th → V → perfect. If it's a softer subdominant (IV) → plagal. The lead-in chord is the give-away. Chapter 6 — Transposition at the Octave (with clef change) Grade 4 asks: rewrite a short passage one octave higher or lower, possibly changing clef. Procedure 1. Identify the target clef (if changing). Treble ↔ bass change requires repositioning every note.2. Shift every note by 7 staff positions (one octave) in the chosen direction.3. Key signature unchanged — transposing at the octave doesn't change key.4. Preserve all accidentals, dynamics, articulation, fingering. Common confusion: clef change ≠ key change Moving a treble passage into the bass clef changes how the same notes look, not what they sound like. Middle C is one ledger below the treble staff; the same note is one ledger above the bass staff. The actual pitch is identical; the notation rearranges. > Where I got stuck: I miscounted octaves on bass clef constantly. The fix that stuck: sketch the grand staff at the top of every transposition exercise during practice, with Middle C clearly marked on both sides. After two weeks the eye snaps to the right ledger without thinking. Worked mini-example Original (treble, C major): Transposed one octave down, in bass clef: Same letters, same key signature, just one octave lower in pitch and on the other clef. Chapter 9 — Voicing (the new technique) By Grade 4 you're playing pieces where one voice (usually the soprano line, top of the RH) carries the melody while the other voices accompany. You need to make the melody louder than the inner voices, with the same hand. Mechanics - The melody-bearing finger uses slightly more weight and arm support.- The accompanying fingers play softer — about 1 dynamic level below.- Wrist relaxes between melodic strikes; the inner voices ride along. Practice drill 1. Play the chord with full RH voicing at one steady volume.2. Replay, this time bringing out the top note (finger 5, usually) — exaggerate.3. Replay, this time bringing out an inner voice (finger 2 or 3).4. Replay, this time bringing out the bottom note (finger 1).5. Now play the actual piece with the top voice highlighted naturally. The first four steps are "isolated voicing"; the fifth is "musical voicing". > Where I got stuck: my finger 5 was too weak to project. The fix: finger 5 isolation exercises — Hanon-style 5-finger patterns where I deliberately over-strike with the pinky. Two weeks of this gave finger 5 the strength I needed. Don't skip this — finger 5 carries 80% of upper-register melodies in classical repertoire. Chapter 10 — Aural at Grade 4 Grade 4 aural adds: - Identify which note of a 4-note phrase has been changed in a played comparison.- Identify cadence type: perfect, imperfect, or plagal (interrupted is Grade 5).- Identify a stronger set of features: dynamics, articulation, rhythm change, key change (to relative minor only). > Trick: for cadence ID, the question is "did it end on the tonic, and how did it get there?">> - Tonic, with a leading-7 chord before → perfect (V → I)> - Tonic, with a softer subdominant before → plagal (IV → I)> - Not tonic, hanging on the dominant → imperfect (something → V)>> Two binary tests in sequence: (a) tonic or not, (b) which chord before. Three answers cover all three cadences. Piano Notes — 03 · Grade 3 Theory & Repertoire
Created2026-05-18|Music
Grade 3 is where the syllabus starts feeling like real music theory: triplets, minor scales in three flavours, three more keys, primary triads (I, IV, V), and your first written chord symbols. The repertoire jumps too — Bach two-part inventions, Burgmüller's middle studies, Schumann children's pieces, simple Tchaikovsky. > Series map: 00 → 01 → 02 → 03 Grade 3 (this post) → 04 Grade 4. Learning Outcome - Use simple time (2/4, 3/4, 4/4) and compound time (6/8, 9/8, 12/8).- Read triplets in simple time.- Build natural and harmonic minor scales for relative minors of all keys covered.- Use major keys C, G, D, A, F, B♭, E♭ and their relative minors (A m, E m, B m, F♯ m, D m, G m, C m).- Identify intervals by quality including augmented and diminished (only A4 / d5 — the tritone — at Grade 3).- Identify and write primary triads (I, IV, V) in root position in all the above keys.- Recognise standard Italian terms for tempo, dynamics, and articulation. Chapter 1 — Three More Keys: A, E♭, and Their Minors | Major | Sharps/Flats | Relative minor ||-------|--------------|----------------|| C | none | A m || G | F♯ | E m || D | F♯ C♯ | B m || A | F♯ C♯ G♯ | F♯ m || F | B♭ | D m || B♭ | B♭ E♭ | G m || E♭ | B♭ E♭ A♭ | C m | A major (3 sharps) and E♭ major (3 flats) extend the symmetric pair around C. > Trick: visualise the circle of fifths as a clock. C is at 12. Each step clockwise adds a sharp (G, D, A, E, B…). Each step counter-clockwise adds a flat (F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭…). Grade 3 covers ±3 steps from C: clockwise to A, counter-clockwise to E♭. Reading a fast key signature When you see three sharps in the order F♯ C♯ G♯, the last is G♯ → up one semitone → A major (or its relative F♯ minor). When you see three flats in the order B♭ E♭ A♭, the second-to-last is E♭ → E♭ major (or its relative C minor). Chapter 2 — Minor Scales: Three Flavours A minor key has one key signature (the natural minor) but musicians use three common scale shapes built from that signature. Natural minor (自然小調) Use the relative-minor key signature, start from the minor tonic. Pattern in semitones: $$\textnormal{W H W W H W W} \quad = \quad 2,1,2,2,1,2,2$$ Harmonic minor (和聲小調) Raise the 7th degree of the natural minor. The interval between 6 and 7 becomes an augmented 2nd (3 semitones) — gives the unmistakable "exotic" sound. Melodic minor (旋律小調) Ascending: raise the 6th and 7th. Descending: revert to natural minor. > Where I got stuck: I kept mixing harmonic and melodic.> - Harmonic minor exists to fix harmony — it gives the V chord its major-3rd leading tone (G♯ in A minor) so the V → i cadence sounds strong. Raise the 7 only.> - Melodic minor exists to smooth melody — the harmonic minor's augmented 2nd (F → G♯) is awkward to sing, so going up you also raise the 6 (F → F♯) to "smooth" the gap. Coming down you don't need the lift, so revert.>> Framing them by purpose (harmony-fixing vs melody-smoothing) made them stop blurring. All three on one diagram Chapter 3 — Triplets A triplet is three notes played in the time of two of the same value. Notated with a "3" above or below a group: In 4/4, a beat that would normally hold two quavers (♪♪) is squeezed to fit three (♪♪♪). The three notes are equal, faster than quavers, slower than triplet semiquavers. Counting triplets Subdivide each affected beat into three syllables: "tri-pa-let" or "1-trip-let, 2-trip-let". Pick one and stick with it. > Trick: when first practising triplets, tap the beat with your foot while saying "1-trip-let, 2-trip-let, 3-trip-let, 4-trip-let" out loud. Foot is the beat (4 taps per bar), voice is the subdivision (12 syllables per bar). Once you can do this without the piano, the piano part is mechanical. Duplet — the inverse In compound time (where each beat naturally divides into 3), a duplet is two notes played in the time of three. Marked "2". Less common at Grade 3 but worth knowing. Chapter 4 — 9/8 and 12/8 (More Compound Time) | Metre | Beats per bar | Beat unit | Feel ||-------|---------------|-----------|------|| 6/8 | 2 | dotted crotchet | Gallop || 9/8 | 3 | dotted crotchet | Slow waltz, lilting || 12/8 | 4 | dotted crotchet | Blues shuffle, slow ballad | In 9/8, count three macro-beats per bar, each dotted-crotchet long, each subdividing into 3 quavers. "1-and-a, 2-and-a, 3-and-a." In 12/8, count four macro-beats: "1-and-a, 2-and-a, 3-and-a, 4-and-a." > Analogy: if 6/8 is a gallop, 9/8 is a canter in three (less symmetric), and 12/8 is a rolling blues shuffle — most "12-bar blues" songs are written in 12/8 even when the notation says 4/4 swung. Chapter 5 — Augmented and Diminished Intervals (just the tritone) Grade 3 expands the interval qualities to include augmented and diminished — but only for the augmented 4th (A4) and diminished 5th (d5), which are the same six-semitone gap (the tritone, 三全音). These two are enharmonic: same sound, different spelling. Whether you call it A4 or d5 depends on note names — count the staff positions: - C → F♯: positions = 4 (C–D–E–F) → 4th → "augmented" (one semitone larger than P4).- C → G♭: positions = 5 (C–D–E–F–G) → 5th → "diminished" (one semitone smaller than P5). > Trick: a tritone divides the octave exactly in half ($12 / 2 = 6$ semitones). It's the most dissonant interval in tonal music — historically called diabolus in musica ("the devil in music") because medieval theorists found it so unstable they avoided writing it. Today it's the engine of jazz and blues. Knowing the history hooks the memory. Chapter 6 — Primary Triads (I, IV, V) A triad (三和弦) = three notes stacked in thirds. Root, third, fifth. The primary triads of any key are built on degrees 1, 4, 5 — the tonic, subdominant, dominant. They handle the bulk of harmonic motion in tonal music. In a major key > Trick: in a major key, the primary triads (I, IV, V) are all major chords. Roman numerals are uppercase to show this. The supportive triads (ii, iii, vi) are minor — lowercase. The vii° is diminished — lowercase + degree mark. In a minor key (harmonic) Because harmonic minor raises the 7th, the V chord becomes major (it has the raised leading tone) — even though the key is minor. > Where I got stuck: V in minor is major. This seems like a contradiction — until you realise it's deliberate: the major V supplies the leading tone that pulls back to the tonic. Without that, the V → i cadence would sound weak. Once you internalise this purpose, the rule is obvious. Writing triads — Grade 3 expectations Root position only. Bass note = root. Stack a 3rd and 5th above. No inversions yet (Grade 4-5 territory). In 4-part writing (Grade 5+) you also need to know how to double notes, but Grade 3 only asks for the three-note triad spelled out. Chapter 7 — Roman Numeral Analysis (gentle introduction) Grade 3 starts using Roman numerals to label chords. They tell you the function without specifying the key — a Roman-numeral analysis transposes for free. | Numeral | Function | Quality in major | Quality in minor (natural) ||---------|----------|-------------------|-----------------------------|| I | Tonic | Major | minor || ii | Supertonic | minor | diminished || iii | Mediant | minor | Major || IV | Subdominant | Major | minor || V | Dominant | Major | minor (or Major if harmonic) || vi | Submediant | minor | Major || vii° | Leading tone | diminished | Major (subtonic, ♭VII) | > Trick: in major, the qualities pattern is M m m M M m d° ("Major, minor, minor, Major, Major, minor, diminished"). Memorise this once; apply to any major scale. The pattern never changes — only the starting key. Chapter 8 — Italian Terms (Grade 3 set) Same as Grade 2, plus: Tempo | Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Grave | Very slow, solemn || Lento | Slow || Allegretto | Slightly slower than allegro || Andantino | Slightly faster than andante (or sometimes slightly slower — context!) || Più mosso | More motion (faster) || Meno mosso | Less motion (slower) || L'istesso tempo | The same speed (when metre changes) | Dynamics + expression | Term | Meaning ||------|---------|| Crescendo / diminuendo | Already known; reinforced || Sotto voce | In a whispered voice || Con brio | With brilliance, fire || Con moto | With motion || Grazioso | Gracefully || Maestoso | Majestically || Tranquillo | Calmly | > Trick: group terms by mood rather than alphabet. "Calm cluster": tranquillo, dolce, sotto voce, cantabile. "Fast/bright cluster": vivace, brillante, con brio, con moto, agitato. When you can't remember the literal translation, your gut feel for the cluster usually gets you the right answer in an exam. Chapter 2 — Semiquavers (Sixteenth Notes) A semiquaver / 十六分音符 = half a quaver = quarter of a crotchet. In 4/4, four semiquavers fit in one crotchet's time. Beaming groups Semiquavers are usually beamed in groups of 4 (one full crotchet beat). Mixed quaver + semiquaver groupings appear too: Counting semiquavers out loud Standard system: "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a" — four syllables per crotchet beat. Each crotchet contains 4 sixteenths; each syllable maps to one sixteenth-note position. > Trick: try saying "ta-ka-di-mi" instead — many drummers use it, it's faster on the tongue than "1 e and a". As long as you commit to one consistent vocabulary, the brain locks onto the subdivision. Dotted quaver + semiquaver pattern The most common Grade 2 rhythm: In count words: "1 . . a, 2 . . a" — the dotted quaver takes 3 sixteenths, the semiquaver takes the last 1. Chapter 3 — Compound Time (6/8) In 6/8 there are two beats per bar, and each beat is a dotted crotchet (= 3 quavers). Don't be fooled by the "6" — you do not count "1-2-3-4-5-6" evenly; you feel two macro-beats, each subdivided into three. | | 6/8 | 3/4 ||---|-----|-----|| Beats per bar | 2 | 3 || Beat unit | dotted crotchet (3 quavers) | crotchet (2 quavers) || Each beat divides into | 3 | 2 || Feel | Gallop / lilt | Waltz | The strong beats are the 1 and the 4. The other digits are subdivisions. > Analogy: simple time is walking — left-right-left-right, two equal steps per beat. Compound time is galloping — DA-da-da, DA-da-da. The "DA" is the macro-beat (dotted crotchet); the "da-da" are the eighth-note subdivisions. Chapter 1 — Pitch Notation Recap Terminology - Pitch (音高) — how high or low a note sounds, measured in frequency.- Accidental — sharp ♯ / flat ♭ / natural ♮ applied to a single note.- Sharp (♯) — raise the pitch by one semitone.- Flat (♭) — lower the pitch by one semitone.- Natural (♮) — cancel a previous sharp or flat. Ledger lines (加線) Grade 1 requires one ledger above and one below in each clef. - Treble: one line above the staff = A5, one line below the staff = Middle C (C4).- Bass: one line above the staff = Middle C (C4) as well (the same note), one line below the staff = E2. > Trick: Middle C is the connector. It sits one ledger below the treble staff AND one ledger above the bass staff. Same key on the piano, two ways to write it. Drill this until it's automatic — Grade 2 and beyond use it constantly. Chapter 1 — Why piano is a good first instrument The keyboard is the clearest visual map of Western music. Every other instrument hides pitch behind a fingering chart; the piano lays the entire pitch space out in front of you, left-to-right, low-to-high. If you understand "how a scale looks" on the piano, you understand it on a guitar, a violin, or in a software synth — you just don't see it as plainly. A second practical reason: the piano is monophonically simple but polyphonically rich. Beginners can produce a clean tone from day one (press → sound), while advanced players spend a lifetime on the same instrument. Few instruments span that range. > Where I started: I wasted my first two weeks googling "best digital piano under $500" instead of practising. The truth is any 88-key weighted keyboard from a major brand (Yamaha, Roland, Casio, Kawai) is fine for the first year. The piano you have and play every day beats the piano you research and never own. Chapter 2 — Picking an instrument Acoustic (real) piano - Upright (直立式) — most homes. Chapter 3 — The keyboard map Black-key landmarks The black keys come in groups of 2 and 3. The 2-group anchors C-D-E; the 3-group anchors F-G-A-B. - The white key immediately left of a 2-group is always C.- The white key immediately left of a 3-group is always F. These two anchors are the only ones you ever need to memorise. The other letters (D, E, G, A, B) are derived by counting in alphabetical order from C or F. > Trick: the 2-group is C-D-E; 3-group is F-G-A-B. Stack the consonants in your head: Car Drives East, Fish Goes Around Back. Cheesy works. Middle C The C closest to the centre of the keyboard. On an 88-key piano this is the fourth C from the bottom — C4 in scientific pitch notation. On a 61-key it's the third C. Either way, find the centre, find the 2-group nearest it, drop your right thumb on the white key to the left of that pair. Octaves Same letter, doubled frequency: $f{n+12} = 2 \cdot fn$. The keyboard pattern repeats every 12 keys (7 white + 5 black). Once you memorise the 7-white-key span (C D E F G A B), the entire 88-key keyboard is mapped. (Smaller players: get a footstool; dangling feet cause back tension.)- Elbows roughly at keyboard height.- Shoulders down and relaxed. No shrugging up to the ears. Hands - Curved fingers, as if holding a tennis ball.- Strike with the fingertip, not the pad.- Wrist is level with the back of the hand — flat, not collapsed, not arched.- Thumb plays with its side, slightly tucked under. AI Prompting (Foundations & Practice)
Created2026-05-15|AI
Prompting is the practice of writing inputs that steer a Large Language Model (LLM) toward useful outputs. It is part interface design, part rhetoric, and part empirical science. This note covers the foundations and the techniques that have been validated by published research, with worked examples you can copy. Learning Outcome - Explain what a prompt is and why model behaviour is sensitive to its phrasing.- Decompose a prompt into its functional parts (instruction, context, examples, format spec).- Apply core techniques: zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, role conditioning, decomposition, and self-consistency.- Recognise common anti-patterns and explain why they fail.- Design an evaluation harness with a golden set + rubric + A/B framework.- Read a research paper on prompting and judge whether its claims will transfer to your task. Chapter 1 - Foundations Terminology - Prompt — the full token sequence the model conditions on before generating the next token.- Completion — the model's generated response.- Token — sub-word unit (4 characters of English on average). All limits (context window, throughput, billing) are measured in tokens.- Context window — maximum prompt+completion length the model can attend to.- In-context learning (ICL) — model adapts its behaviour from examples in the prompt without weight updates. Reported in Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, Brown et al. (2020).- Temperature — sampling parameter; $0$ is greedy, higher values increase diversity.- Top-p / Top-k — nucleus / top-k sampling, alternatives to temperature.- System / developer message — privileged prompt segment used to set persistent behaviour. Mechanism formalised by The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Why prompts matter An LLM is a next-token predictor over a learned distribution $P(tn \mid t1, \dots, t{n-1})$. The prompt is the conditional sequence $t1, \dots, t{n-1}$. Two prompts that read as semantically equivalent to a human can produce very different distributions because the model is sensitive to token-level cues it has learned to associate with answer quality (formality, code-block fences, "Let's think step by step", etc.). This sensitivity has been measured empirically. Lu et al. (2022) showed that simply reordering few-shot examples can swing accuracy on classification benchmarks by tens of percentage points. Sclar et al. (2024) showed that benign formatting changes (newline vs. colon as separator) shift task accuracy by a comparable margin. The practical consequence: prompts are programs. They deserve version control, testing, and review. How the model "reads" a prompt A typical chat-tuned model receives a structured sequence — for instance OpenAI's ChatML or Anthropic's / turn markers — where each turn is tagged with a role. The model has been fine-tuned (instruction-tuned + RLHF or DPO) to: 1. Treat the system / developer message as a high-priority constraint.2. Treat user messages as questions or requests.3. Treat its own prior responses as commitments to maintain consistency with. The training procedure was first detailed in InstructGPT, Ouyang et al. (2022) and the Anthropic RLHF paper, Bai et al. (2022). The hierarchy of trust between roles was later sharpened in The Instruction Hierarchy, Wallace et al. (2024). Chapter 2 - Anatomy of a Prompt A well-formed prompt contains, in some combination, six functional parts. Naming them helps you debug what is missing. | Part | Purpose | Example || --- | --- | --- || Role / persona | Set a frame the model uses to choose vocabulary, depth, formality | "You are a senior database administrator." || Instruction | The actual verb of the request | "Explain why this query is slow." || Context | Domain facts, retrieved documents, prior turns | The SQL query + output || Examples (shots) | Demonstrations of input → output | A prior worked optimisation || Format spec | Constraint on output shape | "Return JSON matching schema X" || Stop / guardrails | Negative constraints, refusal triggers | "If the query is fine, say only." | A minimal template The order matters less than the presence of each part. The OpenAI prompt-engineering guide (OpenAI 2024) and Anthropic's prompt library both converge on the same six-part skeleton. Chapter 3 - Core Techniques Zero-shot Ask the question directly with no examples. Works when the task is well-represented in the training distribution (translation, summarisation, common QA). Few-shot (in-context learning) Provide $k$ input/output pairs, then the new input. Brown et al. (2020) showed few-shot prompting closes a large fraction of the gap to fine-tuning on many tasks without any gradient updates. Tips: - Order examples easy → hard (Lu et al., 2022).- Make examples cover the failure modes you observe in zero-shot, not the easy cases.- Keep formatting identical across shots; a stray newline can degrade accuracy (Sclar et al., 2024). Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting Ask the model to show its work before committing to an answer. Reported in Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, Wei et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic, symbolic, and commonsense reasoning on large models. Two variants worth knowing: - Zero-shot CoT: append "Let's think step by step." Kojima et al. (2022). Cheap and surprisingly effective.- Self-consistency: sample $n$ chains at temperature $>0$, take the majority vote on the final answer. Wang et al. (2022). Improves arithmetic accuracy further at $n \times$ cost. Role / persona conditioning Setting an expert persona biases vocabulary, depth, and inferred audience. The effect is real but smaller than CoT or few-shot; treat it as fine-tuning of style, not capability. Decomposition Break a hard problem into ordered sub-problems and prompt each separately, chaining outputs. Equivalent to Khot et al. (2023) "Decomposed Prompting" or least-to-most prompting by Zhou et al. (2023). The chain is easier to debug than a single monolithic prompt; you can swap or rerun any step. ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) Interleave reasoning steps with tool calls. Yao et al. (2023). The model alternates between (a reasoning step), (a tool call), and (tool result), until ready to emit an . ReAct is the foundation pattern under most "agent" frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex, modern function-calling SDKs). XML tags have the advantage of being self-documenting and survive whitespace normalisation. Anthropic explicitly recommends XML in their prompt-engineering documentation. Markdown structure Models trained on heavy Markdown corpora respond well to headings (), lists, and tables. Use the same conventions in your prompt that you want to see in the output. Numbered constraints Numbered constraints are easier for the model to enumerate against than a paragraph of "and also, make sure to..." Format specifications Three escalating strengths: | Strength | Mechanism | Reliability || --- | --- | --- || Soft | Natural-language description ("return JSON") | 60-80% || Schema | Provide explicit schema (TypeScript / JSON Schema / Pydantic) | 90% || Hard | Use the provider's structured-output / JSON-mode API | 100% (provider-enforced) | If you need a guaranteed shape, use the provider's structured output. Soft format is convenient but will fail on tail cases. Chapter 5 - Reasoning Boosters Self-consistency Already mentioned. Formally: sample $n$ chains $c1, \dots, cn$ at $T > 0$, extract the final answer $ai$ from each, return $\arg\maxa \sumi \mathbb{1}[ai = a]$. The probability that the majority answer is correct converges to the per-sample accuracy raised to a Condorcet-style power as $n$ grows, when individual samples are conditionally independent given the question. Tree of Thoughts (ToT) Yao et al. (2023). Generalises CoT from a chain to a search tree. The model proposes multiple next-thoughts at each step, a value model scores each, and a search algorithm (BFS/DFS) explores. Helps on combinatorial problems (Game of 24, crosswords) where a single chain commits too early. Reflexion Shinn et al. (2023). After a wrong attempt, the model writes a critique of its own trace; that critique is appended to context for the next attempt. The agent improves across attempts within a single session. Step-Back prompting Zheng et al. (2024). Before answering a specific question, ask the model to first derive the general principle that governs the question. The principle then conditions the specific answer. Improves abstract / multi-step reasoning. Chapter 6 - Common Anti-patterns Vague verbs , , , are unmeasurable. The model has to guess a direction. Replace with a measurable verb: , , . Conflicting constraints The model resolves contradictions arbitrarily. Pick one side of each axis and state it. Leading questions The framing forces the model to defend a premise instead of evaluating it. Prefer: Long irrelevant context Liu et al. (2023) Lost in the Middle showed models degrade on retrieval tasks when relevant information sits in the middle of a long context. Prune aggressively. If you must include long retrieved documents, put the most relevant snippets at the start or the end. Negation-only instructions is weaker than Models follow positive constraints more reliably than negative ones. "Polite filler" adds tokens without affecting behaviour. Strip it. Chapter 7 - Evaluation A prompt is a piece of software, so evaluate it like software. Golden set A small, curated set of representative inputs with hand-graded expected outputs. 30–100 items is enough to detect a 5pp accuracy shift. Rubric Decompose "is the output good?" into yes/no axes. For a summarisation task you might score: - Factual (no hallucinated entities)- Complete (all named main points present)- Concise (within length limit)- Format (parses as JSON)- Refusal (correctly refused when no claim present) Each axis scored 0/1 by a human grader or a separate "judge" model. LLM-as-judge Zheng et al. (2023). A stronger model grades a weaker model's output against a rubric. Cheap, scales, but biased toward outputs that look like the judge's style. Use a different family of model as the judge where possible, and spot-check 10% with a human. A/B framework Track $\Delta$ per axis, not just overall. A change that improves factuality but hurts conciseness is still a regression on the conciseness axis. Statistical sanity With $n=100$ binary outcomes, the 95% confidence interval on accuracy is roughly $\pm 10$pp around the observed rate. Differences smaller than that are noise. Increase $n$ or repeat the run to tighten. Chapter 8 - Tool Use and Structured Output Modern model providers expose two APIs that should replace most prompt-engineering hacks: Function calling / tool use You declare a JSON schema for one or more tools; the model picks one and emits arguments matching the schema. The runtime executes the tool and returns the result, which you append to the conversation and prompt again. The model is fine-tuned to know when to call a tool versus answer directly. This is more reliable than ReAct in raw text because the model emits parsed JSON via the API. Structured output (JSON / Pydantic mode) You supply the full output schema; the provider's decoder constrains generation so the response is guaranteed valid against the schema. Eliminates "the model added a friendly preamble that broke my parser" failures. When NOT to use tools If the task is pure transformation of text the model already has the knowledge for (summarise, translate, classify), tools add latency and cost. Use tools when you need fresh data, deterministic computation, or side effects.
