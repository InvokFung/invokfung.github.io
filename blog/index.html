Billiards & Snooker — 15 · Swerve and Special Spots
Created2026-10-14|Sports Billiards
The last lesson in this series is a toolbox for awkward spots. When a ball blocks the line, you can sometimes curve the white round it. When the white stops touching a ball, special rules apply. And a ball hanging in the jaws is easier than it looks, with one trap. For each spot there is more than one answer; the goal is to see them all and choose. > Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack → 12 Safety → 13 Position · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes → 15 Swerve and special spots (this post). Learning Outcome - Curve the white round a ball with a raised cue and side (swerve).- Know what to do when the white is touching a ball.- Pot a ball in the jaws without going in-off.- Compare several answers for the same spot. 1 · Swerve round a ball Post 05 showed that side with a raised cue curves the white towards the side. Here it is used on purpose: 1. Aim past the blocking ball, on the side away from the curve (here left).2. Use side towards the red (here right-hand side, half-way out).3. Raise the back of the cue (here about 20°) and play at medium pace (power 3). | Cue | The same aim and side ||-----|-----------------------|| level | goes straight, misses the red ✗ || raised 20° | curves round the blue into the red ✓ | The room is small: in the model, aims across only about 1° made it round the blue and onto the red. <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b15clipswerve.webp" alt="Clip from behind the white with the cue raised and the tip on the right of the white. The white starts to the left of the blue, curves back to the right after passing it, and hits the red"></div> > Watch out: a raised cue makes a clean hit harder, and too much elevation is a massé, a much bigger curve that is hard to control. Practise swerve before you need it in a match, and prefer a cushion escape (posts 09, 14) when one is open. 2 · A touching ball When the white stops touching another ball, the referee calls "touching ball". Then: - You must play away from it (green). Playing into it (red area) moves it: a foul (a push stroke).- If the touching ball is a ball on (one you may hit), you do not need to hit another ball.- If it is not on, you must still hit a ball on as usual. So a touching ball is a chance as well as a problem: you can play away to a safe place with no ball to hit, when the touching ball is on. 3 · A ball in the jaws A red hanging over the pocket goes in from almost any contact: | Red | Room in the aim ||-----|-----------------|| in the jaws (power 1) | 5.4°: any contact pots it || 50 cm out (power 2) | 1.0° | > Common pitfall: the white follows it in. At power 1 no aim went in-off. At power 2, the fullest hits (within 0.4° of the red's centre) sent the white in-off too. Play it soft, or a little thin, so the white goes away from the pocket. 4 · Many answers: how to choose Most awkward spots have more than one way out. List them, then choose: | Spot | Answers | Choose by… ||------|---------|------------|| a ball blocks the red | one cushion (09), two or three cushions (14), swerve (this post) | the most room; swerve only when cushions are shut || touching a ball on | play away to safety, or pot another red | what is safest for you afterwards || red in the jaws | pot it soft, or thin; use it as a target for a plant (10) | the white's path after || red near the cushion | cut it, double it (08), or play safe (12) | the double kiss risk (11) | > Trick: say the options out loud before you get down. Players who only see one answer often miss a better one. 5 · Try it at the table | Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Swerve ladder | the white, a blocking colour 60 cm on, a red 80 cm beyond | curve round with the cue raised a little more each time || Touching | the white touching a red | play away without the red moving (ask a friend to watch it) || Jaws | a red over the corner pocket, the white 1 m away | pot it ten times without an in-off | Cheat-sheet | | ||--|--|| Swerve | aim past the ball, side towards the target, cue raised, medium pace || Room | small; prefer a cushion when one is open || Touching ball | play away; no need to hit another ball if it is a ball on || Jaws | any contact pots it; soft or thin to stay out || Choose | list every answer first | Planned future extensions This is the last post in the series for now. It can grow without renaming: | Topic | Where it would fit ||-------|--------------------|| Break-building: clearing the colours in order | after 13 || Long pots and the "pot or safe" decision | after 12 || Massé and jump shots (and the rules on them) | after 15 || Snookers to win a frame: needing more points than are left | after 12 || Practice routines (line-up, T-drill, colours) | a separate series post | What's next Go back to 00 · Aiming and play the drills from every post on a real table. Then the planned extensions above. Billiards & Snooker — 14 · Two- and Three-Cushion Escapes
Created2026-10-08|Sports Billiards
Post 09 escaped off one cushion. Sometimes every one-cushion line is blocked too. Then you go round the angles: two or three cushions. The aiming idea is the same mirror, used twice. This lesson shows a real "nothing simple is on" position, the double mirror, and three different ways out. > Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack → 12 Safety → 13 Position · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes (this post) → 15 Swerve and special spots. Learning Outcome - Check the direct line and both one-cushion lines first.- Aim a two-cushion escape with the double mirror.- Know that more than one route is usually open, and how to choose. 1 · Nothing simple is on The red is near the top right corner. The blue blocks the direct line, the pink blocks the route off the top side cushion, and the black blocks the route off the bottom side cushion. (A green sits next to the red, too.) Every simple way is shut. 2 · The double mirror For two cushions, mirror the red twice: 1. Mirror the red in the last cushion the white will touch (here the end cushion): point 1.2. Mirror point 1 in the first cushion (here the bottom side cushion): point 2.3. Aim at point 2. Where the line meets the first cushion is your target. For two cushions that meet at a corner, point 2 is simply the red reflected through the corner. > Watch out: a rolling white comes off each cushion a little flatter (post 07), and with two cushions that adds up. Here the escape needed the first cushion point about 5 cm shorter than the double-mirror line (a turn of about 1.7°). 3 · Watch it: round the angles <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b14clipescape.webp" alt="Clip from the baulk end: two yellow dots mark the cushion points on the bottom side cushion and the end cushion. The white is played at power 4, goes down to the bottom cushion, up to the end cushion and back out to hit the red"></div> Power 4, centre ball: bottom side cushion, end cushion, red ✓. 4 · More than one way out The same position has at least three ways out: | Route | Cushions | Room (power 4) ||-------|----------|----------------|| 1 | top side → end | 1.3° || 2 | bottom side → end | 1.1° || 3 | bottom side → top side → end | 1.1° | On the open table (no blockers), the one-cushion route off the top side cushion had 2.7°. So escapes round the angles have about half the room. That is why you check the simple lines first. How to choose between them: - the most room (route 1 here);- the fewest cushions you are confident with;- where the white ends after the red: a route that leaves it near a cushion, far from the reds, is worth a little less room. 5 · Try it at the table | Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Corner reflection | a red near a corner, the white in baulk | find point 2 through the corner, then the first cushion point || Shorter | the same at power 3 and power 4 | how much shorter the softer one needs || Three ways | the same red; play routes 1, 2 and 3 | which one you hit most often | Cheat-sheet | | ||--|--|| First | direct, then one cushion, then two || Two cushions | mirror in the last cushion, then in the first; aim at the result || Corner | the double mirror is the red reflected through the corner || Rolling white | hit the first cushion a little shorter || Choose | most room, fewest cushions, safe white | What's next 15 · Swerve and special spots. Curving round a ball, touching balls, a ball in the jaws: awkward positions with more than one answer. Billiards & Snooker — 13 · Position Play
Created2026-10-03|Sports Billiards
A break is not a list of pots. It is a chain: each pot must leave the white where the next pot is easy. That is position play, and it uses everything so far: stop, follow and screw (02), where the white goes (03), side (06) and cushions (07). This lesson shows how to think about it: aim for a zone, compare routes, and keep an angle. > Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack → 12 Safety → 13 Position (this post) · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes → 15 Swerve and special spots. Learning Outcome - Aim the white at a zone, not a spot.- Compare routes (stun, follow, screw) by where each lands.- Know why a small angle on the next ball is better than straight. 1 · A zone, not a point After this red, you want the black. The green wedges show every place where the pot on the black is comfortable: a cut of 35° or less, from 25 cm to 1 m away, into either top corner. Anywhere in green is fine. That is much easier to hit than a single spot. | Route (power 3) | White ends | Next black ||-----------------|------------|------------|| stun (a little below centre) | by the end cushion | no comfortable pot ✗ || follow (above centre) | off the end cushion, in the green | a 17° cut ✓ || screw (well below centre) | just past the red, in the green | a 2° cut, almost straight | Two routes reach the zone. Which one is better? That is the next section. 2 · Keep an angle From each landing spot, pot the black three ways (screw, stun, follow) and look where the white goes: | Black from… | The three routes reach… ||-------------|-------------------------|| almost straight (2° cut) | only 13 cm off one line || a 17° angle | up to 99 cm to the side | A dead-straight pot is easy to pot, but the white can only go forward or back along one line. A small angle lets the white go across the table to the next red. So here the follow route is the better choice. > Trick: good players want an angle of roughly a quarter to a third of a right angle on the next ball. Enough to move the white, not so much that the pot gets hard. 3 · Watch it: follow into the zone <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b13clipfollow.webp" alt="Clip from the baulk side: green wedges show the zone for the black. The red is potted into the bottom right corner with follow; the white comes off the end cushion and stops inside the green, at an angle to the black"></div> Above centre, power 3: the red drops, the white comes off the end cushion and lands in the green ✓. 4 · How to plan position 1. Look two balls ahead. Where must the white be for the next pot, and for the one after?2. Draw the zone, not a spot. Bigger is better: a zone that is long along the white's path forgives pace errors.3. Pick the route whose path runs along the zone, not across it. Then a little too hard or too soft still lands in it.4. Choose the simplest tip that does it: no side if no side works (post 06).5. Check the path for kisses and pockets (post 11). 5 · Try it at the table | Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Three routes | a red near a corner, the black on its spot | land in the zone with follow, then with screw || Angle ladder | the black from straight, 10°, 20°, 30° | how far you can send the white after it || Line-up | three reds across the table, then the black | pot all four without losing the angle | Cheat-sheet | | ||--|--|| Zone | every place with a comfortable next pot; aim for the middle of it || Route | the one whose path runs along the zone || Angle | a small one (about 15–30°) beats straight || Tip | the simplest that works; side only when needed || Plan | two balls ahead | What's next 14 · Two- and three-cushion escapes. When one cushion is blocked: around the angles. Billiards & Snooker — 12 · Safety and the Break-off
Created2026-09-27|Sports Billiards
When there is no good pot, the best shot is one that leaves your opponent nothing. That is safety. The first shot of every frame, the break-off, is a safety shot too, and it shows all the ideas at once: a thin hit, the white round the table, and the reds kept tight. This lesson walks through it. > Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack → 12 Safety (this post) → 13 Position · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes → 15 Swerve and special spots. Learning Outcome - Know what a good safety leaves: the white far away, the reds tight.- Play a break-off: thin on the end red, three cushions back to baulk.- See how a slightly thicker hit spoils it.- Pick between safety options. 1 · What good safety leaves A safety is good when, after it, your opponent: - has no easy pot (reds tight, or far from the pockets), and- has the white far from the reds, ideally close to a cushion, which makes a clean hit harder. You do not need a perfect snooker every time. A long, awkward shot for your opponent is often enough. 2 · The break-off The usual break-off: 1. White in the D, between the brown and the yellow.2. Aim to just clip the end red of the back row, on the outside.3. Power 4, a touch above centre.4. The white goes top cushion, side cushion, and back to the baulk end. <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b12clipbreak.webp" alt="Clip from behind the baulk end: the white leaves the D, clips the end red of the pack, runs round off the top and side cushions and comes back past the yellow to the baulk end"></div> In the clip, only 3 reds move more than 4 cm, and the white stops in baulk near the cushion ✓. > Trick: pick the red, then pick a mark on the top cushion where the white should land. Watch that mark, not the red, as you play. Many players also use a touch of side to bring the white off the side cushion more sharply; learn it without side first. 3 · Thickness is everything The same break-off with the aim only a fraction of a degree fuller: | Hit | White touches | Reds moved | White ends ||-----|---------------|------------|------------|| just right (picture 2 above) | the end red | 3 | baulk end, near the cushion ✓ || a bit thick (2) | the end red | 9 | baulk cushion, but reds everywhere ✗ || too thick (1) | two reds | 12 | loose in baulk ✗ | The difference between "just right" and "a bit thick" is 0.8° at the white. Thin is the safe side to miss on: a fraction too thin and you may miss the red (a foul, 4 points), but a fraction too thick opens the reds for your opponent. 4 · Other safety shots The same ideas work later in the frame. Choose by what is on the table: | Option | When | The idea ||--------|------|----------|| thin off a red, white to baulk | the reds are up the table | like the break-off: a thin contact, the white runs back off the top cushion || roll up behind a colour | a colour is near the reds | a slow hit on a red; the white stops behind the colour (a snooker) || put the red safe | a loose red is near a pocket | hit it to a cushion far from the pockets; send the white the other way | Ask, for each option: "what does my opponent have next?" Choose the one that leaves the least. 5 · Try it at the table | Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Break-off | a full rack; the white between the brown and yellow | the white back in baulk, three or fewer reds out || Thickness ladder | the same, a hair fuller each time | at which thickness the pack opens || Mark on the cushion | the same | the white lands on your chosen mark | Cheat-sheet | | ||--|--|| Good safety | white far away, near a cushion; reds tight || Break-off | thin on the end red, power 4, top → side → baulk || Thickness | 0.8° fuller already opens 9 reds || Miss on | the thin side || Choose | the option that leaves your opponent least | What's next 13 · Position play. Pot this ball and land the white in the right place for the next one. References - WPBSA, Official Rules (2022): the break-off, fouls and penalties. <https://wpbsa.com/rules/>- Dr. Dave Alciatore, safety play and the effect of hit thickness on cue ball direction. <https://billiards.colostate.edu/>- Clips come from the physics model with a tightly racked pack. Dev Essentials — 24 · Desk Health — Eye Strain, Posture, Movement and Setup
Created2026-09-25|Dev Essentials
Developers spend thousands of hours a year at a screen. Dry, tired eyes, a stiff neck and sore wrists are common, and mostly avoidable. This last post of the series covers eye strain, desk and chair setup, movement and breaks, simple stretches, warning signs, and sleep. Each recommendation carries an evidence label, because some advice rests on solid research and some is just sensible habit. It ends with a checklist and a small script to measure your own setup, then looks back over the whole series. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics → 24 Desk Health (this post). Part E · Growth outside code. > Watch out: this post is educational, not medical advice. See a doctor, physiotherapist or eye-care professional if you have pain that does not go away, numbness or tingling, weakness, or any change in your vision. Do not wait for a checklist to tell you something is wrong. Learning Outcome - Read the evidence labels and know what each one means.- Explain what causes digital eye strain and apply the 20-20-20 rule.- Set screen distance, screen height, lighting and text size to published targets.- Set chair, desk, keyboard and mouse for a neutral posture, including on a laptop.- Explain why movement matters more than one "perfect" posture.- Say what the Cochrane review found about sit-stand desks.- Recognise the warning signs of repetitive strain injury (RSI).- Measure your own setup and check it with a short script. Chapter 1 — Evidence labels | Label | Meaning ||-------|---------|| Strong evidence | Many good studies agree; systematic reviews support it || Moderate evidence | Some good studies or a review, but with limits (short follow-up, mixed results) || Limited evidence | Few, small or low-quality studies; could change with better research || Expert guidance | Recommended by official or professional bodies (OSHA, AOA, AAO, NHS), mostly on ergonomic principles rather than trials || Rule of thumb | A handy simplification; useful, but the exact numbers are not research results || Anecdotal | People report it helps; no good evidence either way | Most desk advice is expert guidance. That is not a weakness; it just means you should adjust it to your body instead of treating the numbers as exact. Chapter 2 — Digital eye strain Terminology - Digital eye strain (also called computer vision syndrome) — tired, dry, sore or blurry eyes and headaches after long screen use.- Accommodation — the eye's focusing effort. Near work keeps the focusing muscles working.- Glare — bright light reflecting off the screen or shining into your eyes. Causes - Less blinking. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) says people normally blink about 15 times a minute, but only about 5 to 7 times a minute while using computers. The eye surface dries.- Long near focus without a break.- Glare and poor lighting.- Screen too close, too high or too far.- Uncorrected vision problems. The American Optometric Association (AOA) lists them as a cause. An eye test can fix what no desk setup can. The AAO states that eye strain does not injure the eye and does not cause permanent damage. It is still worth fixing: it is uncomfortable and makes work harder. The 20-20-20 rule — expert guidance; one small trial Every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet (about 6 m) away for at least 20 seconds. Both the AAO and the AOA recommend it. The AOA also suggests resting the eyes for 15 minutes after two hours of continuous computer use (expert guidance). What does research say? A 2023 study in Contact Lens and Anterior Eye (Talens-Estarelles and colleagues) gave 29 computer users break-reminder software for two weeks. Eye strain and dry-eye symptoms improved while they used it, but the gains were gone one week after they stopped. Binocular vision and tear-film measures did not change. The study is small and short, so treat it as limited evidence that the habit helps while you keep it up. Screen distance and height — expert guidance | Source | Distance | Height ||--------|----------|--------|| OSHA computer workstations eTool | 20-40 in (50-100 cm), eye to screen | Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level; centre of the screen 15-20° below horizontal eye level || AOA | 20-28 in (about 51-71 cm) | Centre of the screen 15-20° below eye level (about 4-5 in, 10-13 cm) || AAO | About 25 in (about 64 cm), roughly arm's length | Gaze slightly downward | They agree: about an arm's length away, top edge near eye level, and you look slightly down. OSHA adds that people with bifocals may need the screen lower, so they do not tilt their head back to read through the bottom of the lens. Lighting and glare — expert guidance - Place the monitor side-on to windows (OSHA: perpendicular to the window), not facing them or with them behind it. Use blinds (AOA).- Match screen brightness to the room (AAO). A bright screen in a dark room, or a dim screen in a bright room, both strain.- Tilting the screen back too far can catch overhead lights (OSHA). Tilt it so it faces your line of sight.- A matte screen filter can cut glare (AAO). Text size — rule of thumb If you lean in to read, the text is too small. Increase the zoom or the system scaling instead of moving closer. OSHA notes text may need to be bigger on smaller monitors. Blue light and dry eyes - Blue-light glasses — moderate evidence against. The AAO does not recommend them and says there is no scientific evidence that light from computer screens damages the eyes. A 2023 Cochrane review (17 studies, 619 people) found blue-light filtering lenses may not reduce eye strain from computer use in the short term.- Dry eyes — blink on purpose during breaks, and the AAO suggests artificial tears when eyes feel dry (expert guidance).- Dark mode — anecdotal. Many people find it comfortable, especially at night. There is no strong reason to choose one theme over the other; use what feels better. > Common pitfall: buying gadgets before booking an eye test. An out-of-date prescription, or none at all, is a common cause that no filter can fix. Chapter 3 — Posture and setup OSHA's neutral posture — expert guidance OSHA's eTool describes a neutral working posture: - Hands, wrists and forearms straight, in line, roughly parallel to the floor.- Elbows close to the body, bent between 90 and 120 degrees.- Shoulders relaxed, upper arms hanging normally.- Feet fully supported by the floor, or by a footrest if the desk is not adjustable.- Thighs supported and roughly parallel to the floor; knees about level with the hips.- Back fully supported, with lumbar (lower-back) support, upright or leaning back slightly.- Head level, facing forward, balanced over the torso. Set up in this order 1. Chair first. Set the height so your whole foot rests on the floor and the back of the knee is slightly higher than the seat (OSHA). The seat should support most of your thigh without pressing into the back of the knee. Move the lumbar support to fit the small of your back.2. Keyboard at elbow height. OSHA: elbows about the same height as the keyboard, wrists straight, not bent up, down or sideways. Put the mouse right next to the keyboard to avoid reaching.3. If the desk is too high, raise the chair and add a footrest. If it is too low, raise the desk.4. Screen last, using Chapter 2's targets. Cornell University's ergonomics guide adds that a slightly reclined seated posture of about 100-110 degrees is better than a bolt-upright 90 degrees (expert guidance). Laptops — expert guidance Cornell's guide calls laptop design "inherently" problematic: when the screen is at a good height, the keyboard is not, and the other way round. For regular long sessions: - Raise the laptop on a stand so the top of the screen is near eye level.- Use an external keyboard and mouse at elbow height.- Or use an external monitor and keep the laptop as a second screen. The best posture is the next posture — expert guidance No single position is safe for eight hours. Workplace-safety bodies such as WorkSafeNB (Canada) use the phrase "the best posture is your next posture", and OSHA advises changing position through small adjustments, stretching, standing and walking. A 2019 viewpoint in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy (Slater and colleagues, expert opinion) argues there is little strong evidence that "sitting up straight" prevents back pain. The practical message: set up a comfortable, supported base position, then move often. Chapter 4 — Sitting time, breaks and standing desks What the Cochrane review found — limited evidence The Cochrane review "Workplace interventions for reducing sitting at work" (Shrestha and colleagues, 2018; searches up to August 2017) is the latest version found for this post. It included 34 studies with 3,397 office workers from high-income countries. - Sit-stand desks reduced workplace sitting by about 84 to 116 minutes per workday in the short term, and about 57 minutes a day at 3-12 months.- The quality of evidence was low to very low for most interventions, mainly due to study design limits and small samples.- The effects of policies, information and counselling were mostly inconsistent.- There was no evidence on longer-term effects, and the review did not show health benefits such as less disease. So: a sit-stand desk probably helps you sit less, at least at first. Whether that improves long-term health is not proven. Break patterns - Cornell's 20-8-2 pattern — rule of thumb. About 20 minutes sitting, 8 minutes standing, 2 minutes moving and stretching. Cornell presents it as a "ball park goal", not an exact prescription.- Cornell's break types — expert guidance: short eye breaks, micro-breaks (under 2 minutes), and a rest break every 30-60 minutes where you stand up and move.- Standing all day is not the goal. Long, still standing has its own discomfort. Alternate. A tiny reminder script (stop with Ctrl+C): rings the terminal bell where the terminal supports it. A timer app or an OS focus feature does the same job. Chapter 5 — Simple stretches — expert guidance These are from the NHS "sitting exercises" guide, adapted for a desk break. Move slowly, stay within a comfortable range, and stop if anything hurts. The NHS advises a stable chair without wheels for these, so push your office chair against a wall or use another chair. | Stretch | How | Dose (NHS) ||---------|-----|-----------|| Chest stretch | Sit tall, away from the backrest. Pull shoulders back and down, arms out to the sides, gently push the chest forward and up | Hold 5-10 s, 5 times || Upper-body twist | Sit tall, feet flat, arms crossed to opposite shoulders. Turn the upper body to one side without moving the hips, then the other | Hold 5 s, 5 each side || Neck rotation | Sit tall, shoulders down. Turn the head slowly towards one shoulder as far as comfortable, back to centre, then the other side | Hold 5 s, 3 each side || Ankle stretch | Hold the chair, straighten one leg off the floor, point the toes away, then pull them back towards you | 2 sets of 5 per foot || Hip marching | Hold the chair sides, lift one bent knee, lower it with control, then the other | 5 per leg | Stretching during breaks mainly helps you feel less stiff. This post does not claim it prevents injury; the evidence for that is not established here. Chapter 6 — RSI warning signs — expert guidance Repetitive strain injury (RSI) is pain in muscles, nerves and tendons from repeated movement and overuse. Typing is one of the listed causes, along with poor posture at work. The NHS lists symptoms that usually start gradually: - Pain that may feel burning, aching or throbbing- Stiffness and weakness- Tingling, pins and needles, or numbness- Muscle cramps- Swelling The NHS advises seeing a GP (a family doctor) if symptoms do not go away or get worse. Numbness, tingling or weakness deserve a professional look sooner rather than later. > Common pitfall: pushing through wrist or forearm pain to finish a sprint. Early, mild symptoms are the cheap time to change your setup, take breaks and get advice. Do not self-diagnose from the internet. Chapter 7 — Sleep and learning - Sleep helps memory — strong evidence (mainly lab studies). A major review (Rasch and Born, Physiological Reviews, 2013) sums up over a century of research: sleep after learning helps you keep what you learned, and memories are actively consolidated during sleep.- How much — expert guidance. Engineers learn exponential growth for algorithms, then often ignore it for their own money. Compound interest works for you in savings and against you in debt. Small fees grow into large sums over decades. This post teaches the basic ideas with formulas and small Python calculators you can run on your own numbers: budgeting, an emergency fund, debt, compound interest, inflation, index funds, fees, and the mistakes people make under stress. > Series map: 01 Text Encoding → … → 21 Estimating → 22 Design Docs → 23 Money Basics (this post) → 24 Desk Health. For decisions about your own situation, talk to a qualified, regulated adviser. Chapter 1 — Track spending, then budget You cannot plan what you do not measure. Track every outflow for one full month. Most banks let you export a CSV. Put each line into one of three buckets: - Needs — you would face real trouble if you did not pay: housing, basic food, transport to work, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.- Wants — everything else you spend on.- Savings — money moved to savings or investments, plus debt payments above the minimum. The 50/30/20 split is a rule of thumb from Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book All Your Worth. It says to aim for about 50% of after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings. It is a starting point, not a law. In a high-rent city, needs may be well above 50%. That tells you where the pressure is; it does not mean you failed. To use your own data, replace with and add a column. The 470 "left over" is money with no job. Unplanned money tends to disappear, so give it one (often: more savings). > Watch out: these calculators use for teaching. For a real ledger, store money as integer cents or (see Dev Essentials 08 · Floating Point). Chapter 2 — Pay yourself first "Pay yourself first" means you move money to savings on payday, before you spend, instead of saving whatever is left at month end. Leftovers are usually close to zero. The easiest way is an automatic transfer that runs the day your salary arrives. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) calls saving automatically "one of the easiest ways" to make saving consistent. It turns a daily choice into a one-time setup. > Trick: raise the automatic transfer each time your pay goes up. You never get used to spending the extra. Chapter 3 — The emergency fund An emergency fund is cash for surprises: job loss, a medical bill, a broken laptop. It stops a bad month from turning into high-interest debt. - Size. US regulator FINRA says financial planners often recommend three to six months of living expenses. This is common guidance, not a rule. People with variable income, one income in the household, or a job that is hard to replace often aim higher.- Where. Somewhere safe and easy to reach quickly, kept apart from spending money. It is not an investment; its job is to be there.- Start small. If six months feels impossible, the CFPB suggests thinking about the unexpected costs you have actually had before and what they cost. A first target of one month is still a real safety net. Size the fund on needs, not total spending. In a real emergency, the wants stop. Chapter 4 — High-interest debt: compounding against you Credit card rates are high. The US Federal Reserve's G.19 release (September 2026) puts the average rate on card accounts that were charged interest at 22.15% APR for Q2 2026. Rates differ by country and by card; this number is only for scale. APR is the yearly rate. A card with 22% APR charges roughly $22\% / 12 \approx 1.83\%$ per month on the unpaid balance. On a 5,000 balance, that is about 92 of interest in the first month. If you pay 100, only about 8 reduces the debt. Paying 100 a month takes over 11 years and costs more in interest than the original debt. Paying 300 takes under 2 years. FINRA calls paying down high-rate debt, such as credit card debt, "one of the best ways" to improve your financial base. The reason is simple: clearing a 22% debt is like earning a sure 22% return, which almost no investment can promise. > Common pitfall: paying only the minimum. The minimum is designed to keep the account in good standing, not to clear the debt quickly. Chapter 5 — Compound interest and the rule of 72 With compound interest, you earn interest on past interest. For a principal $P$, yearly rate $r$, compounding $n$ times a year, for $t$ years: $$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$$ As $n$ grows without limit, this becomes continuous compounding, $A = Pe^{rt}$. The compounding frequency matters a little. The rate and the time matter a lot. The rule of 72 (an approximation) To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the yearly rate in percent: at 6%, about $72 / 6 = 12$ years. The exact answer for yearly compounding is $$t = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1 + r)}$$ | Rate | Rule of 72 (years) | Exact (years) | Error ||------|------|------|------|| 1% | 72.00 | 69.66 | +2.34 || 2% | 36.00 | 35.00 | +1.00 || 4% | 18.00 | 17.67 | +0.33 || 6% | 12.00 | 11.90 | +0.10 || 8% | 9.00 | 9.01 | −0.01 || 10% | 7.20 | 7.27 | −0.07 || 12% | 6.00 | 6.12 | −0.12 || 18% | 4.00 | 4.19 | −0.19 || 24% | 3.00 | 3.22 | −0.22 | It is almost exact near 8%. At the edges of the table it is off by about 3% (at 1%) to 7% (at 24%) of the true answer. Why 72? For small $r$, $\ln(1+r) \approx r$, which gives $69.3 / \text{rate}$. Since $\ln(1+r)$ is a bit less than $r$, a slightly bigger number fits better for common rates, and 72 divides easily by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12. > Trick: the rule works for debt too. A card balance at 24% with no payments doubles in about 3 years. Chapter 6 — Inflation: real versus nominal Inflation means prices rise, so each unit of money buys less. The nominal return is the number on your statement. The real return is what you gain in buying power: $$1 + r{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r{\text{nominal}}}{1 + \pi}\qquad\text{so}\qquadr{\text{real}} \approx r{\text{nominal}} - \pi$$ where $\pi$ is the inflation rate. - A 5% return with 3% inflation is a real return of about 1.94%.- With 3% inflation, 1,000 held as cash for 10 years buys what 744 buys today.- Cash earning 1% during 3% inflation still loses buying power: 1,000 becomes 822 in today's money. This is why long-term money is often invested rather than held as cash, and why an emergency fund (short-term money) is kept as cash anyway: for it, being available matters more than growth. Chapter 7 — Stocks, bonds, index funds and diversification Terminology - Stock (share, equity) — a small piece of ownership in a company. Value goes up and down with the company and the market. Historically higher long-run returns, with large swings.- Bond — a loan to a government or company, which pays interest and returns the loan amount at the end. Usually smaller swings than stocks; can still lose value, for example when interest rates rise or the borrower fails.- Fund — a pool of many investors' money that buys many stocks and/or bonds.- Mutual fund / ETF — two common fund structures. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) trades on an exchange during the day like a stock; a traditional mutual fund is bought and sold at one price per day.- Index fund — the US SEC's investor site defines it as a mutual fund, ETF or unit investment trust that follows a passive strategy, aiming to match the return of a market index (such as the S&P 500) before fees.- Expense ratio — a fund's yearly running cost as a percentage of your money in it.- Asset allocation — how you split money between asset types (stocks, bonds, cash).- Diversification — spreading money across many investments so that one failure does not sink you. Why diversification helps A single company can fail. A broad index holds hundreds or thousands of companies, so no single one decides your result. The SEC's beginners' guide explains that the right mix of investments may reduce losses and smooth returns without giving up too much potential gain. Diversification does not remove market risk: when the whole market falls, a diversified portfolio falls too, just less wildly than one bad stock. > Common pitfall: "diversifying" into many funds that hold the same companies. Five large-company funds from the same market may be one bet with five fees. Chapter 8 — Risk versus time horizon Time horizon is how long until you need the money. The SEC's guide says the right asset allocation depends largely on your time horizon and your ability to take risk. A toy model shows why. It assumes each year's return is random with a mean of 6% and a standard deviation of 15%, years independent. These are made-up numbers, not a forecast. Real markets have crashes bigger than this model allows, and bad years can cluster. In this model, the yearly average over a long period swings much less than a single year does, and the chance of ending below where you started falls. That is the logic behind a common guideline: money you need within a few years is kept out of volatile assets, while money for decades can ride out bad years. (Note also that the median is below 6%: big swings lower the typical compound result. This is often called volatility drag.) Chapter 9 — Why fees matter A fee is taken every year, from a balance that keeps growing. The loss compounds, just like returns do. The US SEC's investor bulletin on fees shows 100,000 invested for 20 years at 4% a year: with a 1% yearly fee, the result is nearly 30,000 lower than with a 0.25% fee. Now a longer case. Assumptions (made up for illustration): start with 10,000, add 500 every month for 30 years, earn 6.0% a year before fees every year, fee charged monthly as a share of the balance, no taxes or inflation. The gap (90,800) is much bigger than the extra fees paid (about 48,000). The rest is the growth those fees would have earned. The SEC check reproduces the bulletin: 0.50% costs about 10,000 and 1.00% costs about 28,000 compared with 0.25%. > Trick: when comparing funds that track the same index, the expense ratio is one of the few things you know for certain in advance. Returns are uncertain; fees are not. Chapter 10 — Lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging Suppose you have a large sum to invest at once (a bonus, an inheritance). You can invest it all now (lump sum) or feed it in over months (dollar-cost averaging, DCA). One well-known study: Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later" (Shtekhman, Tasopoulos and Wimmer, July 2012). Setup and findings: - Markets: United States (1926–2011), United Kingdom (1976–2011), Australia (1984–2011).- Portfolio: 60% stocks / 40% bonds. DCA: equal monthly amounts over 12 months, the rest held in cash. Results compared over rolling 10-year periods.- Lump sum ended ahead in about two-thirds of periods: 67% (US), 67% (UK), 66% (Australia).- On average the lump-sum portfolio ended 2.3% higher in the US (2.2% UK, 1.3% Australia).- Longer DCA made it worse: in the US, lump sum beat 36-month DCA in about 90% of periods.- DCA did better in falling markets. In US 12-month windows, lump sum lost money in 22.4% of periods and DCA in 17.6%. The authors' reading: lump sum wins more often because stocks and bonds usually beat cash; DCA can make sense for someone mainly worried about short-term losses and regret. Note this is one study of past data; it does not predict any future period. Vanguard published a follow-up on the same question in February 2023. > Watch out: this study is about a sum you already have. Investing part of each paycheck as it arrives is also called DCA, but it is a different situation: the money did not exist earlier. The same paper calls that a prudent way to invest. Chapter 11 — Behaviour: the costly mistakes The biggest risk is often the investor, not the market. - Chasing performance — buying what went up most recently, often near its peak.- Panic selling — FINRA describes it as selling large amounts quickly out of fear of further falls, without careful analysis. It turns a paper loss into a real one and can miss the recovery.- Overconfidence and over-trading — frequent changes add costs and timing mistakes.- Checking too often — daily prices are mostly noise, and noise makes people act. The damage is measurable. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap" 2025 study (one annual industry study) estimated that over the 10 years to December 2024, the average dollar in US mutual funds and ETFs earned about 7.0% a year, while the funds themselves returned about 8.2%. The 1.2-point gap, about 15% of the total return, came mostly from the timing of investors' buys and sells. Defences that work because they remove in-the-moment decisions: 1. Write a one-page plan: goal, time horizon, allocation, what you will do when markets fall 20%.2. Automate contributions (Chapter 2).3. Rebalance on a schedule (for example once a year), not on feelings.4. Rule of thumb: wait 24 hours before any unplanned change. Chapter 1 — Why write design docs A design doc is a short document written before coding that describes the plan and, most of all, the trade-offs. Changing a paragraph is cheaper than changing a service. Malte Ubl's article "Design Docs at Google" (2020) describes the practice there: - Design docs are informal documents. Authors write them before coding starts, to record the high-level plan and key design decisions, with a focus on trade-offs.- Common sections: context and scope, goals and non-goals, the actual design (including system-context diagrams, APIs, data storage), alternatives considered, and cross-cutting concerns such as security, privacy and observability.- Length: about 10-20 pages for a larger project; "mini design docs" of 1-3 pages for smaller, incremental work.- A lifecycle: write and iterate quickly, get a wider review, update the doc during implementation when reality differs, then keep it as a record.- Skip it when the solution is obvious and there are no real trade-offs to discuss, or when fast prototyping matters more. What it buys you: colleagues find the flaw before you write 3,000 lines; everyone reads the same plan; non-goals stop the scope from growing in secret; six months later the "why" is still written down; and the breakdown and risks feed straight into an estimate (see Dev Essentials 21 · Estimating). Chapter 1 — Why estimates slip The planning fallacy Kahneman and Tversky described the planning fallacy in 1979 ("Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures"): people predict how long their own tasks will take by imagining how the work will go, and they underweight what usually happens to tasks like it. Their forecasts come out too optimistic. Buehler, Griffin and Ross tested it in 1994 ("Exploring the 'planning fallacy'", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 67(3), 366-381). In Study 1, students predicted when they would finish their honours thesis: | Prediction asked for | Average predicted days | Average actual days | Finished by that prediction ||----------------------|------------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------|| Best guess (as accurate as possible) | 33.9 | 55.5 | 29.7% || Optimistic (everything goes as well as possible) | 27.4 | 55.5 | 10.8% || Pessimistic (everything goes as badly as possible) | 48.6 | 55.5 | 48.7% | (33 students had finished when the records were closed, so the averages are over those 33.) Even the worst-case guess was too short on average. This is the core lesson: your gut estimate is a best case in disguise. Hofstadter's law An aphorism, not a research finding, from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979). It says a task always takes longer than you expect — even when you allow for Hofstadter's law. It is a joke with a true point — knowing about the bias does not remove it. You need a method, not willpower. The cone of uncertainty (a model) Barry Boehm drew a "funnel curve" in Software Engineering Economics (1981); Steve McConnell later popularised it as the cone of uncertainty. The idea: at the initial concept stage, the real effort can be anywhere from about 0.25× to 4× the estimate (a 16× range). The range narrows as decisions are made — requirements, design, code. Treat it as a model, with two caveats: - McConnell himself says the cone shows the best accuracy skilled estimators can get. It is easy to do worse.- The cone does not narrow by itself. Todd Little (IEEE Software, 2006) studied projects at Landmark Graphics and found estimate errors followed a right-skewed (lognormal) distribution, and the uncertainty range stayed nearly the same through the project — against the popular reading of the cone. Uncertainty shrinks only when you actively remove it. What people forget Most slips are not bad maths. They are missing work: - Unknown unknowns — the library that does not support your case; the API that is rate-limited.- Integration — each part works alone; wiring them together takes days.- Review and rework — code review, QA feedback, security review.- Tests, docs, deploy, config, migrations, monitoring.- Everything else — meetings, support, on-call, holidays, sick days, context switching. Chapter 4 — Ranges, outside view, spikes and buffers Give a range with a confidence level A single date sounds precise but carries no information about risk. Say instead: > "Most likely around 10-11 working days. I am 80% confident it is done within 13, assuming the email provider approves our account this week." Use the same words every time — P50, P80, P95 — so people learn what they mean. A P80 date is missed one time in five; say so up front. Reference-class forecasting (the outside view) Your plan is the inside view. The outside view asks: how long did similar work take before? Bent Flyvbjerg built this into reference-class forecasting ("From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right", Project Management Journal, 2006), based on Kahneman and Tversky's work. Three steps: 1. Pick a reference class of past, similar projects — broad enough to have data, narrow enough to be comparable.2. Find the distribution of outcomes in that class (for example, actual ÷ estimate).3. Place your project in that distribution, and adjust your estimate with it. For a developer, the reference class is often your own history: "The last four API integrations took 1.4× to 2× my estimate." Spikes for unknowns A spike comes from Extreme Programming: a small, throw-away experiment to reduce a technical risk or make an estimate more reliable. Time-box it ("half a day to prove the PDF library can do Chinese fonts"), then estimate with the answer in hand. Explicit buffers Do not pad every task in secret. Padding gets spent (work expands to fill the time) and nobody can see the risk. Instead, estimate tasks honestly and add one visible buffer line at the project level, sized from the P50-P80 gap or from named risks. Report how much buffer is left as the project goes on. Chapter 7 — Communicating estimates Keep estimate, target and commitment apart McConnell's Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (2006) separates three things that people often mix up: - an estimate is a prediction ("P80 is 13 days");- a target is a business wish ("we want it before the conference");- a commitment is a promise ("we will ship the core flow by Oct 21"). When a target is shorter than the estimate, do not shrink the estimate. Change the scope, the people, or the date — and say which. A good estimate message 1. The range and its confidence: "P50 Oct 19, P80 Oct 21."2. The assumptions: "Assumes the email provider approves us by Friday; excludes the admin screen."3. The biggest risks: "Email approval (30% chance of +3 days)."4. When you will update it: "I will re-estimate after the spike on Wednesday." Re-estimate when the facts change An estimate is a forecast, not a vow. When scope grows or a risk happens, update the range at once and say why. Bad news early is useful; bad news on the due date is not. Story points vs hours Story points are a team convention, not a law. Gold for a newcomer. Chapter 2 — The three-pass method The source is S. Keshav, "How to Read a Paper", ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review 37(3), July 2007, pages 83-84. It is two pages long. Keshav has updated it over the years; later versions (for example, one dated February 17, 2016) keep the same method. The key idea: read a paper in up to three passes, not once from start to end. Each pass has its own goal. You can stop after any pass. First pass — the bird's-eye view (about 5-10 minutes) Keshav's steps: 1. Carefully read the title, abstract and introduction.2. Read the section and sub-section headings, and ignore everything else.3. Glance at the math (if any) to see what theory it rests on.4. Read the conclusions.5. Glance over the references and tick off the ones you have already read. At the end, you should be able to answer the five Cs: | C | Question ||---|----------|| Category | What type of paper is this? A measurement paper? An analysis of an existing system? A description of a research prototype? || Context | Which other papers is it related to? What theory does it use? || Correctness | Do the assumptions seem valid? || Contributions | What are the main contributions? || Clarity | Is it well written? | Now you decide. Maybe the paper does not interest you. Maybe you lack the background. Maybe the assumptions look wrong. Stopping here is fine. Keshav says the first pass is enough for papers outside your area that may matter some day. > Trick: the same logic works in reverse when you write. Most readers make only one pass. Clear headings and a tight abstract decide whether anyone reads further. (See Dev Essentials 22 · Design Docs.) Second pass — the content (up to about an hour) Read with more care, but skip details such as proofs. Write key points and questions in the margin as you go. Two special jobs: 1. Look hard at the figures, diagrams and graphs. Are the axes labelled? Are there error bars, so the result is statistically meaningful? Keshav notes that mistakes like these separate rushed work from excellent work.2. Mark relevant references you have not read. They are your reading list for background. After this pass you should be able to summarise the main idea, with evidence, to someone else. Keshav says this pass takes up to an hour for an experienced reader. Sometimes you still will not understand the paper. The topic may be new, the terms unfamiliar, or the writing poor. You then have three choices: (a) put it aside, (b) come back after reading background material, or (c) push on to the third pass. Third pass — virtual re-implementation (hours) The key is to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work in your head (or on paper, or in code). Then compare your version with theirs. The gaps show you both the new ideas and the hidden weak points. - Challenge every assumption in every statement.- Ask how you would present each idea.- Write down ideas for future work. Time: the 2007 version says about four or five hours for beginners and about an hour for an experienced reader. The 2016 version is less optimistic: many hours for beginners and more than an hour or two even for experienced readers. At the end, you should be able to rebuild the paper's structure from memory, and point out implicit assumptions, missing citations and problems with the experiments or analysis. The three passes at a glance | Pass | Time (Keshav) | You read | You come out with ||------|---------------|----------|-------------------|| 1 | 5-10 min | Title, abstract, intro, headings, conclusions, reference list | The five Cs; a go / no-go decision || 2 | Up to 1 hour | Whole paper, skip proofs; study figures | A summary with evidence; a list of unread references || 3 | Hours | Everything, in depth | A mental re-implementation; strengths, weak points, future work | > Common pitfall: starting with pass 3 on page 1. It shows which parts of the idea actually matter.- Look for missing rows: a dataset or setting the method might lose on. > Trick: before reading the evaluation text, write one sentence per figure: "This shows that ." Weigh it. - Peer review is a filter, not a proof. Reviewers check that the work is new, sound and clear. They rarely re-run the experiments.- Venue. A paper at a top venue passed a hard filter. Billiards & Snooker — 11 · Kisses, Cannons and the Pack
Created2026-09-12|Sports Billiards
So far each shot has had one or two balls. Real frames have many, and balls meet again on the way. A kiss is a second, unplanned contact; a cannon is the white bouncing off another ball, on purpose or not. This lesson shows the most common kiss, two ways round it, and how to pot a red and open the pack in one shot. > Series map. ★ Basics: 00 Aiming → 01 The hit → 02 Stop, follow, screw → 03 Where the white goes · ★★ Club: 04 Thin cuts → 05 Side spin → 06 Cut and side into pockets → 07 Cushions → 08 Doubles → 09 Escaping a snooker · ★★★ Advanced: 10 Plants and sets → 11 Kisses and the pack (this post) → 12 Safety → 13 Position · ★★★★ Hard: 14 Two- and three-cushion escapes → 15 Swerve and special spots. Learning Outcome - Spot a double kiss before you play.- Get round it by changing the angle, not the pace.- Pot a red and use the white to open the pack a little.- Check the white's whole path for other balls. 1 · The double kiss <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b11clipkiss.webp" alt="Slow-motion clip, close to the top side cushion: the white hits a red that sits 6 cm off the cushion, full ball. The red hits the cushion, comes straight back and hits the white again, and both balls go off line"></div> A red sits 6 cm off the cushion. You want to double it (post 08). If the white hits it full, the red reaches the cushion, bounces straight back, and meets the white again before the white has gone anywhere. That second contact is a double kiss: the red goes off line. > Watch out: pace does not fix it. At power 3, 4 and 5 the white met the red twice every time. The white is simply too close. 2 · Two ways round it Change the angle, so the white moves out of the red's way after the first contact: | | White comes in | Contacts | Result ||--|----------------|----------|--------|| 1 | straight (full ball) | 2 | ✗ double kiss || 2 | 30° from the right (cut-back double) | 1 | ✓ || 3 | 30° from the left (cross double) | 1 | ✓ | A cut needs more pace (post 08); here power 5. Both work. Choose by where the white goes afterwards. The same kiss happens on a plant or a pot when a ball is close to a cushion and you hit it nearly full into the cushion. Before any shot like that, ask: "after the first contact, does the white get out of the way?" 3 · Pot a red and open the pack Early in a frame the reds sit in a pack. You need them open, but not spread all over the table. One shot can do both: pot a loose red, and let the white run on into the pack. <div class="bclip"><img class="no-lightbox" src="/assets/billiards/b11clippack.webp" alt="Clip from the baulk side: the white pots a red near the pack into the top corner with follow, comes off the side cushion and runs into the side of the pack, which opens and lets a few reds out"></div> Here: a cut of about 20°, top spin, power 4. The red goes in ✓, the white comes off the side cushion and runs into the pack, and four reds come out by more than 6 cm. The pack also stops the white: it ends near the pink, not somewhere loose. > Trick: plan which part of the pack the white meets. A glancing hit on its side or shoulder opens a few reds and keeps the white near them; a hard, full hit spreads reds everywhere, some of them safe for nobody. 4 · Check the white's whole path Before you play, trace the white after the pot: | Question | Why ||----------|-----|| Does it pass close to another ball? | a kiss can knock it off course, or into a pocket || Does it hit the pack? | good if you want them open; plan which side || Does it end touching a ball? | a touching ball is awkward next shot (post 15) | If the path is crowded, change the spin or the pace to move it, or pick another red. 5 · Try it at the table | Drill | Set-up | Look for ||-------|--------|----------|| Kiss check | a red 6 cm off a side cushion; the white straight behind, then 30° to each side | count the contacts || Split | the reds in the pack, one loose red near the top cushion | pot it and get three or four reds out || Shoulder | the same, aim the white at the front, then the side | how many reds open each time | Cheat-sheet | | ||--|--|| Double kiss | a ball close to a cushion, hit full into it || Fix | change the angle (cut from either side), not the pace || Open the pack | pot a red and run into its side or shoulder || White's path | check for kisses, pockets and touching balls | What's next 12 · Safety. When there is no pot: leave the white far away and the reds tight.
