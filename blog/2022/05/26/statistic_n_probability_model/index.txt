11:T1bf33,<p>Statistics turns <em>data you happened to collect</em> into <em>claims about a world you did not observe</em>; probability is the machinery that makes those claims auditable. This post is a single self-contained pass over the foundation — the axioms, random variables, the distribution zoo, the limit theorems, estimation, hypothesis testing (including the two definitions everyone botches), regression, and the exact point where classical statistics becomes machine learning. Written for engineers who already write code and now need to reason about uncertainty without hand-waving. Every number below has been checked and is reproducible with <code>numpy</code> and <code>scipy.stats</code>.</p>

<ul>

<li>Work with random variables through PMF / PDF / CDF, and compute expectation, variance, covariance and correlation from their algebraic properties.</li>

</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-1-—-Probability-foundations"><a href="#Chapter-1-—-Probability-foundations" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 1 — Probability foundations"></a>Chapter 1 — Probability foundations</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology"><a href="#Terminology" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sample space (Ω)</strong> — the set of all possible outcomes. One die: <code>Ω = {1,2,3,4,5,6}</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Event</strong> — any subset of Ω. &quot;The roll is even&quot; is <code>{2,4,6}</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Probability measure (P)</strong> — assigns a number in <code>[0,1]</code> to each event, obeying the axioms.</li>
<li><strong>Mutually exclusive (disjoint)</strong> — <code>A ∩ B = ∅</code>, the events cannot both occur. <strong>Exhaustive</strong> — a collection whose union is all of Ω.</li>
<li><strong>Independent</strong> — knowing one event occurred tells you nothing about the other.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-axioms-Kolmogorov-1933"><a href="#The-axioms-Kolmogorov-1933" class="headerlink" title="The axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933)"></a>The axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933)</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">A1  Non-negativity       P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A</span><br><span class="line">A2  Normalisation        P(Ω) = 1</span><br><span class="line">A3  Countable additivity for pairwise disjoint A₁, A₂, ...</span><br><span class="line">                         P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ...) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + ...</span><br><span class="line">Consequences:  P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)    P(∅) = 0    A ⊆ B ⇒ P(A) ≤ P(B)</span><br><span class="line">               P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> the addition rule loses its correction term only when the events are disjoint. <code>P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)</code> is a special case, not the general rule.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Counting"><a href="#Counting" class="headerlink" title="Counting"></a>Counting</h2>
<p>When every outcome in a finite Ω is equally likely, <code>P(A) = |A| / |Ω|</code> and the problem is pure counting. The only question is whether <strong>order matters</strong>: <code>P(n,r) = n!/(n−r)!</code> for ordered selections, <code>C(n,r) = n!/(r!(n−r)!)</code> for unordered, and <code>n₁·n₂·...·n_k</code> for <code>k</code> independent stages.</p>
<ul>
<li>Three distinct medals among 10 runners (ordered) is <code>P(10,3) = 10·9·8 = 720</code>; a three-person committee from the same 10 (unordered) is <code>C(10,3) = 720/3! = 120</code>.</li>
<li>Five-card poker hands: <code>C(52,5) = 2,598,960</code>. A flush (any suit, straight flushes included) is <code>4·C(13,5)/C(52,5) = 5148/2,598,960 ≈ 0.00198</code> — about <strong>0.198%</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Birthday problem</strong>: <code>P(match) = 1 − (365/365)(364/365)...((365−n+1)/365)</code> gives <strong>0.5073</strong> at <code>n = 23</code> and <strong>0.9704</strong> at <code>n = 50</code>. 23 people beats a coin flip because they generate <code>C(23,2) = 253</code> <em>pairs</em>, not 23 opportunities.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Conditional-probability-and-independence"><a href="#Conditional-probability-and-independence" class="headerlink" title="Conditional probability and independence"></a>Conditional probability and independence</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)                       defined when P(B) &gt; 0</span><br><span class="line">P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)·P(B) = P(B | A)·P(A)</span><br><span class="line">A ⊥ B  ⟺  P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)  ⟺  P(A | B) = P(A)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Roll two fair dice. <code>A</code> = &quot;sum is 8&quot; (<code>5/36</code>: 2-6, 3-5, 4-4, 5-3, 6-2), <code>B</code> = &quot;first die is 3&quot; (<code>6/36</code>), <code>A ∩ B</code> = {3-5} (<code>1/36</code>). So <code>P(A|B) = (1/36)/(6/36) = 1/6 ≠ 5/36</code> — not independent.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> <em>mutually exclusive</em> and <em>independent</em> are near-opposites, not synonyms. Disjoint events with positive probability satisfy <code>P(A|B) = 0 ≠ P(A)</code>, so they are maximally dependent.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Law-of-total-probability-and-Bayes-theorem"><a href="#Law-of-total-probability-and-Bayes-theorem" class="headerlink" title="Law of total probability and Bayes' theorem"></a>Law of total probability and Bayes' theorem</h2>
<p>If <code>B₁, ..., B_k</code> partition Ω then <code>P(A) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ)·P(Bᵢ)</code>. Two factories: F1 makes 70% of units at a 2% defect rate, F2 makes 30% at 5%, so <code>P(defect) = 0.70·0.02 + 0.30·0.05 = 0.029</code>.</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">             P(B | A)·P(A)</span><br><span class="line">P(A | B) = ───────────────────    with P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) + P(B|Aᶜ)·P(Aᶜ)</span><br><span class="line">                  P(B)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Read it as an update: <strong>prior</strong> <code>P(A)</code>, <strong>likelihood</strong> <code>P(B|A)</code>, <strong>posterior</strong> <code>P(A|B)</code>. Which factory made a defective part? <code>P(F1|defect) = 0.014/0.029 ≈ 0.483</code> and <code>P(F2|defect) = 0.015/0.029 ≈ 0.517</code> — F2 makes under a third of the parts but is the more likely source of any given defect.</p>
<h2 id="The-medical-test-and-the-base-rate-fallacy"><a href="#The-medical-test-and-the-base-rate-fallacy" class="headerlink" title="The medical test and the base-rate fallacy"></a>The medical test and the base-rate fallacy</h2>
<p>A disease affects <strong>0.5%</strong> of the population. A test has <strong>99% sensitivity</strong> (<code>P(+|D) = 0.99</code>) and <strong>95% specificity</strong> (so a 5% false-positive rate). You test positive. What is <code>P(D|+)</code>? Natural frequencies over 100,000 people make it obvious:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">100,000 people</span><br><span class="line">├──    500 have the disease (0.5%)   →   495 test positive  ← true positives</span><br><span class="line">└── 99,500 do not                    → 4,975 test positive  ← false positives</span><br><span class="line">Total positives = 5,470        P(D | +) = 495 / 5,470 ≈ 0.0905   →  9.05%</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Same in Bayes form:</span><br><span class="line">P(D | +) = (0.99·0.005) / (0.99·0.005 + 0.05·0.995) = 0.00495 / 0.0547 ≈ 0.0905</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line">sens, spec, prev = <span class="hljs-number">0.99</span>, <span class="hljs-number">0.95</span>, <span class="hljs-number">0.005</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(sens * prev / (sens * prev + (<span class="hljs-number">1</span> - spec) * (<span class="hljs-number">1</span> - prev)))   <span class="hljs-comment"># 0.09049360146252285</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>A &quot;99% accurate&quot; test leaves you with a <strong>9%</strong> chance of being ill, because the false positives come from a pool 199 times larger than the true positives. Ignoring <code>P(D)</code> when computing <code>P(D|+)</code> is the <strong>base-rate fallacy</strong>. Retest with an independent second test and the posterior becomes the new prior: of the 5,470, <code>495·0.99 = 490.05</code> diseased and <code>4,975·0.05 = 248.75</code> healthy test positive again, so <code>P(D|++) = 490.05/738.80 ≈ 0.663</code> — <strong>66.3%</strong>. That is why screening programmes always confirm.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> that second calculation assumes the two tests' errors are conditionally independent given disease status. Two tests using the same biochemical mechanism fail on the same patients, and the true posterior is lower than 66%.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-2-—-Random-variables"><a href="#Chapter-2-—-Random-variables" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 2 — Random variables"></a>Chapter 2 — Random variables</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology-1"><a href="#Terminology-1" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Random variable (X)</strong> — a function from the sample space to the reals. A rule, not a storage slot.</li>
<li><strong>PMF <code>p(x) = P(X = x)</code></strong> — discrete only. <strong>PDF <code>f(x)</code></strong> — continuous only; a <em>density</em>, not a probability. <strong>CDF <code>F(x) = P(X ≤ x)</code></strong> — both; non-decreasing, right-continuous, 0 → 1.</li>
<li><strong>Support</strong> — the values with non-zero probability or density. <strong>i.i.d.</strong> — independent and identically distributed, the default assumption for a sample.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Discrete                           Continuous</span><br><span class="line">p(x) ≥ 0,  Σ p(x) = 1              f(x) ≥ 0,  ∫ f(x) dx = 1</span><br><span class="line">P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = Σ_{a≤x≤b} p(x)      P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx</span><br><span class="line">F(x) = Σ_{t≤x} p(t)                F(x) = ∫_{−∞}^{x} f(t) dt,  f(x) = F′(x)</span><br><span class="line">P(X = a) = p(a)                    P(X = a) = 0  always  ⇒  P(X&lt;a) = P(X≤a)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> because <code>P(X = a) = 0</code> in the continuous case, <code>P(X &lt; a)</code> and <code>P(X ≤ a)</code> agree there — but for a discrete variable they differ by <code>p(a)</code>. Off-by-one errors in binomial and Poisson tails almost always trace back to this.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Expectation-variance-and-their-algebra"><a href="#Expectation-variance-and-their-algebra" class="headerlink" title="Expectation, variance, and their algebra"></a>Expectation, variance, and their algebra</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">E[X] = Σ x·p(x)  or  ∫ x·f(x) dx        E[g(X)] = Σ g(x)p(x)  or  ∫ g(x)f(x) dx</span><br><span class="line">Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − (E[X])²  SD(X) = √Var(X)   (units of X)</span><br><span class="line">E[a] = a   E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + b   Var(a) = 0   Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X)</span><br><span class="line">E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]      ← linearity holds even for DEPENDENT X, Y</span><br><span class="line">E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y]           ← independence required</span><br><span class="line">Var(X ± Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) ± 2·Cov(X, Y)</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">One fair die:  E[X] = 3.5,  E[X²] = 91/6 ≈ 15.1667</span><br><span class="line">               Var(X) = 91/6 − 3.5² = 35/12 ≈ 2.9167,  SD ≈ 1.7078</span><br><span class="line">Two dice:      E[sum] = 7,  Var(sum) = 35/6 ≈ 5.833,   SD ≈ 2.415</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>For independent variables the covariance term vanishes and variances <strong>add in both cases</strong> — subtracting random variables does not subtract uncertainty.</p>
<h2 id="Covariance-and-correlation"><a href="#Covariance-and-correlation" class="headerlink" title="Covariance and correlation"></a>Covariance and correlation</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ)] = E[XY] − E[X]·E[Y]      ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σₓ·σᵧ)</span><br><span class="line">Sample:  r = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)² ),  −1 ≤ ρ, r ≤ 1</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Covariance carries units (kg·cm, dollars·hours) and is hard to read; correlation is the unitless rescaling, hitting <code>±1</code> exactly when <code>Y</code> is an affine function of <code>X</code>.</p>
<p><strong>Correlation measures linear association only.</strong> Let <code>X</code> take <code>−2, −1, 1, 2</code> with equal probability and <code>Y = X²</code> — a perfect deterministic relationship. Then <code>E[X] = 0</code> and <code>E[XY] = E[X³] = (−8 − 1 + 1 + 8)/4 = 0</code>, so <code>Cov(X,Y) = 0</code> and <code>ρ = 0</code>. Zero correlation, total dependence. (<code>np.corrcoef([-2,-1,1,2], [4,1,1,4])[0,1]</code> returns 0 up to floating point; the continuous twin is <code>X ~ Uniform(−1,1)</code>, <code>Y = X²</code>.)</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> <code>ρ = 0</code> means &quot;no <em>linear</em> trend&quot;, never &quot;independent&quot;. Independence implies zero correlation; the converse is false except for jointly normal variables. Plot the scatter before trusting <code>r</code>. And correlation is still not causation — <code>X → Y</code>, <code>Y → X</code>, a common cause <code>Z</code>, and selection effects in how the data was gathered all produce the same <code>r</code>.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="Bernoulli-binomial-geometric"><a href="#Bernoulli-binomial-geometric" class="headerlink" title="Bernoulli, binomial, geometric"></a>Bernoulli, binomial, geometric</h2>
<p><code>P(X = k) = C(n,k)·pᵏ·(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ</code>, valid when trials are independent and <code>p</code> constant. Ten independent requests each failing with probability 0.3:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">P(X = 3) = C(10,3)·0.3³·0.7⁷ = 120 · 0.027 · 0.0823543 = 0.26683</span><br><span class="line">P(X ≤ 3) = 0.02825 + 0.12106 + 0.23347 + 0.26683 = 0.64961</span><br><span class="line">E[X] = 3     Var(X) = 10·0.3·0.7 = 2.1     SD ≈ 1.449</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Geometric: <code>P(X = k) = (1−p)^(k−1)·p</code>. With <code>p = 0.2</code> the expected number of attempts is <code>5</code> and <code>P(X &gt; 10) = 0.8¹⁰ ≈ 0.1074</code>. Like the exponential it is <strong>memoryless</strong> — past failures do not improve your prospects.</p>
<h2 id="Poisson-and-the-binomial-limit"><a href="#Poisson-and-the-binomial-limit" class="headerlink" title="Poisson and the binomial limit"></a>Poisson and the binomial limit</h2>
<p><code>P(X = k) = e^(−λ)·λᵏ/k!</code> — rare events at a constant average rate <code>λ</code> in a fixed window. A support line averaging 3 calls/hour: <code>P(X = 5) = e⁻³·243/120 = 0.10082</code>, <code>P(X = 0) = e⁻³ = 0.04979</code>, <code>P(X ≥ 1) = 0.95021</code>.</p>
<p>If <code>n → ∞</code> and <code>p → 0</code> with <code>np = λ</code> fixed, <code>Binomial(n,p) → Poisson(λ)</code>:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">n = 1000, p = 0.002, λ = 2   Binomial   Poisson</span><br><span class="line">              P(X = 0)       0.13506    0.13534</span><br><span class="line">              P(X ≤ 1)       0.40573    0.40601</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Three decimals of agreement. Rules of thumb: good when <code>n ≥ 100</code> and <code>p ≤ 0.01</code>, usually acceptable from <code>n ≥ 20</code> with <code>p ≤ 0.05</code>. The error is of order <code>p</code>, so it is <code>p</code> — not <code>n</code> — that sets the quality.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> use Poisson when you know a <em>rate</em> but not a <em>trial count</em> — packets dropped per second, defects per square metre, 500-errors per minute. If you cannot say what <code>n</code> is, the binomial was never the model.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Uniform-exponential-normal"><a href="#Uniform-exponential-normal" class="headerlink" title="Uniform, exponential, normal"></a>Uniform, exponential, normal</h2>
<p><code>Uniform(a,b)</code> has constant density <code>1/(b−a)</code>; its practical roles are the output of a random number generator and the input to inverse-transform sampling (<code>F⁻¹(U)</code> has CDF <code>F</code>). <strong>Exponential</strong> is the waiting time between Poisson events:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">f(t) = λe^(−λt)    F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt)    P(T &gt; t) = e^(−λt)</span><br><span class="line">E[T] = 1/λ         Var(T) = 1/λ²</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">λ = 0.1 per minute (mean gap 10 min):</span><br><span class="line">  P(T ≤ 5) = 1 − e^(−0.5) = 0.39347      P(T &gt; 15) = e^(−1.5) = 0.22313</span><br><span class="line">Memorylessness:  P(T &gt; s+t | T &gt; s) = P(T &gt; t)</span><br><span class="line">  P(T &gt; 25 | T &gt; 10) = e^(−2.5)/e^(−1.0) = e^(−1.5) = 0.22313 = P(T &gt; 15)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The ten minutes already spent waiting are worth nothing. The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property (geometric is its discrete twin).</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> memorylessness is usually false for anything that ages. Disks, mechanical parts and user sessions have hazard rates that change over time — reach for a Weibull or a log-normal when the failure rate is not constant.</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>Normal</strong> — <code>f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π)))·exp(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²))</code>, standardised by <code>Z = (X − μ)/σ</code>. The empirical rule is 68.27% / 95.45% / 99.73% within 1, 2, 3 σ; the exact 95% multiplier is <strong>1.96</strong>, not 2. Sums of independent normals stay normal: <code>N(μ₁,σ₁²) + N(μ₂,σ₂²) = N(μ₁+μ₂, σ₁²+σ₂²)</code>. Heights as <code>Normal(170, 8²)</code> cm: <code>P(X &gt; 182)</code> has <code>z = 1.5</code> so <code>1 − Φ(1.5) = 0.06681</code>; <code>P(162 &lt; X &lt; 178)</code> has <code>z = ±1</code> so <code>Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 0.68269</code>.</p>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> scipy <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> stats</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(stats.binom.pmf(<span class="hljs-number">3</span>, <span class="hljs-number">10</span>, <span class="hljs-number">0.3</span>), stats.binom.cdf(<span class="hljs-number">3</span>, <span class="hljs-number">10</span>, <span class="hljs-number">0.3</span>))</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-comment"># 0.26682793199999977 0.6496107184</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(stats.poisson.pmf(<span class="hljs-number">5</span>, <span class="hljs-number">3</span>), stats.expon.sf(<span class="hljs-number">15</span>, scale=<span class="hljs-number">10</span>))</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-comment"># 0.10081881344492448 0.22313016014842982</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(<span class="hljs-number">1</span> - stats.norm.cdf(<span class="hljs-number">182</span>, <span class="hljs-number">170</span>, <span class="hljs-number">8</span>), stats.norm.ppf(<span class="hljs-number">0.975</span>))</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-comment"># 0.06680720126885807 1.959963984540054</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<h2 id="The-four-support-distributions"><a href="#The-four-support-distributions" class="headerlink" title="The four support distributions"></a>The four support distributions</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Log-normal</strong> — <code>log X</code> is normal. Arises from <em>multiplicative</em> accumulation, so it is right-skewed with a long tail. Response times, incomes and city sizes fit far better here than under a normal; the mean is pulled well above the median, so report both.</li>
<li><strong>Student t(ν)</strong> — the sampling distribution of <code>(x̄ − μ)/(s/√n)</code> when <code>σ</code> is estimated. Heavier tails than normal, converging to it as <code>ν → ∞</code> (already close by <code>ν ≈ 30</code>). Used for t-tests and small-sample intervals.</li>
<li><strong>Chi-square(k)</strong> — a sum of <code>k</code> squared independent standard normals, used for goodness-of-fit and independence tests on counts and for inference about a variance. <strong>F(d₁,d₂)</strong> — a ratio of two independent chi-squares each over its df, used for ANOVA and for comparing nested regression models.</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h1 id="Chapter-4-—-Sampling-and-the-limit-theorems"><a href="#Chapter-4-—-Sampling-and-the-limit-theorems" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 4 — Sampling and the limit theorems"></a>Chapter 4 — Sampling and the limit theorems</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology-2"><a href="#Terminology-2" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Population</strong> — every unit you want to claim something about; described by <em>parameters</em> <code>μ</code>, <code>σ</code>, <code>p</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Sample</strong> — what you measured; described by <em>statistics</em> <code>x̄</code>, <code>s</code>, <code>p̂</code>. A statistic is a function of random data, so it is itself a random variable.</li>
<li><strong>Sampling distribution</strong> — the distribution of a statistic across all possible samples of size <code>n</code>. The central object of inference, and <strong>not</strong> the distribution of your data. <strong>Standard error (SE)</strong> is its standard deviation.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Population   Sample                        E[x̄] = μ     Var(x̄) = σ²/n</span><br><span class="line">    μ        x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ                SE(x̄) = σ/√n (use s/√n if σ unknown)</span><br><span class="line">    σ²       s² = (1/(n−1)) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²    σ = 15:  n=25 → 3.00    n=100 → 1.50</span><br><span class="line">    p        p̂ = k/n                                n=400 → 0.75   n=1600 → 0.375</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p><strong>Quadrupling the sample halves the error.</strong> Precision is expensive and gets more expensive the more of it you already have — this single fact should govern every &quot;should we collect more data?&quot; conversation. For a proportion, <code>SE(p̂) = √(p(1−p)/n)</code>, largest at <code>p = 0.5</code>.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> all of this assumes <em>independent</em> sampling. Clustered data — many measurements per user, repeated requests from one client — has an effective sample size far below <code>n</code>. Treating it as <code>n</code> gives standard errors that are too small and intervals that are too narrow. This is the most common way real analyses go wrong.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Law-of-large-numbers"><a href="#Law-of-large-numbers" class="headerlink" title="Law of large numbers"></a>Law of large numbers</h2>
<p><code>x̄ₙ → μ</code> in probability (weak LLN) and almost surely (strong LLN). That is all it says, and it needs a finite mean — for a Cauchy distribution the sample mean never settles down.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> the gambler's fallacy is the LLN misapplied. After ten heads it does not predict compensating tails; it predicts the ten heads become negligible against a growing <code>n</code>. The imbalance is <em>diluted</em>, never <em>corrected</em>.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="The-central-limit-theorem"><a href="#The-central-limit-theorem" class="headerlink" title="The central limit theorem"></a>The central limit theorem</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Let X₁, ..., Xₙ be i.i.d. with finite mean μ and finite variance σ². Then</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">     x̄ₙ − μ</span><br><span class="line">    ────────  →  Normal(0, 1)  in distribution as n → ∞</span><br><span class="line">     σ / √n</span><br><span class="line">equivalently, x̄ₙ is approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n) for large n.</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>What it <strong>does</strong> say: the sampling distribution of the <em>mean</em> (or of any sum) becomes approximately normal regardless of the population shape, provided the variance is finite. What it <strong>does not</strong> say:</p>
<ul>
<li>It does <strong>not</strong> say your data becomes normal. Ten thousand exponential measurements are still exponentially distributed; only the <em>mean</em> is approximately normal.</li>
<li>It does not license a normality assumption about individual observations — which is what regression and t-test assumptions are partly about.</li>
<li>It does not apply with infinite variance: the mean of <code>n</code> Cauchy draws is Cauchy for every <code>n</code>.</li>
<li><code>n ≥ 30</code> is a <strong>rule of thumb, not a theorem</strong>. Mild skew converges by <code>n ≈ 15</code>; strong skew may need hundreds. Bootstrap to check rather than reciting 30.</li>
<li>It says nothing about the tails at finite <code>n</code>. The centre converges fastest and extreme quantiles slowest — which matters if you care about p99 latency.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> numpy <span class="hljs-keyword">as</span> np</span><br><span class="line">rng = np.random.default_rng(<span class="hljs-number">0</span>)</span><br><span class="line">pop = rng.exponential(scale=<span class="hljs-number">1.0</span>, size=<span class="hljs-number">1_000_000</span>)       <span class="hljs-comment"># heavily right-skewed</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">for</span> n <span class="hljs-keyword">in</span> (<span class="hljs-number">2</span>, <span class="hljs-number">5</span>, <span class="hljs-number">30</span>, <span class="hljs-number">200</span>):</span><br><span class="line">    means = rng.choice(pop, size=(<span class="hljs-number">20_000</span>, n)).mean(axis=<span class="hljs-number">1</span>)</span><br><span class="line">    <span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(n, <span class="hljs-built_in">round</span>(means.std(), <span class="hljs-number">4</span>), <span class="hljs-built_in">round</span>(<span class="hljs-number">1</span> / np.sqrt(n), <span class="hljs-number">4</span>))   <span class="hljs-comment"># sd vs σ/√n</span></span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-comment"># the histogram is visibly skewed at n=2, near-symmetric at n=30, normal at n=200</span></span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Worked example. Population <code>Uniform(0,1)</code>: <code>μ = 0.5</code>, <code>σ = √(1/12) ≈ 0.288675</code>. With <code>n = 100</code>, <code>SE = 0.0288675</code>, so <code>P(x̄ &gt; 0.55) ≈ 1 − Φ(0.05/0.0288675) = 1 − Φ(1.7321) = 0.0416</code> — about 4.2%.</p>

<h1 id="Chapter-5-—-Estimation"><a href="#Chapter-5-—-Estimation" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 5 — Estimation"></a>Chapter 5 — Estimation</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology-3"><a href="#Terminology-3" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Estimator <code>θ̂</code></strong> — a rule (function of the sample) for guessing a parameter. <strong>Estimate</strong> — the number it produces on one sample.</li>
<li><strong>Bias</strong> — <code>E[θ̂] − θ</code>. Zero bias means right <em>on average across repeated samples</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Consistency</strong> — <code>θ̂ₙ → θ</code> as <code>n → ∞</code>. <strong>Efficiency</strong> — smallest variance among unbiased estimators; the Cramér–Rao bound is the floor.</li>
<li><strong>Likelihood</strong> — <code>L(θ) = P(data ; θ)</code>, a function of <code>θ</code> with the data fixed. It is <em>not</em> a probability distribution over <code>θ</code>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Since <code>MSE(θ̂) = E[(θ̂ − θ)²] = Var(θ̂) + Bias(θ̂)²</code>, bias and variance are different failure modes and a slightly biased estimator with much lower variance can beat an unbiased one — that observation is the seed of regularisation in Chapter 8.</p>
<p><strong>Why sample variance divides by <code>n − 1</code>:</strong> using <code>x̄</code> in place of the unknown <code>μ</code> makes <code>Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²</code> systematically too small, because <code>x̄</code> is precisely the value minimising that sum for this sample. Bessel's correction compensates exactly, giving <code>E[s²] = σ²</code>.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> <code>numpy</code> defaults to <code>ddof=0</code> (biased, the MLE) and <code>pandas</code> to <code>ddof=1</code>, so the same column yields two different standard deviations depending on which library you asked — <code>np.var([2,4,4,4,5,5,7,9])</code> is <code>4.0</code> while <code>ddof=1</code> gives <code>4.5714</code>. Set <code>ddof</code> explicitly. Note also that <code>s</code> is a <strong>biased</strong> estimator of <code>σ</code> even though <code>s²</code> is unbiased: unbiasedness does not survive nonlinear transformation.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Method-of-moments-and-maximum-likelihood"><a href="#Method-of-moments-and-maximum-likelihood" class="headerlink" title="Method of moments and maximum likelihood"></a>Method of moments and maximum likelihood</h2>
<p>Method of moments sets sample moments equal to population moments and solves. For <code>Exponential(λ)</code> the population mean is <code>1/λ</code>, so <code>1/λ = x̄</code> gives <code>λ̂ = 1/x̄</code>. MLE instead picks the parameter making the observed data most probable — <strong>worked all the way through</strong>:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">1. Likelihood        L(λ) = Πᵢ λ e^(−λxᵢ) = λⁿ · exp(−λ Σᵢ xᵢ)</span><br><span class="line">2. Log-likelihood    ℓ(λ) = n·ln(λ) − λ·Σᵢ xᵢ    (same maximiser, sums not products)</span><br><span class="line">3. Differentiate     dℓ/dλ = n/λ − Σᵢ xᵢ = 0</span><br><span class="line">4. Solve             λ̂ = n / Σᵢ xᵢ = 1 / x̄</span><br><span class="line">5. Confirm maximum   d²ℓ/dλ² = −n/λ² &lt; 0 for all λ &gt; 0  →  strictly concave ✓</span><br><span class="line">Bernoulli, n trials with k successes:</span><br><span class="line">   ℓ(p) = k·ln(p) + (n − k)·ln(1 − p)</span><br><span class="line">   dℓ/dp = k/p − (n − k)/(1 − p) = 0</span><br><span class="line">     ⇒ k(1 − p) = (n − k)p  ⇒  k − kp = np − kp  ⇒  p̂ = k/n</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Here MLE and method of moments coincide; frequently they do not. The Bernoulli MLE being the sample proportion is a good sign the machinery does something sensible. MLEs are consistent, asymptotically normal, asymptotically efficient and <strong>invariant</strong> (the MLE of <code>g(θ)</code> is <code>g(θ̂)</code>), but not generally unbiased — the MLE of <code>σ²</code> divides by <code>n</code>.</p>
<pre><code class="language-python"><figure class="highlight python"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="python"><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> numpy <span class="hljs-keyword">as</span> np</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-keyword">from</span> scipy <span class="hljs-keyword">import</span> optimize</span><br><span class="line">data = np.random.default_rng(<span class="hljs-number">1</span>).exponential(scale=<span class="hljs-number">1</span> / <span class="hljs-number">2.5</span>, size=<span class="hljs-number">500</span>)   <span class="hljs-comment"># true λ = 2.5</span></span><br><span class="line">neg_ll = <span class="hljs-keyword">lambda</span> lam: -np.<span class="hljs-built_in">sum</span>(np.log(lam) - lam * data)</span><br><span class="line"><span class="hljs-built_in">print</span>(<span class="hljs-number">1</span> / data.mean(),                                                 <span class="hljs-comment"># closed-form MLE</span></span><br><span class="line">      optimize.minimize_scalar(neg_ll, bounds=(<span class="hljs-number">1e-6</span>, <span class="hljs-number">50</span>), method=<span class="hljs-string">&quot;bounded&quot;</span>).x)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<h2 id="Confidence-intervals"><a href="#Confidence-intervals" class="headerlink" title="Confidence intervals"></a>Confidence intervals</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">σ known      x̄ ± z₍₁₋α/2₎ · σ/√n        Proportion   p̂ ± z₍₁₋α/2₎ · √(p̂(1−p̂)/n)</span><br><span class="line">σ unknown    x̄ ± t₍₁₋α/2, n−1₎ · s/√n</span><br><span class="line">n = 36, x̄ = 52, σ = 6 known:  SE = 1,    CI₉₅ = 52 ± 1.96·1   = (50.04, 53.96)</span><br><span class="line">n = 25, x̄ = 52, s = 6:         SE = 1.2,  t₀.₉₇₅,₂₄ = 2.0639</span><br><span class="line">                               CI₉₅ = 52 ± 2.4767 = (49.52, 54.48)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>The t-interval is wider; that width is the price of estimating <code>σ</code> from the same 25 numbers. <strong>What &quot;95% confidence&quot; actually means</strong> — a statement about the <em>procedure</em>, not this interval:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>If you repeated the whole experiment many times — new sample, same method — <strong>95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true parameter</strong>. The 95% is a long-run coverage property of the recipe.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Once <code>(49.52, 54.48)</code> is computed, <code>μ</code> is either inside it or not; there is no randomness left to attach a probability to. Under the frequentist framework <code>μ</code> is a fixed unknown constant. Misreadings to avoid:</p>
<ul>
<li>❌ &quot;There is a 95% probability <code>μ</code> lies in (49.52, 54.48).&quot; That is the Bayesian <strong>credible interval</strong> statement and it requires a prior — a legitimate thing to want, just not what this gives you.</li>
<li>❌ &quot;95% of the data lies in this interval.&quot; That is a prediction or tolerance interval, and it is far wider.</li>
<li>❌ &quot;95% of future sample means will fall in this interval.&quot; No — different interval, lower coverage. And ❌ &quot;a wider interval means the estimate is wrong&quot;: a wider interval means less information, honestly reported.</li>
</ul>

<h1 id="Chapter-6-—-Hypothesis-testing"><a href="#Chapter-6-—-Hypothesis-testing" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 6 — Hypothesis testing"></a>Chapter 6 — Hypothesis testing</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology-4"><a href="#Terminology-4" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Null hypothesis (H₀)</strong> — the &quot;nothing interesting happening&quot; model, specific enough to compute probabilities under. <strong>Alternative (H₁)</strong> — what you conclude if H₀ is rejected.</li>
<li><strong>Test statistic</strong> — a number from the sample whose distribution under H₀ is known. <strong>Significance level (α)</strong> — the Type I error rate chosen <em>before</em> seeing the data.</li>
<li><strong>Type I error</strong> — rejecting a true H₀ (false positive, rate <code>α</code>). <strong>Type II</strong> — failing to reject a false H₀ (false negative, rate <code>β</code>). <strong>Power</strong> — <code>1 − β</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Effect size</strong> — how big the difference is, in units someone cares about.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="The-p-value-stated-correctly"><a href="#The-p-value-stated-correctly" class="headerlink" title="The p-value, stated correctly"></a>The p-value, stated correctly</h2>
<blockquote>
<p>A <strong>p-value</strong> is the probability of obtaining a test statistic <strong>at least as extreme as the one observed</strong>, computed <strong>under the assumption that H₀ and all the model's assumptions are true</strong>.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Every clause carries weight. What it is <em>not</em>: ❌ the probability H₀ is true — that is <code>P(H₀ | data)</code>, while a p-value is <code>P(data this extreme | H₀)</code>, the same confused conditional as the medical test in Chapter 1; ❌ the probability the result is &quot;due to chance&quot;; ❌ <code>1 − P(H₁ true)</code>; ❌ a measure of effect size, since at <code>n = 10,000,000</code> a trivial difference gives <code>p &lt; 0.001</code>; ❌ a statement about whether the finding will replicate; ❌ something with a bright line at 0.05, since <code>p = 0.049</code> and <code>p = 0.051</code> are the same evidence.</p>
<p>A large p-value is also not evidence <em>for</em> H₀ — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, especially at small <code>n</code>. &quot;We failed to reject H₀&quot; is the honest phrasing.</p>
<p><strong>Mechanics.</strong> State H₀ and H₁ before seeing the data; choose <code>α</code> and a sample size giving adequate power; compute the statistic; compute the p-value from its null distribution; reject if <code>p ≤ α</code>; and <strong>always report the effect size with its confidence interval</strong>. A two-tailed test asks &quot;is there a difference?&quot;, a one-tailed test asks &quot;is it bigger?&quot; and puts all of <code>α</code> in one tail — easier to reach significance in that direction, impossible in the other. Choosing the tail after seeing which way the data went doubles the real Type I rate.</p>
<h2 id="Worked-tests"><a href="#Worked-tests" class="headerlink" title="Worked tests"></a>Worked tests</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span><br><span class="line">13</span><br><span class="line">14</span><br><span class="line">15</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">z-test — σ = 15 known, H₀: μ = 100, n = 25, x̄ = 106</span><br><span class="line">  SE = 15/√25 = 3        z = (106 − 100)/3 = 2.00</span><br><span class="line">  two-tailed p = 2·(1 − Φ(2)) = 0.0455    CI₉₅ = 106 ± 1.96·3 = (100.12, 111.88)</span><br><span class="line">  → rejects at α = 0.05, barely; the CI excludes 100 and is more informative</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">One-sample t — n = 20, x̄ = 5.4, s = 1.2, H₀: μ = 5</span><br><span class="line">  SE = 1.2/√20 = 0.26833     t = 0.4/0.26833 = 1.4907,  df = 19</span><br><span class="line">  two-tailed p = 0.1525      → do not reject; the 8% difference is not</span><br><span class="line">                               resolvable with 20 observations</span><br><span class="line"> </span><br><span class="line">Two-sample t — A: n=12, x̄=7.8, s=1.4    B: n=12, x̄=6.5, s=1.6</span><br><span class="line">  s²ₚ = (11·1.4² + 11·1.6²)/22 = 2.26      sₚ = 1.5033</span><br><span class="line">  SE = sₚ·√(1/12 + 1/12) = 0.61373         t = 1.3/0.61373 = 2.118,  df = 22</span><br><span class="line">  two-tailed p = 0.0457                    Cohen&#x27;s d = 1.3/1.5033 = 0.865 (large)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> default to <strong>Welch's t-test</strong> (<code>equal_var=False</code>) for two independent samples. It does not assume equal variances, costs almost nothing in power when they are equal, and avoids a pre-test that inflates the error rate.</p>
</blockquote>
<p><strong>Chi-square goodness-of-fit.</strong> A die rolled 60 times, observed <code>[8, 9, 12, 11, 10, 10]</code>, expected 10 each: <code>χ² = (4+1+4+1+0+0)/10 = 1.00</code>, <code>df = 5</code>, <code>p = 0.963</code> — no evidence of unfairness.</p>
<p><strong>Chi-square independence.</strong> A 2×2 table with <code>Eᵢⱼ = (row·col)/grand = 25</code> in every cell:</p>

<p><strong>One-way ANOVA</strong> decomposes variability as <code>SS_total = SS_between + SS_within</code> and forms <code>F = MS_between/MS_within = (SS_between/(k−1)) / (SS_within/(N−k))</code>. A significant <code>F</code> says &quot;not all means are equal&quot; — not <em>which</em>. Follow with pairwise comparisons <strong>and</strong> a correction (Tukey's HSD is built for it).</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> running all <code>C(k,2)</code> pairwise t-tests instead of an ANOVA. With <code>k = 5</code> that is 10 tests and roughly a 40% family-wise false positive rate.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Underpowered studies are doubly bad: they usually miss real effects, and when they <em>do</em> reach significance the estimated effect is inflated, because only the luckiest samples cross the threshold. That inflation is one mechanical driver of failed replications.</p>
<p>Run <code>m</code> independent tests at <code>α = 0.05</code> and <code>P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95^m</code>: <strong>5.0%</strong> at <code>m = 1</code>, <strong>22.6%</strong> at 5, <strong>40.1%</strong> at 10, <strong>64.2%</strong> at 20, <strong>92.3%</strong> at 50. <strong>Bonferroni</strong> tests each hypothesis at <code>α/m</code>, controlling the family-wise error rate — simple, always valid, conservative. <strong>Benjamini–Hochberg</strong> controls the false discovery rate: sort p-values ascending, find the largest <code>k</code> with <code>p₍ₖ₎ ≤ (k/m)·q</code>, reject 1 through <code>k</code>.</p>

<p>Use Bonferroni when a single false positive is expensive (a confirmatory clinical endpoint); use FDR when screening many candidates with a tolerable proportion of duds (genomics, feature screening, dashboards with dozens of metrics).</p>
<h2 id="p-hacking-and-the-replication-crisis"><a href="#p-hacking-and-the-replication-crisis" class="headerlink" title="p-hacking and the replication crisis"></a>p-hacking and the replication crisis</h2>
<p>Every test above is valid <strong>only if the analysis was fixed before the data was seen</strong>. The moment analytic decisions respond to the data, the reported p-value's null distribution is no longer the one you looked up — and this mostly happens without dishonest intent. <strong>Optional stopping</strong>: peeking and stopping when <code>p &lt; 0.05</code>; with enough peeks, an A/B test on two <em>identical</em> variants crosses the line with probability approaching 1. <strong>Outcome switching</strong>: measuring ten metrics, reporting the one that worked. <strong>Subgroup mining</strong>: &quot;significant among iOS users aged 25–34&quot;. <strong>Flexible exclusions</strong>: choosing which outliers to drop after seeing the effect on <code>p</code>. <strong>Model shopping</strong>: trying covariates and transformations until something is significant. <strong>The garden of forking paths</strong>: the same inflation without explicit re-testing, because the analysis <em>would have</em> differed had the data differed. <strong>HARKing</strong>: hypothesising after results are known and presenting exploratory work as confirmatory.</p>
<p>The <strong>replication crisis</strong> is the consequence. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 <em>Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science</em> re-ran 100 studies: 97% of originals reported significant results, 36% of replications did, with replication effect sizes averaging roughly half the originals. Comparable gaps appeared in cancer biology and experimental economics. What helps: <strong>pre-registration</strong> (hypotheses, outcomes, exclusions and analysis plan written down first — keep exploratory work, just label it); <strong>report effect sizes with confidence intervals</strong>, since &quot;d = 0.31, 95% CI (0.05, 0.57)&quot; carries size, direction and precision while &quot;p = 0.03&quot; carries almost nothing; <strong>power the study beforehand</strong>, because post-hoc power computed from the observed effect is a deterministic function of the p-value and adds no information; <strong>publish nulls</strong>, since the file-drawer problem makes the literature a biased sample; and <strong>share data and code</strong>, because reproducibility is the precondition for replicability.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> if you must peek at a running A/B test, use a method designed for it — group sequential designs with alpha spending, or always-valid sequential inference. Repeatedly applying a fixed-sample t-test is not one of those methods.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-7-—-Regression"><a href="#Chapter-7-—-Regression" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 7 — Regression"></a>Chapter 7 — Regression</h1>
<h2 id="Terminology-5"><a href="#Terminology-5" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Response <code>y</code></strong> — what you model; <strong>predictor <code>x</code></strong> — what you model it from. <strong>Residual</strong> — <code>eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ</code>, the part the model failed to explain (not the same as the unobservable error term).</li>
<li><strong>Homoscedasticity</strong> — residual variance constant across fitted values; the opposite is heteroscedasticity.</li>
<li><strong>Multicollinearity</strong> — predictors strongly linearly related to <em>each other</em>. <strong>Leverage</strong> — how far a point's predictor values sit from the centre; high-leverage points can dominate a fit.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="Simple-linear-regression"><a href="#Simple-linear-regression" class="headerlink" title="Simple linear regression"></a>Simple linear regression</h2>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span><br><span class="line">8</span><br><span class="line">9</span><br><span class="line">10</span><br><span class="line">11</span><br><span class="line">12</span><br><span class="line">13</span><br><span class="line">14</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">Model    yᵢ = β₀ + β₁xᵢ + εᵢ,     εᵢ ~ i.i.d. <p>The fitted line always passes through <code>(x̄, ȳ)</code>; <code>stats.linregress</code> on those five points returns slope <code>0.6</code>, intercept <code>2.2</code>, <code>rvalue**2 = 0.6</code>. Interpretation: <code>β̂₁ = 0.6</code> means a one-unit increase in <code>x</code> is <em>associated with</em> a 0.6-unit increase in the mean of <code>y</code> — &quot;causes&quot; only if <code>x</code> was randomised. <code>β̂₀ = 2.2</code> is the prediction at <code>x = 0</code>, meaningless whenever <code>x = 0</code> is outside the data or physically impossible; centring <code>x</code> at its mean makes the intercept the prediction at average <code>x</code>.</p>
<p><strong><code>R²</code> and its limits.</strong> <code>R²</code> is the fraction of the variance of <code>y</code> explained by the model — useful and routinely over-read. It <strong>never decreases</strong> when you add a predictor, even a column of pure noise, so compare models with <strong>adjusted <code>R²</code></strong> or out-of-sample error. A high <code>R²</code> does not mean the functional form is right: <strong>Anscombe's quartet</strong> is four datasets with identical <code>x̄ = 9</code>, <code>ȳ = 7.50</code>, <code>r = 0.816</code>, the same line <code>ŷ = 3.00 + 0.50x</code> and the same <code>R² = 0.667</code> — yet one is linear, one a clean parabola, one a perfect line plus an outlier, and one a vertical stack plus one influential point. Conversely a low <code>R²</code> does not mean the model is useless: with irreducible noise (human behaviour, financial returns) an <code>R²</code> of 0.05 can carry a real, well-estimated, economically significant slope. And <code>R²</code> says nothing about causality, bias, or whether the model holds on new data.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Trick:</strong> always plot the data and the residuals. Summary statistics exist to compress, and what breaks a regression is exactly what compression discards.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Multiple-regression-and-its-assumptions"><a href="#Multiple-regression-and-its-assumptions" class="headerlink" title="Multiple regression and its assumptions"></a>Multiple regression and its assumptions</h2>
<p>In <code>y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + β_p x_p + ε</code>, each <code>βⱼ</code> is the expected change in <code>y</code> per unit of <code>xⱼ</code> <strong>holding the other predictors in the model fixed</strong>. That conditional clause is why a coefficient can flip sign when a variable is added — the classic Simpson's paradox situation.</p>

<h2 id="Multicollinearity-and-categorical-predictors"><a href="#Multicollinearity-and-categorical-predictors" class="headerlink" title="Multicollinearity and categorical predictors"></a>Multicollinearity and categorical predictors</h2>
<p>Strongly correlated predictors leave the model unable to attribute credit between them. Predictions stay fine; individual coefficients become unstable, with huge standard errors and signs that flip on refit. <code>VIFⱼ = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ)</code>, where <code>R²ⱼ</code> comes from regressing <code>xⱼ</code> on all other predictors: <code>VIF = 1</code> is uncorrelated, <code>&gt; 5</code> is worth investigating, <code>&gt; 10</code> is serious and the coefficients are not individually interpretable. Remedies: drop a redundant predictor, combine them into an index or principal component, collect more data, or use ridge regression (Chapter 8), designed for exactly this.</p>
<p>A categorical variable with <code>k</code> levels enters as <code>k − 1</code> <strong>dummy</strong> columns; the omitted level is the <strong>reference</strong> and every coefficient is a contrast against it.</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">colour ∈ {red, green, blue}, reference = red:  y = β₀ + β₁·is_green + β₂·is_blue</span><br><span class="line">   β₀ = mean(red)      β₁ = green − red      β₂ = blue − red</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>Including all <code>k</code> dummies alongside an intercept makes the design matrix singular — the <strong>dummy variable trap</strong>. <code>pd.get_dummies(..., drop_first=True)</code> and the <code>C()</code> operator in <code>statsmodels</code> formulas handle it.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> encoding an unordered category as integers (<code>red=1, green=2, blue=3</code>) tells the model that blue is three times red and green sits halfway between. Ordinal encoding is valid only for genuinely ordered levels with roughly equal spacing.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Logistic-regression"><a href="#Logistic-regression" class="headerlink" title="Logistic regression"></a>Logistic regression</h2>
<p>With binary <code>y</code>, linear regression predicts probabilities outside <code>[0,1]</code> and has structurally heteroscedastic errors. Model the <strong>log-odds</strong> instead, fitted by maximum likelihood using the Bernoulli likelihood from Chapter 5:</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span><br><span class="line">6</span><br><span class="line">7</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">odds = p/(1 − p)      logit(p) = ln(p/(1 − p)) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + β_p x_p</span><br><span class="line">inverse (logistic / sigmoid):   p = 1 / (1 + e^(−(β₀ + Σ βⱼxⱼ)))</span><br><span class="line">logit(p) = −4 + 0.05·score</span><br><span class="line">  score =  80:  logit = 0.0  →  p = 1/(1 + e⁰)  = 0.500</span><br><span class="line">  score = 100:  logit = 1.0  →  p = 1/(1 + e⁻¹) = 0.731</span><br><span class="line">  score = 120:  logit = 2.0  →  p = 1/(1 + e⁻²) = 0.881</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p><code>βⱼ</code> is the change in <em>log-odds</em> per unit of <code>xⱼ</code>; exponentiate for an <strong>odds ratio</strong>, so <code>β₁ = 0.7 → OR = e^0.7 = 2.01</code> means each extra unit multiplies the odds by about 2.01, holding the others fixed (<code>OR = 1</code> no effect, <code>&gt; 1</code> increases, <code>&lt; 1</code> decreases). Note that the same 20-point score change above moves the probability by 23 points then 15: the effect on the <em>probability</em> is nonlinear even though the effect on the <em>log-odds</em> is constant. In <code>statsmodels</code>, <code>np.exp(sm.Logit(y, sm.add_constant(X)).fit().params)</code> gives the odds ratios and <code>np.exp(fit.conf_int())</code> their intervals.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> an odds ratio is not a risk ratio. They are close only when the outcome is rare (<code>p &lt; 0.1</code>); for common outcomes the OR sits much further from 1 than the relative risk, so reading &quot;twice as likely&quot; from <code>OR = 2</code> overstates the effect. Report absolute probabilities at representative predictor values too. Evaluate with the confusion matrix, precision/recall, ROC-AUC and calibration — and note that accuracy on an imbalanced problem is the base-rate fallacy in disguise: always predicting &quot;no disease&quot; is 99.5% accurate on the Chapter 1 population and useless.</p>
</blockquote>

<h1 id="Chapter-8-—-Where-statistics-becomes-machine-learning"><a href="#Chapter-8-—-Where-statistics-becomes-machine-learning" class="headerlink" title="Chapter 8 — Where statistics becomes machine learning"></a>Chapter 8 — Where statistics becomes machine learning</h1>
<p>Everything above is mostly <strong>inference</strong>: estimate a parameter, quantify uncertainty, decide whether an effect is real. Machine learning uses the same mathematics to optimise a different target — <strong>out-of-sample prediction error</strong>. The methods overlap almost entirely; the objectives do not.</p>
<h2 id="Terminology-6"><a href="#Terminology-6" class="headerlink" title="Terminology"></a>Terminology</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Training error</strong> — error on the data used to fit; optimistically biased, always. <strong>Generalisation error</strong> — expected error on new data from the same distribution; the quantity you actually care about.</li>
<li><strong>Overfitting</strong> — the model learned noise specific to the training set (train error low, generalisation error high). <strong>Underfitting</strong> — too rigid to capture the signal (both high). <strong>Hyperparameter</strong> — a knob not learned from the training data. <strong>Regularisation</strong> — a penalty on complexity, trading a little bias for a lot of variance.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">E[(y − f̂(x))²]  =  Bias(f̂(x))²  +  Var(f̂(x))       +  σ²</span><br><span class="line">                   ────────────     ────────────      ──────────────────</span><br><span class="line">                   wrong shape      unstable across   irreducible noise —</span><br><span class="line">                                    samples           no model removes it</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>
<p>High bias / low variance is a linear model on curved data: wrong the same way every time. Low bias / high variance is a degree-15 polynomial through 20 points: fits each training set beautifully and differently. The best model is rarely the most flexible; it is the one minimising the <em>sum</em>.</p>

<h2 id="Train-validation-test"><a href="#Train-validation-test" class="headerlink" title="Train / validation / test"></a>Train / validation / test</h2>
<p>Train fits the parameters, validation chooses hyperparameters and model families, and test is touched <strong>exactly once</strong> to estimate generalisation error (60/20/20, or 80/20 with cross-validation inside the 80). <strong>k-fold cross-validation</strong> replaces a single validation split: partition into <code>k</code> folds, fit on <code>k − 1</code>, score the held-out one, rotate, average. <code>k = 5</code> or <code>10</code> is standard, uses the data far more efficiently, and gives a variance estimate alongside the mean.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Watch out — leakage.</strong> Fit scalers, imputers and feature selectors <strong>inside</strong> the CV loop, on the training fold only; standardising the full dataset before splitting leaks test information and produces a score that will not survive production. <code>sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline</code> makes the correct thing the easy thing. Two more traps: split time-ordered data by time (<code>TimeSeriesSplit</code>), never randomly, or the model sees the future; and split grouped data by group (<code>GroupKFold</code>), or the same user lands on both sides.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="Regularisation-as-a-statistical-idea"><a href="#Regularisation-as-a-statistical-idea" class="headerlink" title="Regularisation as a statistical idea"></a>Regularisation as a statistical idea</h2>
<p>Ridge and lasso are not ML inventions bolted onto regression — they are <code>MSE = Var + Bias²</code> from Chapter 5 turned into an algorithm: accept a little bias, cut a lot of variance, lower the total.</p>
<pre><code class="language-text"><figure class="highlight text"><table><tr><td class="gutter"><pre><span class="line">1</span><br><span class="line">2</span><br><span class="line">3</span><br><span class="line">4</span><br><span class="line">5</span></pre></td><td class="code"><pre><code class="text"><span class="line">OLS       minimise  Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²                              (no penalty)</span><br><span class="line">Ridge     minimise  Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + λ·Σ βⱼ²                    (L2)</span><br><span class="line">Lasso     minimise  Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + λ·Σ |βⱼ|                   (L1)</span><br><span class="line">Elastic   minimise  Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + λ(α·Σ|βⱼ| + (1−α)·Σ βⱼ²)</span><br><span class="line"> </span></code></pre></td></tr></table></figure></code></pre>

<p>The failure mode is using one where you needed the other. Decide which you are building <strong>before</strong> fitting anything: it changes the split, the model class, the metric, and what you may conclude.</p>
<h2 id="Where-the-data-comes-from"><a href="#Where-the-data-comes-from" class="headerlink" title="Where the data comes from"></a>Where the data comes from</h2>
<p>None of this happens on clean arrays — the real pipeline is extract, aggregate, reshape, then model.</p>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>aggregation stage</strong> is usually a database job. The MongoDB course's <strong>aggregation basics post</strong> covers <code>$group</code>, <code>$bucket</code> and the accumulators that compute group means, counts and variances server-side — where the group summaries feeding an ANOVA or a chi-square table should come from.</li>
<li><strong>Time-ordered measurements</strong> bring their own independence problems; the <strong>time-series at extreme scale post</strong> covers bucketing and windowing, and the statistical point is that consecutive observations are autocorrelated, so the effective <code>n</code> is below the row count.</li>
<li><strong>Vector search and RAG</strong> are cosine similarity plus nearest-neighbour retrieval — geometry built on the covariance ideas of Chapter 2; see <strong>Atlas Search and vector search</strong> and the <strong>production RAG build</strong>, and note that evaluating a retrieval system is a hypothesis-testing problem carrying every multiple-comparison hazard from Chapter 6. The Python side starts with <strong>data structures</strong> — <code>dict</code>, <code>list</code>, comprehensions — which is what <code>pandas</code> is built on and what you reach for when reshaping records before they hit <code>numpy</code>.</li>
</ul>

<p>A study reports <em>&quot;mean improvement 4.2 points, 95% CI (0.3, 8.1), p = 0.037.&quot;</em> Which statements are correct? (1) There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in (0.3, 8.1). (2) Repeated many times, about 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true mean. (3) There is a 3.7% probability the null is true. (4) Data would be at least this extreme in 3.7% of studies if the true effect were exactly zero. (5) It is significant at <code>α = 0.05</code>, so the effect is important. (6) The interval is wide relative to the effect, so the study is consistent with anything from negligible to large.</p>

<h2 id="Exercise-7-—-Fit-and-diagnose"><a href="#Exercise-7-—-Fit-and-diagnose" class="headerlink" title="Exercise 7 — Fit and diagnose"></a>Exercise 7 — Fit and diagnose</h2>
<p>Simulate <code>y = 3 + 2x + ε</code> with <code>ε ~ Normal(0,1)</code>, <code>n = 100</code>. Fit with <code>statsmodels</code> and confirm the coefficients recover the truth within their standard errors. Then break one assumption at a time and note which diagnostic reacts: multiply the errors by <code>x</code> (heteroscedasticity → funnel in residuals-vs-fitted and wrong SEs; refit with <code>cov_type=&quot;HC3&quot;</code>); add a <code>0.5x²</code> term (nonlinearity → U-curve in residuals-vs-fitted); draw <code>ε</code> from a t with 2 df (heavy tails → Q-Q plot bending at both ends); generate <code>ε</code> as an AR(1) series with <code>φ = 0.9</code> (dependence → Durbin–Watson far below 2 and visible runs in residuals-vs-order).</p>

<h1 id="Cheat-sheet"><a href="#Cheat-sheet" class="headerlink" title="Cheat-sheet"></a>Cheat-sheet</h1>

<td>Union / complement / conditional</td>
<td><code>P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)</code>; <code>P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)</code>; <code>P(A given B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B)</code>; independent ⟺ <code>P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)</code></td>

<td>Bayes / counting</td>
<td><code>P(A given B) = P(B given A)·P(A)/P(B)</code> — always check the base rate; <code>P(n,r) = n!/(n−r)!</code> ordered, <code>C(n,r) = n!/(r!(n−r)!)</code> unordered</td>
</tr>

<td>Variance toolkit</td>
<td><code>Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²</code>; <code>Var(aX+b) = a²Var(X)</code>; <code>Var(X ± Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)</code> if independent</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Correlation</td>
<td><code>ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σₓσᵧ)</code> in <code>[−1,1]</code> — <strong>linear</strong> association only</td>

<td>Discrete zoo</td>
<td>Binomial <code>np</code>, <code>np(1−p)</code>; Poisson <code>mean = var = λ</code>; Geometric <code>1/p</code>; binomial → Poisson when <code>n</code> large, <code>p ≤ 0.01</code>, <code>λ = np</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Continuous zoo</td>
<td>Exponential <code>1/λ</code>, <code>P(T&gt;t) = e^(−λt)</code>, memoryless; Normal 68/95/99.7, exact 95% is <code>1.96</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Standard error / CLT</td>
<td><code>SE = σ/√n</code>, so quadruple <code>n</code> to halve it; the CLT normalises the <strong>sampling distribution of the mean</strong>, not your data</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Sample variance / MLE</td>
<td>divide by <code>n − 1</code> (<code>numpy</code> needs <code>ddof=1</code>); MLE = write <code>L</code>, take <code>log</code>, differentiate, solve, check <code>ℓ″ &lt; 0</code></td>
</tr>
<tr>

<td><code>x̄ ± t₍₁₋α/2, n−1₎·s/√n</code>; 95% of intervals <em>from this procedure</em> cover <code>θ</code> — not <code>P(θ ∈ this one)</code></td>

<h1 id="Where-to-go-deeper"><a href="#Where-to-go-deeper" class="headerlink" title="Where to go deeper"></a>Where to go deeper</h1>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bayesian statistics</strong> — Chapter 1's theorem generalises to full posteriors over parameters, which is where the &quot;95% probability the parameter is in this interval&quot; statement legitimately lives. Gelman et al., <em>Bayesian Data Analysis</em>; implement with <code>PyMC</code> or <code>Stan</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Causal inference</strong> — regression coefficients are associations; turning them into causal claims needs potential outcomes, DAGs, backdoor adjustment, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity.</li>
<li><strong>Experimental design and time series</strong> — allocation, blocking, randomisation and stratification (directly applicable to A/B testing, sequential designs and CUPED-style variance reduction); then ARIMA, state-space models and cross-validation that respects time ordering, for the case where Chapter 7's independence assumption fails by construction.</li>
<li><strong>Generalised linear models</strong> — logistic regression is one member of a family, alongside Poisson regression for counts, gamma for skewed positives and ordinal models for ranked outcomes. <strong>Hierarchical / mixed-effects models</strong> — the principled answer to clustered data; partial pooling is regularisation derived from the data structure. <strong>Resampling</strong> — the bootstrap gives intervals for statistics with no tractable sampling distribution, and permutation tests give exact p-values without distributional assumptions.</li>
<li><strong>Statistical learning</strong> — <em>An Introduction to Statistical Learning</em> is the natural sequel to Chapter 8, with <em>The Elements of Statistical Learning</em> as the deeper version.</li>
</ul>
